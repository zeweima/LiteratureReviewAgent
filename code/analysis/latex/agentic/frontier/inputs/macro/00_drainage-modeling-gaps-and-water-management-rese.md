### Material: Data and Experimental Validation Needs

\subsection{Overview}

A recurring conclusion across the tile-drainage literature is that theory,
models, and management hypotheses have outrun the field data available to
validate them. The studies surveyed here converge on a shared research
frontier defined less by a lack of ideas than by a lack of evidence: process
representations that remain untested outside the conditions in which they were
built, models whose parameters cannot be constrained without measurements that
were never made, and management practices whose benefits are asserted from
short records at single sites. This section synthesises the identified data and
experimental-validation needs. Several broad lines of evidence emerge. First,
process-based drainage and water-quality models (DRAINMOD and its derivatives,
SWAT, APEX, HYDRUS, CRHM, and numerous salinity codes) are repeatedly flagged as
requiring further testing across soils, climates, crops, and management systems,
with particular weaknesses in phosphorus, macropore/preferential-flow, and
seepage components \parencite{Youssef2006,Askar2021b,Askar2025,Radcliffe2015}.
Second, there is a strong and near-universal call for long-term, year-round, and
multi-site monitoring to close water and nutrient budgets
\parencite{Kladivko2001,Sharpley2019,Frankenberger2024}. Third, rapidly maturing
remote-sensing and geophysical methods for mapping drainage networks need
systematic evaluation of the site conditions under which they succeed
\parencite{Carlsen2024,Wienken2024}. Fourth, emerging management
strategies -- controlled drainage, drainage water recycling, and edge-of-field
treatment -- lack the coordinated field-scale experiments and simulations needed
to quantify their trade-offs \parencite{Hay2021,Youssef2018,Mendes2020}. Finally,
authors repeatedly emphasise improvements to experimental design, statistical
methodology, uncertainty quantification, and the reporting of site
characterisation, management, and economic data
\parencite{Pagliari2021,Singh2022b,Gates2002}. The subsections below organise
these needs by domain.

\subsection{Validation and improvement of process-based drainage models}

The most frequently cited need is more rigorous, independent testing of
simulation models beyond the conditions in which they were calibrated.
\textcite{Youssef2006} note that further research is needed to test the model for
different management practices and soil and climatological conditions, and that a
more rigorous field testing should be conducted to validate application to
drained lands -- a need they describe as common to nearly all simulation models.
Similar generic calls for further validation, broader-condition testing, and
parameter sensitivity analysis recur for pesticide, salt, and flow models
\parencite{Branger2009,Hirekhan2007,Yang2008b,Xian2017}. \textcite{Yang2008b}
stress that soil-related coefficients -- particularly the drainage-volume/water-table-depth
relationship -- must be adjusted for local soil and water conditions before the
improved DRAINMOD extensions can be applied elsewhere, and propose integrating
remote sensing and GIS for input preparation and eventual incorporation into
decision-support tools. \textcite{Xian2017} likewise call for testing across
different climates, soils, and agronomic practices and for alternate approaches
to improve simulation of drainage peaks. \textcite{Mendez2004} argue that more
experimental studies in different soils and climates are needed to verify
modelling findings, and that long-term simulations of 50 to 100 years are
required to assess whether a given drainage design meets chosen criteria.

\subsubsection{DRAINMOD, controlled drainage, and drainage water recycling components}

Extensions of DRAINMOD are singled out for continued development and testing.
\textcite{Askar2021b} call for more testing of newly incorporated hydrological
and biogeochemical components -- especially the macropore flow, phosphorus cycle,
and sediment components -- with rigorous, quantitative assessment of performance
under varying water management, farming practices, and climatic conditions.
\textcite{Askar2025} extend this to the phosphorus component specifically, urging
validation across a range of soils, drainage characteristics, and climates,
recommending that key coefficients become user inputs for flexibility, and
highlighting the soil-erosion and macropore-flow components as priorities for
rigorous testing. For drainage water management and recycling (DWR/DWM),
\textcite{Moursi2022} call for testing DRAINMOD-DWR against field-measured data
and for developing routines that simulate the fate and transport of nitrogen,
phosphorus, and sediment in the storage reservoir; \textcite{Moursi2023} add that
the physical, chemical, and biological processes in the reservoir must be
investigated to optimise treatment efficiency and to identify conditions under
which reservoirs become nutrient sources, evaluated under varying site conditions
over longer periods. Related model-development needs include a time-variable
surface-depression-capacity input and expansion to wastewater and urban modules
\parencite{Abduljaleel2023}, and evaluation of a DRAINMOD-ANN model against
experimental data across different soil textures and field conditions
\parencite{Awad2022}. \textcite{Youssef2018} outline the experimental verification
required for controlled drainage: side-by-side comparison of controlled and free
drainage with extensive measurement of mineral-N concentrations in the root zone,
shallow groundwater at multiple depths, and drainage outflow, sustained long
enough to capture responses to precipitation, cropping, and fertilisation, plus a
separate simulation study to assess effects on vertical seepage across soils.
\textcite{Skaggs2010} emphasise that controlled-drainage experiments on drainage
volumes and N losses must be conducted at field or watershed scale so that
seepage impacts are properly represented, while \textcite{Skaggs2006} note that
additional research is needed to test relationships and develop equations for a
wider range of drain depths and surface storages before simplified drainage-design
methods can be accepted for general use. Complementary evaluation methods and
alternative Ks-measurement relationships are recommended alongside DRAINMOD in
data-scarce settings \parencite{NabiSichani2012}.

\subsubsection{Phosphorus, sediment, and macropore transport in water-quality models}

Phosphorus and its transport through macropores emerge as the least mature
component across water-quality models. \textcite{Radcliffe2015} provide the most
comprehensive agenda: field experiments using a nested, paired research design
are needed to improve phosphorus models for artificially drained fields, and
several codes require structural additions -- SWAT and ADAPT should route
macropore flow directly to tile drains or lateral flow to ditches, APEX needs a
macropore component, HSPF and P-indices in artificially drained areas should
simulate water and phosphorus movement to ditches or drains, DRAINMOD should be
expanded to simulate P losses to drains and P attached to sediment in runoff,
PLEASE needs a runoff component, and HYDRUS could be used experimentally to
understand transport to drains and parameterise simpler models -- with all model
studies quantifying the accuracy and uncertainty of predicted P losses.
Consistent with this, \textcite{Guo2020b} recommend modifying tile-drainage,
macropore-flow, and P-transport processes in the APEX/NTT interface, incorporating
physically based tile-drainage subroutines and macropore nutrient-transport
pathways, including particulate-P losses through tiles, and accounting for soil-test-P
effects over the full simulation period. \textcite{Ford2017} call for further
testing under varying fertiliser management (broadcast and incorporated, organic
and inorganic), additional field data to constrain subsurface-P parameter ranges
for tools such as the Nutrient Tracking Tool, and a better understanding of how
macropore flow and P loads respond to tillage, cover crops, fertiliser management,
and drainage water management. \textcite{BinShokrana2023b} identify specific
improvements to a DRAINMOD P component -- better partitioning of fertiliser P into
pools (especially under ponded conditions), improved macropore-flow simulation,
testing across climates, soils, crop varieties, and management, and refined
P-only performance statistics. \textcite{Gowda2012} anticipate further developing
the DRAINMOD suite to simulate phosphorus fate and transport in drained land.
Supporting empirical work is also required: \textcite{McDowell2001b} call for
further testing of CaCl$_2$-P as an estimator for dissolved reactive phosphorus in
drainage water from other soils and for determining how water and CaCl$_2$
extractions behave under different rainfall intensities and durations in the
field; \textcite{Morrison2013} recommend additional field research on how drainage
parameters affect runoff, subsurface drainage, and P losses, and improving
regression models with P saturation, soil-test P, and fertilisation rates;
\textcite{Sanford2016} require field-scale work with fluctuating flow rates and P
concentrations and tests using actual drainage effluent; and \textcite{Jia2012}
call for long-term monitoring of water-quality trends and investigation of the
sources and transport of P.

\subsubsection{Salinity, salt balance, and unsaturated-zone coupling}

In arid and irrigated settings, needs cluster around salt-transport validation and
coupling saturated and unsaturated processes. \textcite{Ebrahimian2015} recommend
applying models capable of simulating salt and nitrate concentrations in drainage
water and the soil profile to mitigate environmental impacts, while
\textcite{Sarangi2006} note that neural-network models can predict effluent
salinity and reclamation period from minimal input data but that SALTMOD may be
preferred for detailed salt-balance estimation. \textcite{Hirekhan2007} caution
that more evaluation studies are needed before firm recommendations on salt-transport
simulation can be made. Several Chinese studies press for tighter coupling of
water and salt dynamics: \textcite{Han2025} propose coupling future models with
unsaturated water-salt models such as HYDRUS-1D and UBMOD-S for more accurate
water and salt content in both zones and for developing regional subsurface-pipe
layout parameters; \textcite{Wang2025c} plan to integrate process-based models
(HYDRUS-2D/SWAP) with multi-objective optimisation and to consider climatic
variability, soil texture, groundwater mineralisation, and crop water stress; and
\textcite{Wang2024c} call for continuous model refinement, long-term simulation,
and exploration of additional factors affecting salt flux under varying
conditions. \textcite{Liu2022} recommend adding a detailed root-growth module,
automatic TDR monitoring of water and salt, evaluation under different irrigation
methods and salt levels, and validation with large field datasets, while
\textcite{Liu2026} extend simulation horizons to include climate-change scenarios,
long-term salt accumulation in deep soil and groundwater, and real-world
scalability. \textcite{Guo2025} similarly seek longer observation periods, a wider
range of environmental variables, optimal leaching quotas under diverse
conditions, and cost-benefit analyses, and further elucidation of the links among
surface salt accumulation, temperature, and groundwater depth. \textcite{Kelleners2000}
warn that model assumptions must be re-verified when applied to new areas and that
proper incorporation of regional seepage would require a fundamentally different
modelling approach. Earlier salinity-modelling series similarly staged validation
and salt-flow analysis across successive papers \parencite{KARAJEH1994}.

\subsubsection{Hydrological structure, seepage, and preferential flow representation}

A cross-cutting structural gap is the representation of seepage, groundwater
exchange, and preferential flow. \textcite{DaneshYazdi2016} suggest that
groundwater contributions could be incorporated through a double-storage model and
residual storage added if hydrochemical data were available, requiring further
data to refine interpretations. \textcite{Feng2018} argue that lateral seepage
must be allowed to vary during simulation and be included among model inputs, and
\textcite{Kelleners2000} echo the difficulty of representing regional seepage.
\textcite{Kompanizare2024} recommend adapting new tile-drainage modules to
different soil textures such as clay loam where preferential flow strongly affects
saturated storage and tile flow, adding explicit unsaturated-flow representation
where groundwater is disconnected from surface water, integrating with frozen-soil
and water-quality modules, and expanding to larger areas. \textcite{Reid2012}
stress that more work is needed to quantify the range of macropore flows to tile
drains across soil types and management systems, that seasonal (growing versus
non-growing) hydrologic regimes should be treated as discrete, and that a
framework is offered to encourage others to gather suitable data for testing.
\textcite{Allaire2011} call for testing each preferential-flow component
individually, validating them in the field, and analysing the individual impact of
each component on water quality, including extreme values. \textcite{Ojeda2018}
require a more accurate, independent method for determining daily subsurface
drainage from one layer to the next given preferential flow, plus sensitivity
analysis to identify key parameters needing measurement. \textcite{Ulén2016} note
that quantification of macropore flow paths is necessary for proper understanding
of subsurface-drain leaching losses and that a dynamic model accounting for frozen
soil and air temperature, together with measured data on soil structure and
soil-P, is urgently needed. \textcite{Frey2012} call for characterising near-surface
preferential flow paths across different soil, land-use, and climatic settings, and
\textcite{LESAFFRE1988} note that a better understanding of by-pass flow through
cracks is necessary to extend model application to full-year operation, alongside
continued field experimentation and automatic calibration of water-table shape
coefficients. Nitrogen-cycle and hydrological process representations attract
parallel needs: \textcite{Malone2017} suggest developing a surface-residue
component, a more detailed drainage approach, more thorough investigation of soil
field capacity for denitrification and N losses, and mobile/immobile water phases
to improve nitrate transport; \textcite{Gillette2018} call for more empirical data
on interactions between soil-water retention and the soil-N budget, including
mineralisation and denitrification, and for well-tested models and the use of
soft data and complete N budgets during calibration. Analytical and theoretical
advances -- rigorous theoretical analysis, formulas for sloped and multilayer
soils, and field examination -- are sought for improved subsurface-drainage designs
\parencite{Tao2016,Tao2019}, and time-step selection is flagged as a
study-specific choice requiring pre-assessment \parencite{Awad2022b}. Other
process needs include temporal variability in decay coefficients and deterministic
approaches for land-use effects on tile-drained water quality \parencite{Rudra2005},
improved transient and inversely calibrated representations \parencite{Sedaghatdoost2018},
and machine-learning surrogate models that expand training datasets across diverse
climatic and hydrogeological conditions, incorporate transient processes and
robust/stochastic optimisation, and integrate with real-time control
\parencite{Lei2026}.

\subsection{Long-term, year-round, and multi-scale monitoring}

Across nutrient, salt, and hydrological studies, authors emphasise that short,
seasonally incomplete records are inadequate. \textcite{Kladivko2001} argue that
year-round measurement of water flow and chemical concentration is necessary, for
at least several years on new sites, to determine which parts of the year should
receive management priority, and that tile flow versus surface runoff contributions
must be assessed at watershed scale. \textcite{Sharpley2019} present a broad agenda
calling for a more holistic understanding of subsurface hydrology, year-round data
collection to close water and nutrient balances, continued research on P movement
through drainage systems across soils in the Corn Belt, study of legacy N and P
pools and historical fertiliser effects, quantification of snowmelt-driven losses,
and evaluation of single, stacked, and edge-of-field practices and their
trade-offs. \textcite{Frankenberger2024} provide detailed monitoring
recommendations for drainage water management studies: high-frequency discharge,
surface-runoff, and seepage monitoring to capture the complete water budget;
process-based measurements including microbial activity, denitrification,
metabolism, soil-P pools, organic matter, infiltration rate, and redox potential;
consistent documentation of land management and DWM design (tile spacing, outlet
depth, timing of water retention); and a publicly available dataset supporting
re-analysis. \textcite{Williams2017} call for quantifying the relationship between
field-scale processes and watershed response, detailed management and soil-carbon
information as a priority in watershed water-quality studies, and further work on
dissolved organic carbon dynamics and sources including groundwater seepage and
in-stream production. \textcite{Drake2018} recommend field measurements of
discharge to validate hydrologic models and water monitoring over the entire year
even at reduced frequency, with continued monitoring and modelling to narrow
uncertainty in conservation-practice performance. Long-term monitoring is likewise
sought for edge-of-field and treatment systems -- accumulation and biofouling of
recycled-concrete-aggregate drains \parencite{McCulloch2017}, long-term maturation
of surface-flow constructed wetlands for P retention with attention to locally
available filter materials and cost-efficiency \parencite{Mendes2020}, long-term
performance of stormwater-retention devices \parencite{Xu2022}, and long-term and
negative groundwater-level effects with accumulated multi-field discharge data
\parencite{Okuda2020}. \textcite{Furukawa2008} call for careful evaluation of
leaching components and total carbon dynamics in relation to drainage systems,
including a quantification method for reduced carbon. \textcite{Frankl2023}
recommend tracking soil-property metrics as standard practice, controlling for
tillage, deeper sampling to resolve profile carbon changes, and pairing
water-quality studies with monitoring of changing physical and biological
properties over time.

\subsection{Transferability across soils, climates, crops, and management}

A pervasive limitation is that findings from single sites or narrow conditions
cannot yet be generalised. \textcite{Singh2022b} recommend that controlled-drainage/subirrigation
studies be conducted outside the U.S. Midwest and Canada across diverse climates,
crops, and soils; that additional studies compare irrigation methods in terms of
water supply and water-balance components; and that site characterisation --
including field slope, soil particle-size distribution by horizon, hydraulic
conductivity, drainage class, and soil moisture -- be reported, together with
measures of variability. \textcite{Bilotta2008} stress that work must be repeated
at larger scales and on different soil types as key modulating factors, and
\textcite{Goeken2015} call for investigations across geographic regions, diverse
perennial species and mixtures, and varied precipitation and weather.
\textcite{Nuutinen2001} recommend repeating earthworm-drainage studies on soils of
different texture and under less rainy conditions and including other earthworm
species, while \textcite{Nuutinen2011} seek horizontal-transect observations to
strengthen source-area interpretation and long-term relation of colonisation to
soil structure and hydrology. Climate-focused transferability needs include
high-resolution climate-change scenarios and effects on lodging, erosion, and
agrochemical transport \parencite{Rasmussen2018}; analysis of hydrological-model
contributions to total uncertainty using multiple models and assessment at
additional French sites under different climates and soils \parencite{Jeantet2022};
and validation of drainage-warming and soil-texture effects on crops via further
study and modelling \parencite{Jin2008}. Crop- and practice-specific extensions
are sought for paddy and trench drainage \parencite{Rahimi2024,Jia2023},
mole-drainage durability over longer periods and rainy seasons \parencite{JHA1995},
controlled-drainage principles suited to local crops and agronomy in new regions
\parencite{Sojka2020}, and interactions of crop rotation and tillage on subsurface
drainage \parencite{Ojeda2018}. \textcite{Yu2018} call for further field
evaluation of a salt-flushing method, comparison with intermittent ponding,
sprinkling, and drip irrigation, an analytical solution for generalisation, and
tests in complex heterogeneous soils. Assumptions must be re-verified whenever
models are transferred to new areas \parencite{Kelleners2000,Youssef2006}, and
localised design methods must be developed to reflect local agro-technical
conditions \parencite{Tlapáková2017}.

\subsection{Remote sensing and geophysical mapping of drainage networks}

Detecting and mapping buried drainage systems is a fast-moving frontier where
methods work in some conditions but lack systematic guidelines. \textcite{Wienken2024}
describe a functioning non-invasive detection method as indispensable, call for
urgent development of holistic and efficient methodologies for aging drainage
systems, and recommend combining UAV flights with ground-penetrating radar (GPR)
for improved mapping and characterisation. \textcite{Allred2020} lay out an
extensive UAV agenda: additional visible, multispectral, and thermal-infrared
surveys at multiple farm sites visited repeatedly over two years to define the
range of site conditions under which each imagery type maps drain lines; soil
temperature and emissivity measurements in small test plots of variable soil,
wetness, and cover; advanced processing (band stacking with decorrelation stretch,
minimum-noise-fraction and principal-component transforms); supervised
machine-learning algorithms to distinguish drain-line responses from field-operation
features; and decision-tree guidelines for practitioners. Related UAV/thermal work
seeks site-condition guidelines, test-plot quantification of surface thermal
behaviour, advanced image processing, and machine learning to differentiate
responses \parencite{Allred2021}, and extends thermal-infrared methods
to drainage-outlet detection under Midwestern conditions while assessing the
hydrologic impacts of outlets on ditches and streams \parencite{Allred2022}.
\textcite{Carlsen2024} recommend reconsidering the often-cited 2.54~cm minimum
rainfall for imagery acquisition, investigating deep learning and multitemporal
acquisition for drained-area segmentation and tile detection, exploring synthetic
aperture radar, determining the spatial resolution necessary for tile delineation,
performing more large-scale deep-learning studies, bridging computer-science and
geoscience practices through uniform evaluation standards, and expanding publicly
available drainage-map datasets. Machine-learning mapping across regions is further
advanced by calls for deep-learning and transformer-based encoder-decoder
architectures, higher-resolution imagery (harmonised Landsat/Sentinel-2, Planet,
or aerial), broader spatial and temporal application, and integration into
hydrologic and water-quality modelling frameworks \parencite{Wan2024}, and by
reducing tracing uncertainty through multiple interpreters, ground-truthed
datasets, additional raster covariates (soil type, surface cover, time of year),
wind-turbine training examples, and satellite time series \parencite{Redoloza2023}.
Geophysical evaluations echo these needs: near-surface geophysical data from more
sites and diverse soils \parencite{Allred2005}; systematic testing of antenna
configuration, frequency, and antenna-to-pipe orientation across soil types for
GPR \parencite{Allred2013}; prediction of GPR suitability via electromagnetic
simulation (e.g., gprMax) from EMI measurements and understanding dependence on
soil type, residue, tillage, ground wetness, and prior rainfall
\parencite{Koganti2020,Koganti2021}; concurrent EMI/GPR and complementary
drone-imagery and magnetic-gradiometer surveys \parencite{Koganti2020}; and
resistivity/electromagnetic tracer approaches designed for slope and known drain
orientation \parencite{Jouen2018}. Optical, radar, and topographic covariate
studies also seek more frequent imaging \parencite{Tlapáková2017}, repeated SAR
acquisitions with ground-based measurements to track crop growth across drainage
ridges \parencite{Meunier2001}, UAV-based cover-crop biomass and N-uptake mapping
across landscapes \parencite{Xu2018}, and richer topographic and soil covariates
in statistical drainage models \parencite{Harris2018}.

\subsection{Water balance, seepage, and groundwater interactions}

Closing the water balance -- particularly seepage and groundwater exchange --
remains a persistent data gap. \textcite{Grose2022} call for further exploration
to identify the contribution of groundwater relative to other sources during
stormflows, and \textcite{DaneshYazdi2016} would incorporate groundwater through
double-storage modelling. \textcite{Sedaghatdoost2018} note that providing
groundwater-table data in objective functions would help inverse modelling recover
soil properties more precisely, and \textcite{Feng2018} advocate incorporating
variable lateral seepage. Regional groundwater assessments call for further
radiocarbon sampling and continued rigorous evaluation of changing resource origins
\parencite{Tweed2018}, monitoring of groundwater chemistry at recharge zones of
well-fields for both shallow and deep flow systems \parencite{Dragon2016}, and
additional geologic and hydrologic data plus analysis of deep percolation through
silts and clays \parencite{BOOKMAN1959}. Aquifer characterisation needs include
accurate descriptions of the hydraulic properties of different aquifer layers
\parencite{KAMRA1991} and intensive investigation of the impervious-layer depth to
assess effluent-salinity variation under alternative drainage designs
\parencite{KAMRA1994}. \textcite{Stuetz2004} recommend greater use of test wells to
establish hydrochemical conditions and greater attention to water quality and well
design to minimise biofouling. \textcite{Schilling2022} suggest transient
simulations to test steady-state assumptions and further research on the capacity
of waterways to process excess water, and \textcite{Xu2022} note that updated local
groundwater-dynamics information is necessary for siting retention devices.
Watershed-scale salt and water studies plan continued data collection with
unsteady flow and mass-transport models, sensitivity and stochastic analysis, and
consideration of canal lining, horizontal subsurface drainage, and river-level
management \parencite{Gates2002,Burkhalter2006}. Groundwater as an alternative
water source and the effects of drainage from saline-alkali lands on groundwater
are flagged for investigation \parencite{Zheng2025b}, as are the interrelation of
irrigation practice and drainage design \parencite{Sinai2005} and long-term
groundwater-level effects of new drainage \parencite{Okuda2020}. \textcite{Keller2008}
suggest that long-term amelioration of nitrogen contamination may require perennial
deep-rooted crops and are investigating applicability at larger watershed scales.

\subsection{Experimental design, statistics, and uncertainty quantification}

Several authors identify methodological rather than substantive gaps.
\textcite{Pagliari2021} call for confirming whether beta distributions or time
series are the best approaches for analysing field-scale, unreplicated paired
drainage experiments, or whether new statistical methodologies are needed for
controlled-drainage studies. \textcite{Andrus2014} argue that directed
investigations of specific hypotheses are more productive than replicate spatial
analyses of additional biofilters and recommend more frequent shallow sampling at
specified spacings. \textcite{Okuda2020} note that field experiments with
replication are needed to enable statistical analysis, and \textcite{Wamono2016b}
recommend matching tractor power to implement size, comparing on a percentage
basis, and adding measurements of draft, slip, and trafficability. Uncertainty
quantification recurs strongly: stochastic and sensitivity analyses to address
parametric uncertainty \parencite{Gates2002,Burkhalter2006}, robust/stochastic
optimisation for parameter and input uncertainty \parencite{Lei2026}, explicit
estimation of prediction accuracy and uncertainty in phosphorus models
\parencite{Radcliffe2015}, reduction of interpreter-based mapping uncertainty
\parencite{Redoloza2023}, and analysis of the hydrological model's contribution to
total uncertainty using multiple models \parencite{Jeantet2022}. Improved sampling
and measurement designs are recommended for buffer and riparian studies, including
more sampling wells to capture spatial variability, tracer studies to attribute
flow proportions, and both surface and dissolved N$_2$O measurements
\parencite{Davis2019}, and dye tracing, control wells, and paired free-flowing
watershed comparisons at saturated-buffer sites \parencite{Jacquemin2020}. Improved
model performance is also sought through better evapotranspiration estimation and
additional predictor variables \parencite{Morrison2013}, additional SWAT performance
metrics, parameter refinement, longer observation periods, and larger datasets
\parencite{Zheng2025b}, and independent evaluation of calibrated models against
withheld data \parencite{Schoups2005}. \textcite{Schoups2005} further suggest
spatially distributing parameters to remove trade-offs between shallow and deep
water tables and multi-objective optimisation with additional criteria.

\subsection{Economic feasibility, management data, and databases}

A final cluster of needs concerns the socio-economic and data-infrastructure
context of drainage research. Economic feasibility analyses are repeatedly
requested: cost-benefit analysis of surface amendments and energy savings
\parencite{Wamono2016b}, regional economic benefits of drainage water recycling
under future climate and droughts with optimised reservoir dimensions
\parencite{Kosari2024}, overall economic benefit and inter-BMP interactions of
integrated systems \parencite{Li2021b}, comprehensive cost-benefit analysis of
subsurface drainage accounting for short- and long-term effects
\parencite{Guo2025}, economic feasibility of drainage as a preventive measure
against salinisation \parencite{Datta2000,HalbacCotoaraZamfir2022}, cost and
consumption in paddy fields \parencite{Jia2023}, cost-efficiency of edge-of-field
technologies \parencite{Mendes2020}, and cost reduction and technology
sophistication \parencite{Okuda2020}. Detailed management and soil data are
emphasised as prerequisites for reliable inference: verification of soil
information by field sampling and farmer records with accurate management data
\parencite{JORJANI1991}, detailed agricultural-management information and soil-carbon
change as watershed-study priorities \parencite{Williams2017}, sound pedological
and geological knowledge for interpreting drainage concentrations
\parencite{Jacobsen2007}, and richer site characterisation and variability
reporting \parencite{Singh2022b}. Data-infrastructure and regionalisation needs are
prominent in the German mapping work of \textcite{Hirt2005,Hirt2005b,Hirt2011},
which calls for comprehensive databases of tile-drained land proportions by soil
type, validation of existing databases, extrapolation from eastern to western
Germany and to European soil maps at the mesoscale, and inclusion of drained areas
in water- and nutrient-flux models so that mitigation measures can be designed; the
approach is transferable where representative drainage data and medium-scale soil
maps exist. Coordinated, coupled field-and-simulation research programmes are
called for to advance drainage water recycling across hydrology, crop-yield,
water-quality, complementary, and implementation categories, with monitoring across
sites and climates to document downstream load reductions and reservoir behaviour
\parencite{Hay2021}, alongside soil-moisture-sensor and remote monitoring of
control structures, validation under increasing climate variability, and
additional well-characterised sites \parencite{Willison2021}. Continued monitoring
of salinity and multi-scale water quality is essential to refine drainage-reuse
strategy \parencite{ElAgha2020}, and forestry, land-use, and topographic effects on
runoff generation and nitrogen retention require further analysis in Nordic and
Baltic settings \parencite{Deelstra2014,Deelstra2014b}. Site-specific reservoir and
constructed-wetland sizing \parencite{Haverstock2017}, coupling of soil-water
simulation with management-scenario testing \parencite{Bulot2017}, verification of
sulphur-loss and other process models under a range of climatic, management, and
soil conditions \parencite{SMITH1983}, and evaluation of forest water-management
systems and best management practices \parencite{MCCARTHY1992} round out the
distributed set of data and validation needs that define this research frontier.

### Material: Subsurface Drainage Flow Modeling Research Gaps

\subsection{Overview}

Despite decades of field experimentation and steady advances in numerical
simulation, the community that studies water movement in subsurface (tile)
drainage systems consistently identifies a set of persistent, mutually
reinforcing knowledge gaps. Across studies spanning humid temperate cropland,
arid and semi-arid irrigated plains, saline coastal soils, and tropical
oases, authors converge on a common message: the processes that most strongly
govern drain hydrographs---preferential and macropore flow, spatially and
temporally variable soil hydraulic properties, and the coupling between soil
pore-water flow, pipe flow, and surface water---remain incompletely
characterised and imperfectly represented in the models used to guide design.
This section frames the research frontier around five interlocking lines of
inquiry that recur throughout the literature: (i) the characterisation and
mechanistic modelling of preferential and macropore flow; (ii) the need for
coupled surface--subsurface and pipe-flow hydraulic models, including
pressurised-flow and open-ditch processes; (iii) the quantification of
spatial and temporal heterogeneity in soil hydraulic properties and the data
required to constrain it; (iv) improved calibration, validation, and
long-term monitoring protocols; and (v) the revision of design criteria and
management strategies, including controlled drainage, novel drainage
structures, and adaptation to climate change. A cross-cutting theme is that
progress in each area is limited less by conceptual understanding than by the
availability of field data at the right spatial and temporal resolution to
test, calibrate, and generalise the models. Headline priorities include
mechanistic characterisation of macropore--drain connectivity, coupling of
in-pipe surface flow with soil pore-water flow, denser and depth-resolved
hydraulic-conductivity sampling, longer continuous monitoring records, and the
explicit incorporation of climate-change scenarios into design.

\subsection{Preferential and macropore flow: characterisation and modelling}

The representation of preferential flow is perhaps the most frequently cited
frontier. \textcite{Gaillot2023} argue that the relative contribution of
preferential flow to drains needs to be better characterised as a function of
soil type, soil water content, and agricultural practice, and that models must
then be tested for extrapolation to other fields while accounting for soil
variability and drainage-network characteristics. \textcite{Fox2004}
similarly call for the degree of direct connectivity between macropores and
subsurface drains to be characterised using more mechanistic approaches, for
the ``express fraction'' to be evaluated under varying saturated--unsaturated
flow conditions above the tile drain, and for the modified model to be tested
under different climate, soil, drain-configuration, and chemical-application
conditions. In the U.S. Midwest, \textcite{Askar2020} recommend further
testing of the simple empirical approaches DRAINMOD uses to represent
macropore flow, emphasising that rigorous testing requires water-table-depth
measurements alongside drainage and surface-runoff data, direct field
measurement of macropore-flow inputs, and detailed data on crack geometry,
temporal crack dynamics with soil moisture, and the area contributing to
surface runoff.

Improvements to macropore process descriptions are proposed across several
model frameworks. \textcite{Li2023d} call for further study of preferential
flow in macropore channels and appropriate modification of the RZWQM2
macropore component---better partitioning of soil water between matrix and
macropore flow, improved macropore channel flow to shorten the drainage-peak
recession time, and calibration of macropore parameters against sub-hourly
drainage data. At watershed scale, \textcite{Poon2021} suggest adjusting the
percolation algorithm to account for dynamic macropore connectivity or
effective macroporosity and to include a soil-moisture factor describing
matrix--macropore water transfer during the growing season, noting that
experimental measurements of soil macroporosity and site-specific measurements
of major soil types and management practices are needed to select appropriate
connectivity values. \textcite{Villholth1998b} propose including small-scale
diffusion across the micropore space or adding more domains to the model
description, while cautioning that such remedies demand more elaborate models
and more parameters, and stress the importance of site-specific measurements
of matrix and macropore hydraulic conductivity. \textcite{Villholth1998}
further note that the dynamics of macropore structure at the soil surface and
their influence on chemical transport deserve additional study, and that
whether vertical macropore transport occurs in deeper saturated layers---
especially in areas dominated by regional groundwater recharge---remains to be
investigated.

Several groups highlight structural and dynamic controls on macroporosity that
models neglect. \textcite{Holbak2021} recommend including hysteresis effects,
investigating the interrelationship between biopore-flow initiation and
hysteresis under laboratory and field conditions, and validating biopore
models across the soil profile against datasets combining biopore
measurements, soil-water dynamics, and solute leaching in drained fields.
\textcite{Zhao2001} raise the possibility that additional years of manure
application could significantly improve soil structure and macropore
development, and \textcite{Wang2022} identify the reestablishment of
macropores and nutrient transfer through cracks as targets for improving EPIC
simulation precision. \textcite{Varvaris2018} call for scrutiny of the
calibrated saturated hydraulic conductivity of macropores---and the resulting
indirect estimate of potential preferential-pathway flow---and propose a
three-dimensional dual-permeability model to capture spatial geological
layering, lateral macropore-flow velocities, and non-systematically
tile-drained fields. Tracer- and geophysics-based identification of
preferential pathways is repeatedly recommended: \textcite{Tähtikarhu2025}
suggest ground-penetrating radar and tracers for flowpath identification and
the inclusion of preferential flowpaths, more comprehensive open-ditch
processes, and soil-frost processes to improve model representation, while
\textcite{Salo2017} argue that direct measurements of flow routes are more
useful than aggregated discharge for calibrating spatially variable parameters
and that tracer-tracked pathways require solute-transport simulation.

\subsection{Coupled surface--subsurface, pipe-flow, and open-ditch hydraulics}

A recurrent structural gap is the absence of models that couple soil
pore-water flow with surface flow inside drains and ditches.
\textcite{Xin2016b} call for future model development to couple surface water
flow in drains with pore-water flow in soils to represent the temporally
varying hydraulic-head condition around the drain, to extend analytical
solutions to include rainfall and evapotranspiration via source and sink terms
for combined drainage and infiltration, and to develop a more comprehensive
treatment of heterogeneous soils, complex pore-water flow, and uncertainty in
spatially variable capillary-fringe thickness and capillary rise.
\textcite{Nousiainen2015} propose enabling simulation of water flow in drain
pipes within FLUSH to capture pressurised flow and clogging effects, adding a
description of open-ditch networks to extend the model to larger areas, and
assigning distinct potential-evapotranspiration series to areas under
different crops. Pressurised-flow dynamics are a specific frontier:
\textcite{Henine2010} argue that coupling subsurface pipes with a feedback
linkage to the water table could introduce a validated temporary-retention
function under conditions exceeding design capacity and broaden the scope of
drainage-hydrology investigations, and \textcite{Henine2014} add that a
complete study of drainage overpressure at the catchment outlet should account
for surface runoff and its interaction with the water table during pipe
pressurisation. \textcite{Saadat2018} similarly suggest separately analysing
periods when pipes are pressurised using pressure-transducer data.

Entrance resistance and near-drain flow representation remain unresolved for
older and heterogeneous systems. Entrance
resistance must be considered when modelling old drainage systems---for
example by assigning a higher pressure head to water in gravel at the drain
boundary---and a smaller element size is needed near the drain for
detailed pressure-head study. \textcite{Messing2006} raise the question of
whether heavy-rain water transport should be characterised by a rapid onset of
flow through the permeable backfill trench followed by slower, continuous
transport from the inter-trench zone, and how this partitioning influences
drain-water quality. The choice of model dimensionality is itself a research
question: \textcite{Alakukku2010} advise a two- or three-dimensional approach
when predicting the proportional fractions of drain flow and surface runoff or
simulating groundwater level in sloping fields, and \textcite{Carlier2007}
call for further development before applying their method to larger domains
such as networks of drain plots, simpler modelling of drainage collectors, a
better representation of soils with extreme hydrodynamic parameters, and a more
precise averaging of hydraulic conductivities for unsaturated flow.
\textcite{FIPPS1989} note that the regions at the top and bottom of a slope,
and the entire flow region when the water table falls below the drains, require
additional analysis. Broader integrating frameworks are also sought:
\textcite{Manguerra1998} argue that SWAT should be extended to include and
directly handle the physical processes governing water movement to subsurface
drains, expecting better performance from explicit representation, and
\textcite{SAVABI1993} report that subsurface lateral water movement and
rainfall interception---absent from the WEPP hydrology model---are being
incorporated, alongside a need for better hydraulic-roughness estimation for
runoff routing.

\subsection{Spatial and temporal heterogeneity of soil hydraulic properties}

Nearly every modelling study attributes residual error to unresolved
heterogeneity in soil hydraulic properties, particularly hydraulic
conductivity. \textcite{DeSchepper2017} suggest that a more detailed
representation of macropores, fractures, sand lenses, and other
heterogeneities could improve reproduction of observed hydraulic-head
variability, and that including specific storage and residual saturation in
calibration would improve performance. \textcite{Wesström2003} note that other
approaches are needed to account for variation of properties such as hydraulic
conductivity with depth, and \textcite{Karandish2018} propose increasing the
number of soil layers or gradually varying hydraulic parameters, adopting a
dual-porosity model for paddy soils, and accounting for temporal variation in
hydraulic parameters during simulation. Several authors call explicitly for
denser and depth-resolved sampling: \textcite{PalaciosVélez2004} recommend at
least one hydraulic-conductivity measurement per hectare (five per plot for
plots under 5~ha) and a more detailed study of the spatial distribution of
conductivity, the location of the impermeable layer, and the appropriate
drain-spacing approach, together with post-construction comparison of designed
and observed conductivity and water-table height. \textcite{Messing2006} argue
that more points along the transect between trench lines are needed to capture
spatial variability, and \textcite{Tähtikarhu2025} call for more measurements
of vertical hydraulic conductivity, measurement of horizontal conductivities,
and a more sophisticated description of water-retention properties that
accounts for hydrophobicity and hysteresis at field scale.

Anisotropy and long-term change in soil properties form a related sub-frontier.
\textcite{Akis2015} call for studies of long-term spatial variability under
drained and undrained conditions with anisotropy, a vadose-zone hydrological
model to understand salt-movement and water-flow relations to saturated
conductivity, and understanding of spatial anisotropy in the drainage--
infiltration relationship in the upper 1.5~m. \textcite{Moustafa1998} likewise
call for study of long-term spatial variation of soil-water properties as
affected by subsurface drainage and closer-spaced sampling to resolve true
spatial structure. \textcite{Koivusalo1999} recommend relating soil hydraulic
conductivity to root-zone deficit, describing time-dependent soil-structure
changes during drying, and obtaining measured field-scale values for
macroporous clayey soil. \textcite{DeBoer2006} stress that saturated hydraulic
conductivity and the relationship between drainage volume and upward flux must
be defined with reasonable accuracy, supported by a comprehensive soil survey
as DRAINMOD input. At watershed scale, \textcite{Schilling2015} suggest
incorporating subsoil porosity variations by linking porosity to SSURGO
bulk-density data and spatially refining model results with greater horizontal
and vertical resolution of soil hydraulic properties.

\subsection{Calibration, validation, parameterisation, and data needs}

Beyond the properties themselves, authors emphasise improved calibration
strategy and the acquisition of complementary observations.
\textcite{Boico2022} recommend calibration with parameter-estimation software
to overcome poor representation of soil hydraulic properties and the
implementation of multiple conceptual models to represent plausible
heterogeneity structures. \textcite{Schneider2022} propose a more flexible
parameterisation for drain-sensitive parameters such as the conductivity of
the uppermost geologic layers, a fully distributed conductivity field with
pilot-point calibration, additional observation types such as soil moisture to
reduce overparameterisation, finer horizontal and vertical resolutions with a
more complex unsaturated-zone description and explicit drain representation,
and integration of proxy datasets such as machine-learning predictions of
drain probability or average drain flow with physically based models.
\textcite{DeSchepper2017} suggest combining catchment- and drainage-discharge
calibration, and \textcite{Sedaghatdoost2019} recommend using drainage
salinity data alongside other measured data in the objective function.
\textcite{Brighenti2023} propose testing alternative objective functions such
as the Kling--Gupta efficiency and establishing relationships between
plot-scale measured tile flow and watershed-scale calculated tile flow.

Complementary in-situ observations are repeatedly identified as limiting.
\textcite{Askar2020} stress that water-table-depth measurements must accompany
drainage and surface-runoff data. \textcite{Salo2017} argue that more output
variables such as groundwater level should be considered when assessing
drainage practices. \textcite{Varvaris2018} identify a need to determine the
unknown source continuously recharging a tile network during dry periods, to
obtain in-situ soil-water-content measurements for a more reliable calibrated
flow model, and to run conservative-tracer tests with solute-transport
simulation. \textcite{Hwang2024} call for extended field observations---soil
profiling, hydraulic conductivity, water-retention curves, infiltration rates,
water-table fluctuations, and soil-moisture change over time---and a
complementary modelling study of how drainage design influences hydrology and
crop yield. Several authors emphasise that model claims must be tested against
field data before recommendations are made: \textcite{Askar2021} state that
the potential limitations of DRAINMOD-P must be evaluated against
field-measured data prior to recommending further modifications;
\textcite{Salazar2008} caution that tests on different soil types are needed
before recommending DRAINMOD runs with ROSETTA-estimated saturated
conductivity; \textcite{Singh2020b} and \textcite{Malota2024} call for
additional testing across regions, soils, and climates; and
\textcite{Holbak2021} require further field and laboratory experiments to
validate models across the soil profile. \textcite{Sinai2006} recommend
verifying that field conditions match the assumptions underlying DRAINMOD, and
\textcite{He2002} note that models other than DRAINMOD should be used when
groundwater flow lines must be computed across a landscape.

\subsection{Long-term monitoring and revision of design criteria}

A strong current in the literature is that short-term studies mislead and that
design guidance must rest on long records. \textcite{Wang2006b} argue that
drainage design should not be based on year-by-year short-term DRAINMOD
simulations, that long-term runs give better average yield predictions to
guide spacing, and that the benefits of controlled drainage must be
demonstrated and quantified locally. \textcite{Ale2009} call for analysing
drainage water management under wider spacings (20--40~m) over an approximately
100-year climatic record and for exploring uncertainty in vertical-conductivity
calibration. \textcite{Häggblom2019} note that studying interannual
variability in supplementary drainage requires longer simulation periods with
hourly input data, and \textcite{Malota2015} state that long simulation
periods are required to assess shallow-water-table control and that verifying
design criteria is paramount before adoption. \textcite{Salo2019} recommend a
longer monitoring period to reveal long-term effects of installation method,
studying variation between sections of the same drainage method to separate
machinery effects, and automatic measurements to compute drainage response time
after rain events. Long-term monitoring and
evaluation of drainage configurations are emphasised to validate results and
refine design recommendations, and \textcite{Kladivko2023} caution against attributing
drainage responses to a management practice that occurred only shortly before.
\textcite{Chang2012} call for longer observation of water and salt movement in
deep soil layers, and \textcite{Deelstra2015} anticipate further analysis of
soil physical data and better description of the interaction between weather,
soil physical conditions, groundwater behaviour, and drain discharge as
prerequisites for correct design under climate change.

Many site studies conclude that established design criteria require
re-evaluation. \textcite{Christen2001} call for new drainage design criteria
protecting crops while minimising drain-water salinity and volume,
reassessment of criteria in light of land-use and irrigation change, and novel
saline-water disposal options. \textcite{Muhammad2021} recommend updating
criteria and decreasing drain depth to 120~cm at some sites and 100~cm at
others while holding spacing constant, and \textcite{Malota2016} call for
revisiting design criteria to account for land slope and for recalculating
drain spacing (currently 54~m in clay and 74~m in clay-loam soils).
\textcite{Bahçeci2009} argue that a lower drain-discharge rate should inform
future design in the Harran Plain, while \textcite{Kahlown2002} conclude that
subsurface drainage interventions must not go deeper than 2~m and that findings
still require field and laboratory confirmation. \textcite{Mante2022} state
that the recommended 12--15~m (up to 90~m) spacing for sandy loams must be
reviewed and paired with controlled-traffic farming at narrower spacing
($\le$12~m, 0.9~m depth), and \textcite{Nelson2017} call for determining the
cost-effectiveness of tile spacings below 6.1~m in claypan soils.
\textcite{HajAmor2019} identify a need to design new drainage systems---
including standard guidelines for depth and spacing---and to test them in situ
before extension, and \textcite{Skaggs2017} advocate standardising the
definition of the drainage coefficient and the consistent use of the Kirkham
coefficient and drainage intensity to enable comparison across studies.
\textcite{GALLICHAND1992b} suggest implementing design procedures within
CAD/GIS-based drainage-design software.

\subsection{Controlled drainage, drainage water management, and novel structures}

Controlled drainage and drainage water management (DWM) constitute a
management frontier where hydrologic understanding is still maturing.
\textcite{Youssef2021} call for well-coordinated experimental and modelling
research on how seepage affects controlled-drainage performance for reducing
flow and nitrogen losses, and for monitoring guard lines of free-drainage plots
to evaluate lateral seepage from managed to non-managed field portions;
\textcite{Pease2017} likewise cite field experiments as necessary to represent
seepage in discharge reduction and propose examining slow spring
profile-drawdown strategies. \textcite{Lahdou2019} recommend examining
potential increases in overland flow due to controlled drainage through field
measurement or detailed modelling, and \textcite{Ale2009} note the influence of
vertical-conductivity uncertainty on the DWM contribution to seepage.
\textcite{Craft2018} argue that modelling excess-moisture stress is vital to
understanding drawbacks of DWM adoption, that drainage routines within a
controlled-outlet system may need re-examination to simulate volume when the
outlet opens in spring, and that spring nitrogen loading is a priority.
A further priority is extending representative Midwest soils,
including multiple GCMs, verifying the model's ability to simulate DWM effects
on yield and drainage volume with field data, and evaluating climate-change
impacts on drainage-climate outputs. \textcite{VandeCraats2021} propose
expanding anticipatory drainage-base management to include evapotranspiration,
subirrigation, spatially variable seepage, and heterogeneity, and to address
which weather forecasts and forecast lengths to use and how forecast
uncertainty affects confidence in optimal strategies. \textcite{Salla2022}
call for investigating how collector-ditch water-level management can increase
controlled-drainage efficiency and how control schedules affect the
groundwater table seasonally and under future conditions, and
\textcite{Strock2025b} identify multiple and irregularly timed inflow sources
as a knowledge gap and advocate automation of low-grade weirs to adjust
drainage intensity responsively. \textcite{Güngör2023} caution that
controlled drainage should not be overlooked because a very rapid water-table
decrease may increase irrigation demand in dry summers, and
\textcite{Dou2023} note the need to adjust drainage depth, spacing, and period
to local characteristics.

Novel drainage structures and materials define a further set of open
questions. \textcite{Ren2023} call for improving HYDRUS-2D boundary
conditions, studying whether the negative pressure of siphon-type drainage
changes during head drawdown, verifying siphon drainage in the field, and
considering pipe perforation rate and geotextile specifications.
\textcite{Tao2025b} recommend long-term measurement to determine the working
time of their system relative to construction costs and to balance reduced
waterlogging with soil-water retention and pollution reduction, and
\textcite{Li2021} propose new structures that adjust water-holding capacity,
permeability, and aeration while eliminating capillary barriers and hysteresis,
inhibiting rapid vertical movement and bypass flow so that water converges
horizontally into the pipe. Multi-level and bi-level configurations warrant
further study: \textcite{Hornbuckle2007} call for investigating multi-level
systems under differing conditions, actively managing shallow and deep drains,
and increasing deep-drain spacing commensurate with shallow-drain drainage,
and \textcite{Verma1998} suggest bi-level drainage as controlled drainage via
the depth of the shallow drain. Envelope and perforation design remain open:
\textcite{Alavi2021} recommend testing the Hydroluis drainpipe under different
gradients, rates, soils, and inter-pipe distances; \textcite{Wu2022} call for
considering envelope-material conductivity, soil textures, and perforation
shape and pattern; \textcite{Oyarce2017} suggest installing an experimental
prototype in an actual soil profile and evaluating different screen types; and
\textcite{HajAmor2019} recommend replacing existing systems with geotextile
envelopes to prevent sand clogging. Numerical techniques can characterise fluid behaviour in larger module
arrangements and flow conditions without expensive laboratory experiments, and
\textcite{Nelson2011b} observe that a drainage/subirrigation system should have
remained in drainage mode longer than tested.

Optimal layout and burial-depth determination in saline and problem soils
recur in recent Chinese studies. \textcite{Tian2023} stress considering the
appropriate ditching depth when determining optimal pipe-layout parameters and
selecting it according to actual soil conditions, and \textcite{Tian2024}
recommend burying the pipe as far above the restrictive/critical layer as
possible when shallow, breaking a shallow restrictive layer by deep ploughing,
and combining methods to enhance drainage. \textcite{Tian2023c} identify
layout-parameter selection as the next research target and suggest amendments
such as organic fertiliser, straw biochar, or low-permeability mulch film, and
\textcite{Zhang2024d} call for adding factors such as buried-pipe depth and
fertiliser ratios to improve the saline-soil improvement system.
\textcite{Zhai2023} advocate optimising hidden-pipe layout through a
multi-scheme decision model and distinguishing nitrous-oxide-emission sources,
and \textcite{Han2023} plan to include construction cost when clarifying the
appropriate range of buried depths. \textcite{Qian2025b} recommend improving
the design drainage objective when using models to set pipe parameters,
\textcite{Ren2021} call for studying combination forms under different rainfall
and unsaturated initial conditions using mathematical models of economic
factors, and \textcite{Zheng2025} propose field experiments on the interaction
between drain spacing and outlet height to develop water- and salt-regulation
control strategies. \textcite{SerranoCoronel2018} state that water-movement
behaviour under different configurations must be studied, and
\textcite{Zegeye2021} suggest modifying drain depth and spacing using better
soil-permeability information and integrating bank dewatering with other gully
rehabilitation measures.

\subsection{Watershed-scale representation, drain mapping, and connectivity}

Scaling drainage processes from plot to catchment raises its own frontier.
\textcite{Muma2016} defer the distributed impact of subsurface drainage on
catchment behaviour to further work, and \textcite{RevueltaAcosta2021} propose
extending the WEPP model to quantify subsurface drainage under controlled water
tables and at watershed scale, and to improve estimates under shallow water
tables during winter. \textcite{Thomas2016} note that describing basin shape
and topographic impacts on tile-drainage contribution to streamflow requires
further research and that calibration and validation for prediction remain to
be investigated. \textcite{Rahman2014} offer their coupling of a watershed
model with streamflow-response analysis as a transferable approach for
evaluating land-use and climate impacts, and \textcite{Schilling2015} identify
the effect of regularly spaced tile drainage on groundwater travel times as an
open question. Streamflow prediction in tile-drained landscapes remains
difficult: \textcite{Wessel2022} state that improving predictions of
subsurface runoff through tile drains is a pressing need, that the validity of
the curve-number approach at different locations must be confirmed, and that
whether a modified curve-number method or a more process-based model is
required must be determined.

Locating and inventorying drainage networks is a prerequisite for many of
these analyses. \textcite{Yimer2023} call for a global study of inter-regional
differences, methods other than interpolation to fill missing values,
approaches to differentiate tile from ditch drains using soil-moisture
attributes, and local groundwater-level measurements in preference to global
datasets. \textcite{Kratt2020} aim to better constrain the optimal
meteorological and soil-water conditions for successful drainage-pipe
detection and to quantify feedbacks between albedo, soil moisture, and heat
transfer to predict optimal data-collection timing, and
\textcite{Allred2018b} note that textural analysis of soil cores is needed to
confirm supposed texture differences, that mapping efficiency could improve
with an all-terrain-vehicle-mounted sled, and that widespread adoption might
yield insight on drainage-practice intensity at small watershed scales.
Surface-ditch connectivity is highlighted as a management-relevant frontier:
\textcite{Moloney2020} argue that mapping surface ditches may be more efficient
than locating individual in-field drains, that future phosphorus-loss and
risk-assessment models should include connectivity metrics via surface ditches,
and that policymakers must consider lag times in nutrient release.
\textcite{Opoku2024} propose applying a bespoke integrated connectivity risk
ranking for phosphorus and nitrogen along open ditches and further assessing
the temporal and spatial variability of soil, weather, drainage system, and
hydrogeochemistry. \textcite{Calsamiglia2018} call for improving connectivity
(IC) algorithms for Mediterranean lowland catchments, continuous monitoring to
establish IC thresholds, and further investigation of thresholds representing
the (dis)activation of connected areas by rainfall frequency and magnitude.

\subsection{Closed depressions, potholes, and edge-of-field practices}

Drained closed depressions and potholes are singled out as poorly understood.
\textcite{Martin2019} argue that the hydrologic behaviour of drained closed
depressions must be better understood to inform conservation practices, that
the frequency, extent, and mechanisms of inundation matter for management, and
that future modelling cannot assume uniformity of potholes at watershed scale.
\textcite{Williams2020} call for research on surface and subsurface hydrologic
processes in drained closed depressions at additional locations, quantification
of the relationship between surface runoff, subsurface tile drainage, and
antecedent soil moisture, and data on the longevity of blind inlets under
no-tillage. \textcite{Feyereisen2015b} report ongoing research on nutrient and
agrochemical fate, alternative nutrient management, the longevity of blind and
gravel inlets, and the effect of a subsurface drainage network on nutrient
losses in closed depressions. Edge-of-field and treatment-system design also
present gaps: \textcite{Gordon2021} suggest raising outlet boards to hold more
water and allow more infiltration, planning for both surface and shallow
groundwater flow, sealing the bottom where groundwater contamination is a
concern, and further studying combined surface/subsurface nitrogen removal with
phosphorus-removing plant harvest; \textcite{Feyereisen2015} call for verifying
design retention times for agricultural residues and incorporating the most
appropriate hydraulic conductivity into bioreactor design models; and
\textcite{Bhattarai2009} note that placement of subsurface drainage beneath
vegetative filter strips needs careful consideration, that uniform surface flow
without preferential paths is essential, and that a filter strip without
subsurface drainage may be effective where rapid water-table lowering is not
required.

\subsection{Climate change, coupled processes, and integrative directions}

Adapting drainage design to a changing climate is an explicit priority.
Climate-change effects and region-specific action plans must be a fundamental
component of drainage design and sizing, and
\textcite{Deelstra2015} tie correct design to understanding weather--soil--
groundwater--discharge interactions under increased precipitation.
\textcite{Tuohy2018} recommend assessing software applicability across a wider
range of Irish drainage systems and soils, quantifying drainage-efficiency
implications for grassland productivity, trafficability, and return on
investment, and using such models to assess climate-change impacts on design
and performance. \textcite{Malota2024} likewise call for assessing model
performance over longer periods and different climates and soils and for
investigating climate-change and soil effects on drainage performance and crop
yields.

Coupled and integrative process representations round out the frontier.
\textcite{Moroizumi2004} note that water-vapour movement and a coupled heat-and-
water-flow model are required for relatively dry soils, and
\textcite{Koivusalo1999} call for a time lag before the steady-state soil-
moisture profile is reached during dry periods and a more consistent
potential-evapotranspiration procedure such as Penman--Monteith.
\textcite{Salo2015} highlight the ability to connect hydraulic functions to
solute variables to enable simulation of density-driven flow, and
\textcite{Varvaris2018} envisage an integrated three-dimensional
hydrobiogeochemical model of solute transport and nitrogen dynamics.
\textcite{Zhang2024} call for addressing intermittent ponding,
evapotranspiration, soil heterogeneity, and three-dimensional flow effects, as
well as uncertainty in the mass-transfer-rate coefficient, and
\textcite{Afruzi2014} note that ponded surface drainage by trapezoidal ditches
warrants further research, while \textcite{Chahar2010} indicate that partially
penetrating ditches require a different analytical solution and that slope
stability may be addressed by refilling ditches with free-draining material.
Model improvement for specific outputs is also flagged: \textcite{Verma2014}
anticipate future model versions enabling more detailed scientific evaluation
of drainage projects, and \textcite{Nousiainen2015} call for further study of
distributed soil hydraulic properties on inefficiently functioning drains.
\textcite{Augeard2005} plan further experiments to confirm winter runoff and
validate suspended-sediment-concentration evolution as a surface-runoff
indicator, supported by hydrological modelling of runoff and pressure heads.

Finally, several authors emphasise that technical advances must be coupled with
socio-economic considerations, extension, and demonstration.
\textcite{BenAissa2013} advocate a participatory approach to design, testing
mixed traditional-ditch and modern-pipe systems and adequate envelopes in situ,
and designing systems for easy farmer inspection and maintenance, echoed by
\textcite{HajAmor2019} on raising farmer and stakeholder awareness of water and
soil management. \textcite{MCFARLANE1992} call for greater extension through
demonstrations and documented case studies and for better research on drains'
effects on salinity, erosion, structural decline, and phosphate export, and
\textcite{Mante2018} note that field managers must weigh the benefit of
narrower spacing against impacts on field operations and long-term yield.
Cost feasibility is a recurring concern---for changing drainage layout, and
\textcite{Imran2021} stresses it specifically for redesigning main drains for
free gravity outflow---and \textcite{Malota2015} suggest linking DRAINMOD
outputs to a drainage-system cost-minimisation model. \textcite{Lamsodis2011}
add operational recommendations, including annual drain-mouth cleaning and
managed control of beaver populations, while \textcite{DarziNaftchali2017}
argue that surface- and groundwater susceptibility to nitrogen losses should be
a major criterion in drain depth and spacing design and that measures are
needed at first planting after installation. \textcite{HARRIS1993} note that
an increased drainage design standard may be necessary to avoid pipe damage and
that direct drilling on sloping undrained land should be avoided, and
\textcite{CAMP1982} stress ensuring adequate surface and internal drainage for
flat-planted sugarcane. Collectively these directions point toward an
integrated research agenda in which improved process representation, denser and
longer-term data, and explicit economic and management context advance
together.

### Material: Controlled Drainage and Irrigation Water Management

\subsection{Overview}
Integrating controlled drainage (CD) with irrigation water management is repeatedly framed less as a settled practice than as a research frontier: the recurring call is to move away from passive, one-way ``unilateral'' subsurface drainage toward systems that give farmers active, location-specific control over the water table and over the coupled irrigation--drainage water balance \parencite{Ayars2006b,Kesicka2023b,Ritzema2023}. Across arid, semiarid, humid, and irrigated command-area settings, authors converge on a small set of open problems: (i) developing design criteria, structures, and decision-support tools for \emph{active} rather than passive management \parencite{Ayars2006b,Youssef2023,Saadat2017}; (ii) automating and data-driving water-balance control through sensors and models \parencite{Youssef2023,Ikonen2023,Isomäki2024}; (iii) coordinating irrigation with drainage adjustments, including subirrigation embedded in regional water systems \parencite{Wahba2005,deWit2022,deWit2024}; (iv) sustaining drainage-water reuse and salinity control while protecting downstream water quality \parencite{WESTCOT1988,Sharma1998,Ayars2015}; (v) adapting design to changing crop water needs, climate extremes, and irrigation modernization \parencite{Rui2024,Ritzema2007,Ziaeifar2025}; and (vi) resolving the institutional, economic, and participatory barriers that condition adoption \parencite{Ritzema2011,Castellano2019,Qureshi2008}. A cross-cutting message is that many recommendations remain qualitative and site-specific, and that long-term, multi-scale evidence is still lacking. Kesicka \textcite{Kesicka2023b} frames replacing unilateral drainage with methods of controlling drainage outflows as a current global challenge, and continued research is broadly expected to keep refining drainage techniques to optimize agricultural water use.

\subsection{From passive to active, adaptive management}
A foundational research direction is the transition from ``no or passive management to active management with defined water management objectives'' \parencite{Ayars2006b}. \textcite{Ayars2006b} argue that structures for controlling water-table position should be \emph{required} in new drainage design and considered for retrofitting existing systems where practical, and that there is a pressing need to develop new design criteria and management methods for controlled drainage to support sustainable irrigated agriculture---a claim they note requires long-term field studies, possibly combined with modeling, for validation. Similar reasoning appears in system-design recommendations to include structures for controlling groundwater-table (GWT) position in new subsurface drainage designs \parencite{Dukhovny2018}, to incorporate ``more options for control'' so farmers can optimize integrated water management \parencite{Ritzema2023}, and to expand ``changeable controlled drainage networks'' across arable lands \parencite{Mahmoud2021}. \textcite{Strock2011} anticipate that producers may eventually view controlled and reduced drainage as soil- and water-management \emph{tools} rather than mitigation practices, and argue that fine-tuning drainage intensity should be a priority in designing all new systems.

The quantitative payoff of active management remains an open question. \textcite{Watkins2024} call for research on the crop-yield benefits achievable by actively managing the subsurface CD outlet during extremely wet periods---hypothesizing that active management could minimize wet-stress on roots and raise yields---and separately for study of how shallow drainage combined with CD affects drainage and nutrient loading and downstream water quality. \textcite{Saadat2017} highlight the need to examine the cost--benefit trade-off of active management, and \textcite{Helmers2012} suggest that yield reductions observed under CD could likely be mitigated with greater management of the system, while cautioning that installation cost, producer management burden, and the acreage of applicability must be weighed before broad implementation. Seasonally differentiated control is another explicit hypothesis: \textcite{Asgari2021} propose free drainage for quick waterlogging control in normal and wet seasons, CD in dry seasons to alleviate deficit stress, and possibly supplemental surface ditches and supplemental irrigation to manage the extremes.

\subsection{Sensors, automation, and decision-support systems}
Several groups identify automation and data-driven control as the next technological step. \textcite{Youssef2023} call for a new generation of drainage control structures and decision-support tools for active management of CD during the growing season, for sensor-based, data-driven water-management systems on poorly drained soils with frequently shallow water tables, and more broadly for technologically advanced systems that adapt to changing weather patterns. \textcite{Ayars2015} likewise identify decision-support systems for adaptive control of integrated irrigation and drainage systems as a need. In Finland, \textcite{Ikonen2023} lay out a concrete research agenda: improved dynamic modeling and data assimilation of the field water balance, a feasible water-balance control-problem formulation (cost function and constraints), fusing control automation with the physical drainage control-well infrastructure, and gaining practical experience from end-users and experimental peatland agriculture. \textcite{Isomäki2024} argue for data-driven water-management systems and appropriate control schedules for combined controlled subsurface drainage and open-ditch damming, noting that proactive management of control structures according to meteorological conditions can increase the probability of keeping water-table depth in the optimal range. Automatic water-depth control is also proposed for aerobic rice, where \textcite{Arif2021} introduce a conceptual sheet-pipe automatic water-depth control system that still needs field testing, alongside an opportunity to examine the ET$_c$--production relationship for aerobic rice. Earlier work anticipated these directions, describing farm decision aids and management modeling systems---slowly being developed---that would incorporate water quantity and quality, crop growth, management alternatives, and economic evaluation \parencite{THOMAS1995}.

\subsection{Integrating irrigation with drainage and subirrigation}
Coordinating irrigation adjustments with drainage control is a persistent open problem. \textcite{Wahba2005} stress that when CD is implemented, appropriate reductions in irrigation application must follow, requiring coordination and training among irrigation authorities, drainage authorities, and farmers. A recurring recommendation is to improve integration between irrigation and drainage networks and to use CD to help farmers optimize on-farm water management \parencite{Ritzema2011,Ritzema2016}. \textcite{Dukhovny2018} argue that controlled drainage (CTD) requires a genuinely integrated irrigation-and-drainage water-management approach, with careful site selection (favoring homogeneous soil texture with little sand or sand--gravel near the control structure) and careful monitoring of all water-balance components.

Subirrigation embedded in regional water systems is a distinct and largely unresolved frontier. \textcite{deWit2022} argue that regional-scale water needs for subirrigation should be known before responsible incorporation into regional water management, framing correct implementation of CD with subirrigation as an integral, catchment-wide question; they call for spatial and temporal insight into water availability at each location, optimized pumping strategies to minimize water use, and cautious use of alternative resources such as industrial or treated wastewater only where quality is sufficient and side effects are limited. The companion modeling study \parencite{deWit2024} shows how the SWAP model could identify suitable field sites and forecast the hydrological consequences of subirrigation before implementation, explore effects across crops and crop characteristics, and examine drainage design, water-supply design, soil type, resistance to downward seepage, and surface-water management under varying meteorology; it also suggests optimizing water supply by starting later, supplying water only when crop transpiration is potentially reduced, and coordinating surface-water levels to avoid drainage losses to ditches. Longer-term studies are needed to disentangle whether soil-property changes under drainage-subirrigation arise from the practice itself or from interactions with irrigation water quality, and whether drip or sprinkler methods interact with soil properties \parencite{Kaur2023}. Related integrative directions include continuing to combine water-table control (via CD and subirrigation) with agrochemical applications to minimize nutrient and pesticide losses \parencite{BENGTSON1995}, and pairing shallow drainage with wider perennial-crop strips along drainageways \parencite{Schilling2007}.

\subsection{Design criteria for changing crops, climate, and irrigation modernization}
A broad theme is that design criteria must be revisited for evolving conditions. \textcite{Rui2024} argue that future drainage design should account for changing crop water needs, evolving weather, and recent engineering advances, suggesting more efficient---ideally controllable---systems to improve long-term productivity of poorly drained soils. Design criteria should be revisited to meet environmental concerns \parencite{Akram2013}, and drainage design and water management should be optimized in the face of climate change through adaptive strategies such as adjusting planting schedules, improving water productivity, and enhancing drainage to mitigate waterlogging \parencite{Ziaeifar2025}. \textcite{Ritzema2023} recommend explicitly incorporating the impact of irrigation modernization into drainage design to account for changing soil-water and salinity regimes. Ageing systems, new crop varieties, crop diversification, water scarcity, deteriorating water quality, and wet/dry climate extremes are all cited as drivers of continued drainage research \parencite{Ritzema2007}, and changes in drainage-water quality are expected to require changes in drain-system design and installation \parencite{Ritzema2006}. Further research is needed to serve emerging and least-developed countries and to cope with climate, land-use change, and drainage-water quantity/quality requirements \parencite{Ritzema2011}.

Design intensity itself is an open research question. \textcite{Dialameh2025} note that where adjusting drain spacing is infeasible, other water-management practices may be needed to mitigate increased drought severity, and that future design decisions may need to account for management and economic factors beyond those modeled. \textcite{Singh2009} caution that reduced drain spacing would require increased capacity across the entire drainage network---a factor for future network design---and would heighten the emphasis on best management practices to reduce nutrient loss. \textcite{Castellano2019} advocate a systems approach integrating drainage, crop--soil processes, and nutrient-loss-reduction practices to design drained cropping systems that mitigate and adapt to climate change, and call for identifying long-term cost savings (e.g., greater fertilizer nitrogen-use efficiency) that could offset the higher installation costs of altered drain depth and spacing. \textcite{Strock2011} recommend detailed analysis with DRAINMOD or another drainage model to develop optimum drainage rates and drain spacing across U.S. locations and soils. Fundamental design questions persist about which soil parameters are most critical and what confidence intervals are required for each \parencite{Ayars2015}, and about proper design guidelines for specific geologic conditions such as the Mid-Atlantic states \parencite{SHIRMOHAMMADI1995}. The optimum design is repeatedly framed as a cost--benefit balance \parencite{HARDJOAMIDJOJO1982}, and irrigation is emphasized as necessary to reduce deficit soil-water conditions where drainage alone cannot raise yields \parencite{HARDJOAMIDJOJO1982}.

\subsection{Drainage-water reuse, salinity, and water-quality frontiers}
Managing salt and reusing drainage water remain heavily research-dependent. \textcite{Jia2007} frame understanding salt-and-water-balance dynamics as the first step toward better management of irrigated agriculture, providing evidence to target and fairly evaluate field water-management practices and improve regional water-use efficiency. \textcite{Ayars2015} enumerate needs to develop management approaches for subsurface drainage in arid areas, to reduce crop salt-tolerance and groundwater-management concepts to a manageable producer system, to quantify drainage-water-treatment efficiency (loading, flow rates, lifespan, maintenance, design), to refine salt-management strategies for integrated systems, and to develop and evaluate alternatives for managing, remediating, and removing nitrogen from drainage water.

Reuse-focused research spans agronomy, scheduling, and safety. \textcite{WESTCOT1988} list needs for site-specific load--flow relationships for trace elements, expanded knowledge of germination and early-seedling salinity tolerance, ratings of crops' use of saline groundwater, improved irrigation scheduling that lets farmers meet crop ET from a portion of the high water table while minimizing upward salt flow, wider commercial applicability of direct drainage-water reuse, better understanding of bioaccumulation and trace-element immobilization in evaporation basins, and caution in reusing salts until safety is better known. Field guidance likewise remains provisional: substituting drainage water up to 9~dS/m for wheat at post-germination stages where non-saline canal water is scarce, then reverting to non-saline canal water for one or two years after 6--7 years of drainage-water use, with careful case-by-case evaluation \parencite{Sharma1998}. Keeping drainage effluent as low as possible by improving irrigation efficiency and blending fresh and drainage water, plus disposal options such as farm ponds for storing and evaporating drainage water, are proposed \parencite{Datta2002}, as is minimizing effluent by improving irrigation efficiency and farm-level reuse while maintaining soil health \parencite{Qureshi2008}. Autumn or winter irrigation combined with subsurface pipe drainage is suggested for leaching excess soil salt \parencite{Dou2022}, seasonal groundwater undulation is deemed necessary to prevent salt accumulation \parencite{Mirlas2021}, and proper irrigation schemes---shifting from traditional to drip irrigation---are recommended to sustain growth while flushing salts \parencite{Mirlas2021}. Root-zone salinity and the water table should be kept low, at least in early growth stages \parencite{Singh2019}. Non-conventional treatment via engineered in-stream and off-stream wetlands is being explored at pilot scales \parencite{AbdelDayem2007}, and drainage water outflow is argued to require treatment before discharge to surface or groundwater \parencite{Filipovic2015}. Emerging soil-remediation directions include exploring acidic biochar in saline-alkaline soils and its synergy with subsurface drainage, plus plant-remediation desalination and other collaborative measures \parencite{Liu2025f}. Reclamation innovations include harvesting subsurface-drainage water in dryland/semiarid sites for livestock, windbreaks, gardens, or salt leaching, and establishing alfalfa in saline-seep areas fitted with engineered drainage wells \parencite{Steppuhn2017}. Progressive-flooding and progressive-leaching methodologies still require experimental validation and extension to finer-textured soils \parencite{Youngs2000}. Salinity control is argued to belong within a basin-wide salt-balance context \parencite{Qureshi2008}, and comprehensive assessment of waterlogging and salinity together (rather than separately) is recommended to yield more reliable findings \parencite{Valipour2014}.

\subsection{Nutrient, pesticide, and water-quality research needs}
Water-quality outcomes of controlled and water-table-management systems remain incompletely quantified. \textcite{FAUSEY1995} identify a need to evaluate the full potential of CD and water-table management for water quality, spanning short- and long-term effects on the fate and transport of agricultural chemicals, soil properties, productivity and trafficability, surface/subsurface water and sediment movement, root-zone biological dynamics, and plant development across crops; they also flag CD for animal-waste disposal on cropland, winter denitrification of residual soil nitrogen via CD, interfacing drainage with constructed or enhanced wetlands, and---as a high priority---a comprehensive water-table-management model incorporating solute transport, with results folded into practical management guides. \textcite{RITTER1995} add needs to quantify preferential flow in drainage-water pollution, develop field- and watershed-scale pollution-control modeling, determine year-round nitrate and pesticide leaching, gain more field experience with subirrigation and CD, document tillage effects on water quality, interface agrichemicals with livestock manures where tile drainage is used, and investigate cover crops for water-quality management. Continued pesticide degradation/movement studies and improved simulation of chemical losses under high water tables are being pursued \parencite{THOMAS1995}, and monitoring of drainage impacts on nutrient and pesticide transport---treating drainage as part of whole-farm or watershed management---is recommended \parencite{SHIRMOHAMMADI1995}. At watershed scale, controlling or slowing surface runoff and tile flow, with drainage-water-management devices and denitrification bioreactors at tile outlets, is proposed to cut nitrate loads \parencite{Li2015}, and strategies addressing both hydrology and nitrogen supply, focused at the drainage-district scale, are argued necessary for meaningful downstream benefits \parencite{Ikenberry2014}. Optimizing irrigation is crucial to minimize nitrogen leaching (e.g., from turfgrass) \parencite{Filipovic2015}, and coupling CD with conservation practices that reduce surface runoff is recommended \parencite{BinShokrana2023}. Reduced tillage should be combined with efficient subsurface drainage to minimize surface runoff \parencite{Koskiaho2002}, and CD is offered as one way to meet both crop-production and water-quality goals \parencite{Kladivko2005}. Nutrient replenishment under the drainage--irrigation--cropping system---including zinc fertilization---is needed for optimum yields \parencite{Gul2013}, and understanding nutrient-loss mechanisms during waterlogging and the benefits of nutrient application is an open question \parencite{Manik2019}.

\subsection{Modeling, monitoring, and long-term evaluation}
Authors repeatedly call for stronger modeling, longer records, and better monitoring. Long-term studies are broadly needed to validate active-management approaches \parencite{Ayars2006b}, and the long-term effects of CD---especially in arid and semiarid regions---remain understudied \parencite{Wang2020c}. \textcite{Wang2020c} further urge defining soils by internationally accepted standards (or fuller descriptions), investigating CD from a climate standpoint, combining future designs with proposed underlying mechanisms for more reliable evaluation, and classifying situations to understand CD's contribution to total nitrogen in drainage water. Watershed-scale water-table-management research is needed to develop best management practices and select the best water-management scenario, and more flexible CD schemes may alter impacts on tile drainage, surface runoff, and shallow-water-table days \parencite{Golmohammadi2016}. \textcite{Kim2023} present the SWAT-tile model as opening opportunities for water-quality research and tile-depth installation, and as a viable strategy for managing flow and nitrate transport at field and watershed scales. Steady/transient simulations of steady recharge are framed as an initial step toward broader analyses that could propose conceptual modifications to traditional design for water-quality control \parencite{GRISMER1993}. Regional modeling calls include extending simulation periods to capture dynamic equilibrium and long-term sustainability, completing calibration of groundwater flow-and-transport models, and refining field data on irrigation efficiencies, canal seepage, drainage, and return flows \parencite{Triana2010}. Environmental monitoring and decision-support systems should be designed at project onset to work hand-in-hand with drainage operations, built bottom-up and documented progressively \parencite{Quinn2009}. Additional model-supported directions include using a different field-scale hydrologic model to evaluate CD's yield effect \parencite{BinShokrana2023} and exploring interactive tools such as DRAINFRAME \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}. Remote sensing to detect drainage and salinity needs---via drones and satellite imagery \parencite{Yannopoulos2020}, and refined, upscaled remote-sensing assessment of waterlogged/saline areas \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}---is another data frontier. Reviewing prior research and projects is urged to avoid trial-and-error and reduce failure risk in future rehabilitation/construction \parencite{Valipour2014}.

\subsection{Crop, soil, and agronomic research directions}
Refining the biophysical targets of control is an active frontier. The required water-table depth must be refined by crop, soil type, and season, and it must be verified that shallow drains can control root-zone salinity over the long term before farmer implementation \parencite{Christen2001b}. Root-development responses to tile drainage---and consequent effects on plant water uptake during drought---need further study \parencite{Nash2015b}. Further work is needed on drainage and desalination in unsaturated soil and on the design/engineering of tile drainage under drip irrigation \parencite{Chen2021}. Winter crops that leave soils bare during hot summers warrant more investigation, and longer research is needed on CD in sugarcane \parencite{Akram2013}. On soils with high perched water tables, progress depends on understanding factors limiting crop growth and developing improved strategies, potentially including mole drainage or increasing drainable porosity by loosening soil above the drain \parencite{Muirhead1996}; strategies to reduce flow to shallow subsurface drains and more sustainable practices are also needed \parencite{Muirhead1996}. Soil-management options such as subsoiling, deep-rooted cropping, and drainage trenches are viable short-term solutions \parencite{Guedessou2021}. \textcite{Manik2019} outline a waterlogging-tolerance agenda: increasing soil-profile de-watering through soil-improvement and agronomic strategies, greater specificity of management--environment and management--management interactions, discovering new non-oxygen-associated QTLs, testing their effectiveness alone and combined under multiple constraints, evaluating agronomic/yield/quality effects, and developing management packages for varieties with diverse tolerance genes---plus cost/benefit comparison of different drainage strategies. Salt-tolerant germplasm, groundwater-abstraction regulation, and crop diversification (e.g., salt-tolerant medicinal and aromatic species) are recommended \parencite{Qureshi2008}, as are salt-tolerant crops such as eucalyptus or phreatophytes and drainage installation where leaching with poor-quality water is infeasible \parencite{Sarwar2001}. Supplementary measures---gypsum application, salt-resistant varieties, and irrigation-efficiency improvement---are needed to enhance subsurface-drainage benefits \parencite{Ritzema2008b}. Pressurized irrigation and reduced irrigation amounts are recommended to lessen waterlogging and drainage needs \parencite{Khorramian2022}, more research is needed on leaching requirements for modern irrigation in heavy clay soil \parencite{Mahmoud2021}, and alternatives such as surface irrigation and improved fertility management alongside proper drainage should be weighed for claypan soils \parencite{MOSTAGHIMI1989}.

\subsection{Economics, institutions, and adoption}
A large share of the frontier is socio-economic and institutional. Economic analysis is repeatedly flagged as future work \parencite{Kladivko2005,Saadat2017,Manik2019}, and installation cost, producer management burden, and applicable acreage must be assessed before broad implementation \parencite{Helmers2012}. A broader economic analysis is urged to reveal opportunities for improving water management, reducing drainage costs, and enhancing land/water quality, including the opportunity cost of drainage-investment funds, the scope to shrink drainage facilities through better water management, and pricing/allocation policies that motivate improvement \parencite{Wichelns2002}. The overall economic component of water-quality-management systems needs to be determined and incorporated into design \parencite{THOMAS1995}, and identifying long-term cost savings to offset higher installation costs, plus incentives and cost-share programs that couple drainage upgrades with edge-of-field nitrate-loss reduction, is recommended \parencite{Castellano2019}. Cost reduction is itself a research target: local manufacture of PVC pipes and synthetic envelopes and further labor-cost reductions \parencite{Datta2002}; and new low-cost drainage techniques, cheaper installation technologies, new drainage materials (pipes, envelopes), and national standards \parencite{Yannopoulos2020}.

Institutional and participatory needs are pervasive. Recommendations include shifting from standardized to flexible, location-specific solutions reflecting stakeholder preferences \parencite{Ritzema2011,Ritzema2016,Ritzema2023}; balancing top-down against bottom-up through participatory management across planning, design, installation, operation, and maintenance \parencite{Ritzema2016}; improving farmer participation throughout the drainage-infrastructure lifecycle \parencite{Ritzema2023}; and better identifying stakeholders' needs, preferences, and willingness to contribute while linking their tacit knowledge with researchers' explicit knowledge \parencite{Ritzema2011}. Capacity development---integrating research, education, and advisory services---is emphasized \parencite{Ritzema2016}, along with the substantial education and policy change needed to lower adoption barriers \parencite{Castellano2019}, public/institutional education programs on drainage and water-table management \parencite{SHIRMOHAMMADI1995}, and addressing negative perceptions that conflate agricultural drainage with wetland drainage before the application base can expand \parencite{THOMAS1995}. Scientists and policy institutions should help farmers adopt innovations through financial support and knowledge exchange \parencite{Kesicka2023b}. Governance recommendations include developing procedural/organizational frameworks and national or regional support groups, working groups for diverse agro-hydro-climatic regions, and multi-departmental coordination committees in command areas \parencite{Singh2019c}; enhanced inter-agency coordination for water allocation \parencite{Qureshi2008}; a dedicated state-level Drainage Division and national drainage institution, standardization of components, and promotion of private-sector reclamation and local SSD installation \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}; drainage policy papers with time-bound reclamation action plans \parencite{Ritzema2008b,Valipour2014}; and appropriate institutional arrangements for farmers' participation \parencite{Ritzema2008b}. Adaptive regulatory frameworks that promote local irrigation/drainage flexibility and grower innovation, communicate successes, recognize failures early, and disseminate information are recommended \parencite{Quinn2009}. Programs to create awareness and promote subsidies and micro-finance \parencite{Satyanarayana2007}, to convince farmers to clear drainage blockages and maintain systems \parencite{Manjunatha2004}, and to ensure participatory operation and maintenance for sustainability \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007} round out the adoption agenda. Continued technical and financial support is deemed essential to sustain irrigated agriculture \parencite{Qureshi2008}, research in irrigated agriculture is ``not yet over'' \parencite{Ritzema2007}, and a participatory approach is demanded by changing socio-economic conditions \parencite{Ritzema2007}. Practical framing choices---outsourcing/privatizing drainage activities \parencite{AbdelDayem2007}, budgeting for certified local consultants to support decentralized design \parencite{Ritzema2023}, and switching from gravity to pumped drainage to enable reuse and shrink open-drain dimensions \parencite{Ritzema2023}---are also proposed. Large-scale drainage investment tied to adjusted irrigation planning is viewed as unavoidable in the long run \parencite{Sarwar2001}, and accumulated reclamation experience is expected to inform future developments worldwide \parencite{TeKrony2004}.

\subsection{Implementation, maintenance, and scaling}
Finally, several frontier items concern getting systems built, maintained, and scaled. CD could be propagated on a large scale in irrigation command areas \parencite{Karegoudar2019}, and adopting CD, drain-water reuse, and standardized components is called for \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}. For large-scale implementation, trencher and trenchless drainage machines may be considered \parencite{Ritzema2008b}. Later-stage management and maintenance are highlighted as important, with sludge removal from drainage pipes identified as the chief issue in later utilization \parencite{Yan2013b}; upgrading and carefully maintaining existing tile drainage---and exercising more care in construction and maintenance---is recommended for better efficiency \parencite{AbdElMageed2009}. Efficiency-oriented site actions include better canal maintenance, gate operations, and application techniques, converting gravity delivery to closed (piped) delivery, widely implementing drip and sprinkler networks, preventing erosion/sedimentation in storage structures, ensuring good subsurface-drainage management against salinity and waterlogging, and avoiding deep-groundwater use to prevent saltwater intrusion \parencite{Kanber2019}. Modeling procedures can pre-screen appropriate field sites and forecast hydrological consequences before implementing subirrigation \parencite{deWit2024}. Sustainable effluent disposal must ultimately be resolved \parencite{Datta2002}, low-cost and easily understood CD options should be pursued within an integrated water-saving approach \parencite{Wahba2005}, and profitable, feasible water-management alternatives for problem soils should be selected on a site-specific basis \parencite{MOSTAGHIMI1989,Sharma1998}.

### Material: Watershed Modeling for Drainage Management

\subsection{Overview}

Watershed modeling occupies a pivotal but unfinished position in the science of drainage water management: it is the principal tool for translating field-scale process understanding into landscape-scale predictions of nutrient and sediment fate, yet the models themselves, the data that constrain them, and the scales at which they are applied remain the subject of active methodological debate. The recurring call across this literature is to \emph{refine, build, calibrate, and validate} watershed hydrological models used to assess how drainage water management affects the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles \parencite{Sunohara2015}, and to pair such modeling with large-scale efforts that prioritize where drainage water management will yield the greatest benefit \parencite{Ross2016}. Several intertwined research lines emerge from the findings. First, models require improved representation of processes that are currently poorly resolved---phosphorus sorption--desorption, preferential flow, in-stream processing, channel-network routing, and reactive transport---together with explicit treatment of uncertainty \parencite{Wang2020b,Kujawa2020}. Second, a persistent scale problem separates well-characterized fields from the watersheds where water-quality goals are set, motivating explicit field-to-watershed linkage and upscaling frameworks \parencite{Williams2015,Kyllmar2023}. Third, the empirical base for calibration and validation remains thin: long-term, year-round, coordinated multi-site monitoring is repeatedly identified as the binding constraint \parencite{Christianson2015,Bauwe2020}. Fourth, the field is called on to expand beyond its intensively tile-drained Midwestern core to less intensely drained, newly drained, and geographically underrepresented watersheds \parencite{Kujawa2020,Christianson2015}. Finally, modeling is increasingly asked to serve decision support---placement of stacked conservation practices, adaptive management, and prioritization of critical source areas---rather than description alone \parencite{Kröger2013,Tomer2015}. The subsections below organize these open questions by the modeling task they most directly challenge.

\subsection{Refining process representation in watershed models}

A large share of the frontier concerns processes that current watershed models represent crudely or omit. For phosphorus, \textcite{Wang2020b} set out an explicit development agenda: incorporate dynamic P sorption/desorption processes and new subroutines for direct P loss from manure, fertilizer, and dung; integrate pedotransfer functions for the P sorption coefficient and distinct P pools keyed to soil characteristics and weathering; include in-stream phosphorus processes and critical-source-area identification based on variable source areas; and, crucially, model preferential flow and connectivity between fields and adjacent water bodies. Preferential flow recurs as a first-order gap. \textcite{Djodjic2002} argue that preferential-flow losses of P in structured soils must be incorporated into phosphorus indices and decision-support systems, that identification of preferential pathways at the field scale would sharpen leaching-risk assessment, and that employing more than one hydrological model within a decision-support system may better describe water and pollutant transport. Consideration of preferential flow in the determination of critical source areas, especially at field or sub-field scale, is likewise flagged as necessary \parencite{Wang2020b}. The adequacy of our understanding of subsurface hydrologic pathways---and whether they can be accurately modeled at the watershed scale---remains an open question in its own right \parencite{Sims1998}.

Channel networks and in-stream processing are a second underdeveloped domain. \textcite{Giraud1997} emphasize that as watershed size increases, channel networks must be modeled as a whole set of interdependent reaches---whether looped or dendritic---and that modeling flow paths and travel time of water within the network is essential to predicting non-point-source pollution reaching coastal waters. At the watershed scale, more detailed methods of channel-network description and extrapolation may reduce uncertainty, and evaluation of historical land management and channel stability from aerial photography could further inform the process \parencite{Williamson2025}. Better representation of instream biogeochemical processes is proposed as a route to enhance the reductions generated by best management practices \parencite{Schilling2012b}, and the interaction between hillslopes and riparian areas needs stronger representation to predict low-flow events \parencite{Basu2010}.

Sediment dynamics require analogous refinement. Estimating gully and river-bed erosion will demand different approaches than upland modeling, and the contribution of gully areas to the overall sediment budget deserves closer scrutiny, while the use of WEPP for upland sediment flux needs verification against well-recorded observations \parencite{Maalim2013}. A more robust treatment of sediment from non-field sources should explicitly represent the mechanisms and physical processes behind streambank failure and downstream mobilization \parencite{Dalzell2018}. At the catchment scale, sediment budgets could underpin the design of control and management strategies for lowland agricultural catchments with extensive underdraining, and their precision should be carried forward explicitly into future budget refinement \parencite{Walling2002}.

\subsection{Uncertainty, model evaluation, and structural choices}

Beyond adding processes, the findings press for rigorous quantification of uncertainty and disciplined model evaluation. Assessments of model uncertainty should be built into P-transport modeling \parencite{Wang2020b}, and structural uncertainty deserves explicit treatment---for example by including water-quality models beyond SWAT when projecting hydrologic and nutrient change \parencite{Kujawa2020}. Standardized evaluation metrics recur as a methodological aspiration, with the coefficient of efficiency of Nash and Sutcliffe proposed for future watershed-model evaluations \parencite{STONE1995,SAVABI1995}. For the specific problem of drainage water management effectiveness, \textcite{Shedekar2021} argue that a probabilistic approach accounting for stochastic variation in effectiveness across time and scales may be more appropriate than a single static value or range, and that large datasets combining short- and long-term monitoring and modeling at regional scales may soon be needed to support large-scale assessments. Model transferability is itself a research question: whether a representative 10--20~km\textsuperscript{2} scale identified in one watershed applies to others in the region needs to be examined, and the validity of a given modeling approach must be established by comparison against hydrograph data from additional watersheds \parencite{Basu2010}. Newly proposed physically based tools warrant similar scrutiny---for instance, applying the Advanced Terrestrial Simulator (ATS) to larger watersheds with different soils and topography to probe scaling and physiographic controls, and extending it to explore land-use change, surface-drainage modification, future climate, and drainage intensification \parencite{Rathore2025}. Where field data are unavailable, calibrated and validated physically based models such as HYDRUS-2D are recommended for reliable flux estimation, provided calibration and validation datasets are made more comprehensive \parencite{Naderi2025}. Individualized, station-by-station analysis is also advised for regional trend work, since a single approach may not yield accurate results across heterogeneous stations \parencite{Kumar2009}.

\subsection{The field-to-watershed scaling problem}

A defining open problem is the disconnect between field-scale process knowledge and watershed-scale response. \textcite{Williams2015} identify quantifying the relationships between field-scale processes and watershed response as a critical need for developing water-quality strategies, and cast the field--watershed linkage as pivotal for tile-drained regions of the Midwest and beyond. Integrating field and watershed models for BMP calibration---and scaling field results up to larger areas---is an explicit modeling priority \parencite{Wang2020b}. Small, intensively monitored catchments are proposed as the empirical bridge: they can reveal system functioning, serve as a basis for upscaling to agricultural land where information is less detailed, and provide the data for calibrating and validating modeled outputs \parencite{Kyllmar2023}. Scaling questions also arise for individual practices. Effectiveness estimates for drainage water management should be scaled up from field to watershed \parencite{Wang2021}, and phosphorus mitigation likewise needs scale-up from field to watershed in future work \parencite{Wang2021}. Selenium-removal and other treatment findings are called to be extended to scaled-up operational flow-through systems \parencite{Gao2003}, and complex questions remain about the effectiveness of widespread practice implementation at watershed scales, compounded by lag times in water-quality response and low adoption rates in key watershed locations \parencite{Roley2016}. Continued large-scale watershed modeling is repeatedly recommended to prioritize the areas that will benefit most from drainage water management \parencite{Ross2016}.

\subsection{Data, monitoring, and long-term records to constrain models}

Nearly every modeling ambition is bottlenecked by data. \textcite{Christianson2015} call for more intensive year-round monitoring in some locations, improved monitoring of newly drained areas, ditch-drained areas, and surface intakes, more long-term studies with coordinated controls across multiple sites and years, and continued construction of region-specific drainage water-quality data for underrepresented regions---adding that understanding drainage-design impacts grows more important as aging infrastructure is replaced and upgraded. Long-term records are singled out as necessary to disentangle the possibly counteracting effects of land management and climate change on biogeochemical and hydrological processes, with the corollary that evaluating policy measures will demand patience \parencite{Bauwe2020}. Long-term, catchment-scale study is deemed essential to understand management and climate-change impacts \parencite{Kelley2017}, and the establishment and use of long-term studies tracking the effects of management changes on runoff phosphorus losses is a standing recommendation \parencite{Gillingham2000}. Follow-up investigations of implemented strategies are described as critical for adaptive management \parencite{Schilling2012b}, and long-term watershed data are advocated as the foundation for models that target high-pollution-potential areas and project watershed responses to BMP implementation \parencite{Lemke2011}.

The tracer and water-age literature adds specific data demands for constraining transport models. Further quantification of water ages in drained agricultural landscapes with high-frequency (daily to sub-daily) tracer measurements is needed, along with additional water and tracer datasets across fields with differing management, soils, and climates, and more complex models capable of representing both celerities and velocities \parencite{Williams2021}. Event sampling is flagged as an important next step to constrain the younger water fraction, and investigating the isotope dynamics of ``bulk'' soil water would more completely characterize water partitioning \parencite{DimitrovaPetrova2020}. Further quantification of water ages across drained landscapes would clarify the trade-offs between maintaining drains for food production and the exacerbation of flooding and contaminant transport \parencite{DimitrovaPetrova2020}. High-resolution specific-conductance data are expected to enable more end-member-mixing hydrograph separations as monitoring networks grow, with the caveat that method choice should reflect the flow and specific-conductance characteristics of the stream \parencite{Kronholm2015}. Improved national drainage-extent datasets open new analyses of drainage impacts and nutrient-control strategies, though further study of how farmers report ambiguous cases such as nonpatterned drainage would aid interpretation \parencite{Jame2022}. Coordinated, multidisciplinary, and international collaboration---including consistent reporting of basic performance parameters such as influent/effluent concentrations, hydraulic residence and loading rates, and watershed-to-treatment ratios---is urged to build the shared evidence base for constructed-wetland and treatment performance \parencite{Messer2021,Sims1998}.

\subsection{Extending to underrepresented and less intensely drained landscapes}

A clear frontier is geographic and physiographic breadth. Applications developed in intensely drained watersheds should be extended to watersheds that are \emph{not} intensely drained \parencite{Kujawa2020}. Regional trend and discharge analyses call for larger basin areas, longer study periods, and replication across multiple watersheds while controlling for weather variability, including the influence of small sub-field wetlands \parencite{Yang2017}. Drainage-flow analyses should be extended to larger drainage networks---for example the many US Midwest networks exceeding 29~ha---and linked to measured nitrogen loading across drainage scales and across additional states \parencite{Christianson2025}. Broader applicability is sought by explicitly addressing regional variability, external factors, and climate change \parencite{Naderi2025}, and additional studies across a broad range of climates and topographic features are recommended to strengthen emerging hydrologic paradigms for headwater streams \parencite{King2009}. The importance of physical-hydrologic dynamics as drivers of nutrient loss in dryland regions worldwide remains to be assessed \parencite{Kelley2017}. Regional hydro-climatic feedbacks---including the effects of biofuel expansion and of wetland drainage on regional hydrology and climate---are proposed as further avenues \parencite{Kumar2009}. Effects of drainage-related structures on wetland hydrology across a range of site and meteorological conditions require dedicated field data \parencite{Tangen2018}, and inland-basin remediation treatments are recommended for replication across affected areas \parencite{Kumar2006}. Theoretical drainage results are also candidates for generalization---for example to the case of deep impervious barriers and to water tables drained by channels or rivers \parencite{Zimmer2023}.

\subsection{Modeling to guide practice placement, stacking, and decision support}

Increasingly, watershed modeling is asked to inform \emph{where} and \emph{how} to deploy practices. New conservation-planning tools are needed to determine the best placement of practices in the landscape and to evaluate alternative conservation scenarios, ideally via a hydro-biogeochemically based tool that compares practice effectiveness by landscape position and identifies downstream sediment-deposition zones \parencite{Kröger2013}. Coupling practice implementation with site- and watershed-scale monitoring and modeling is presented as a way to advance water-quality improvement and practice-effectiveness assessment simultaneously, within an adaptive-management framework that maintains effectiveness over time \parencite{Kröger2013}. Simulation models are proposed to test and refine watershed scenarios, alongside continued development of placement criteria for new and emerging practices and better understanding of controls on nutrient-removal efficiency \parencite{Tomer2015}. Identifying and targeting the critical areas within watersheds that generate the largest share of nutrient and sediment export is a shared priority \parencite{Lemke2011}, echoed by calls to identify fields, farms, or watersheds exporting environmentally significant phosphorus via subsurface runoff and to improve soil-testing and characterization methods for prioritization \parencite{Sims1998}. Coarse-scale loss estimates are valued for flagging where deeper modeling and mitigation of nitrogen, phosphorus, and sediment losses should be pursued at catchment and regional scales \parencite{McDowell2021}. Decision-support systems are expected to advance through GIS-based non-point-source models that better capture spatial and temporal variability, incorporation of remote sensing, and software interfaces linking their components \parencite{Djodjic2002}. Combining ("stacking") drainage water management with other in-field and edge-of-field practices to meet phosphorus-reduction goals is an explicit design frontier \parencite{King2022}, and the nitrogen-removal capacity of stacked conservation practices is highlighted as a hypothesis to test \parencite{Roley2016}. Conservation planning in tile-drained watersheds is expected to require combining surface-water BMPs with practices that intercept and retain subsurface runoff, supported by watershed plans and by policies and incentive programs that specifically target tile-drainage nutrient sources \parencite{Lemke2011}. Wider involvement of land managers in regulatory assessments---supported by online teaching tools and analytical services---is likewise encouraged \parencite{Stutter2018}, and cost-share programs promoting drainage water management for watershed nitrogen and phosphorus targets are advocated \parencite{Sunohara2015}.

\subsection{Coupling nutrient, carbon, and greenhouse-gas processes}

The modeling frontier extends to biogeochemical coupling that current watershed tools rarely close. Reactive and sediment-transport models coupled to physically based hydrology are proposed to reveal drivers of adverse watershed impacts and to generate new insight into watershed-scale nutrient transport \parencite{Rathore2025}. For phosphorus specifically, investigating the multitude of P species and flow pathways for all relevant practices, and continuing to study surface-runoff dynamics---particularly under controlled drainage---are identified priorities \parencite{Mitchell2023}. Future work on the interaction between legacy soil phosphorus and hydrology in drained landscapes and closed depressions is needed to quantify P-loss risk \parencite{Mumbi2024}, and phosphorus-management strategies should jointly incorporate precipitation-pattern dynamics and soil-nutrient management, prioritizing application rate, timing, and placement plus legacy-P mitigation, especially where extreme weather is intensifying \parencite{Mumbi2025b}. Mechanistic controls on elevated, solubility-driven phosphorus loads to Lake Erie and other downstream waters require further study of spatial and temporal interactions between site characteristics and P solubility \parencite{Hanrahan2020}. Greenhouse-gas coupling is a notable gap: quantifying fluxes---including carbon sequestration, pre-implementation emissions, and dissolved nitrous oxide in tile drainage and conservation-practice outflows---is called for \parencite{Mitchell2023}. Coordinated, large-scale studies are needed to constrain the climate-mitigation potential of conservation drainage practices, few of which have simultaneously measured N\textsubscript{2}O, CH\textsubscript{4}, and change in soil organic carbon at the same sites, or included dissolved-gas measurements \parencite{Hall2025}. Practice-specific unknowns include the fate of water held back by control structures, interactions among bioreactor design, catchment hydrology, and organic media, controls on soil-organic-carbon accrual and N\textsubscript{2}O yield in saturated buffers, and N\textsubscript{2}O implications of drainage water recycling \parencite{Hall2025}. In the Midwest, longer-term dynamics of water-quality-wetland carbon storage, nitrous oxide, and methane over a wetland's expected lifetime---and the effects of upgrading drainage infrastructure and drainage coefficients on greenhouse gases and downstream hydrology and sediment---remain to be resolved \parencite{Mitchell2022}. Carbon budgets should also be integrated into wetland design models when very high nitrate loadings require carbon supplementation, within a more balanced and integrated approach to whole-system wetland evaluation over time \parencite{Kadlec2012}. Dissolved organic matter dynamics likewise demand attention to the range of relevant scales, changes in DOM quality and microbial availability across watershed scales, paired-watershed designs, and sampling that accounts for the episodic influence of subsurface drainage and extreme precipitation \parencite{Dalzell2011,Bellmore2015}.

\subsection{Practice-scale modeling frontiers: wetlands, controlled drainage, and drainage water recycling}

Detailed process modeling of individual conservation practices is a recurring need. Dynamic modeling at time-steps on the order of hours is required to approximate nitrate-N removal in response to the complex flow patterns of tile-drain-fed wetlands, alongside measures to reduce or supplement phosphorus removal and means to buffer and more gradually release tile-drain flows \parencite{Tanner2011}. Constructed-wetland siting and sizing depend on the wetland-to-catchment area ratio and seasonal discharge regime, and long-term studies over a broad range of hydraulic and nutrient loads are needed to establish sustainable nitrogen-retention efficiency \parencite{Steidl2019}. Wetland size ratios need refinement for each local context, together with deeper study of N\textsubscript{2}O emission processes, the N\textsubscript{2}/N\textsubscript{2}O ratio, and pesticide-metabolite accumulation \parencite{Tournebize2017}. Before large created-wetland projects proceed, critical questions of location, design, and function must be explored, supported by adaptive-management research and studies of optimal wetland siting for nitrogen removal across the Mississippi River Basin \parencite{Kovacic2006}. Passing tile-drained water through wetlands is proposed as a leading strategy for lowering nitrogen loads to Midwest rivers and ultimately the Gulf of Mexico, motivating analyses that identify nitrogen-management and drainage-remediation strategies and even reconsideration of river management \parencite{Baeumler2020}. Upstream improvement of water quality before infiltration to aquifers---potentially requiring substantial increases in forested area to lower recharge nitrate---is advanced as a watershed-scale design principle \parencite{Billy2013}. Constructed-wetland studies for irrigation reuse of treated drainage water are ongoing \parencite{Madramootoo2007}, and whole-wetland behavior must be assembled and interpreted over periods long enough to identify temporal trends \parencite{Kadlec2012}.

For controlled and managed drainage, future research should track complete water and nutrient budgets within and across field boundaries \parencite{Shedekar2021} and investigate effects on water and nutrients lost by other pathways---surface runoff, preferential flow, groundwater recharge, and biological uptake \parencite{Ross2016}. Additional research on drainage water management should span a range of soil textures, cropping systems, and climates and evaluate effects on surface and subsurface phosphorus dynamics \parencite{King2022}, with greater attention to phosphorus given the paucity of research and to combinations with practices such as sub-irrigation \parencite{Ross2016}. Whether drainage water management within channelized streams can restore more natural hydrology---and how such hydrologic change affects headwater biogeochemistry---requires comprehensive, integrated hydrology--water-quality--ecology assessment \parencite{King2009}. The potential of controlled tile drainage to reduce downstream flood risk by significantly reducing watershed-scale stream discharge is highlighted for further development \parencite{Sunohara2015}, and control structures on subsurface tile drainage, together with wetland restoration, are noted as promising means of storing more water on the landscape in spring \parencite{Dalzell2018}. Long-term monitoring with moisture sensors and automated systems is sought to improve drainage water recycling efficiency in tile-drained landscapes \parencite{Kaur2021}, and models such as DRAINMOD-DWR are needed to aid the design and management of recycling systems, supported by longer-term performance monitoring across weather, soil, and design conditions and by economic optimization of implementation and management costs \parencite{Moursi2024}. Full-scale field studies of drainage water recycling and saturated buffers under varied conditions, and study of emerging water-quality-wetland types such as reconstructed oxbows, round out the practice-scale agenda \parencite{Mitchell2023}. Restoration of multipurpose oxbows should continue with careful monitoring to determine whether observed relationships with tiles are representative, including further study of temperature differences among oxbow types \parencite{Leberg2024}, and installed weirs and additional floodplain work are being used to resolve surface- and groundwater-flow directions at restored oxbow sites \parencite{Schilling2017}. Edge-of-field filters and phosphorus-sorbing materials warrant testing at higher runoff loadings and in both surface and subsurface pathways \parencite{Plach2023}, filter and grass strips need evaluation of dormant-season loading and vegetation harvest \parencite{Holly2016,Vallée2015}, and the long-term effectiveness and wider deployment of small constructed wetlands and grass strips deserve continued evaluation \parencite{Vallée2015}.

\subsection{Integrated, adaptive, and stakeholder-connected modeling frameworks}

A final cross-cutting frontier is methodological integration. \textcite{Dollinger2015} articulate perhaps the most explicit long-range vision: developing numerically explicit modeling approaches at the landscape scale that integrate and couple the major processes governing the provision of landscape services by ditches, optimizing the spatial arrangement of management practices according to catchment heterogeneity and ditch connectivity, and defining efficient multi-service maximization strategies informed by an a priori, stakeholder-valued ranking of services---while acquiring new knowledge on feedbacks between processes that have most often been studied in isolation. Better mapping of key ditch characteristics (hydraulic, vegetation, and sorption properties) and study of poorly understood processes such as pollutant sorption and degradation are prerequisites \parencite{Dollinger2015,Herzon2008}. Landscape-scale, integrated approaches tailored to local conditions---combining on-farm changes with off-site wetland and riparian restoration and attentive site- and landscape-level design and monitoring---are broadly endorsed \parencite{Blann2009}. Managing catchment nitrogen export is argued to require acknowledging groundwater as well as farm-scale losses and intercepting edge-of-field pathways such as tile and open drains, using stream-rehabilitation and drainage water management tools especially during wet periods \parencite{Goeller2019}. Truly integrated watershed management is said to require better information on the controlling processes---such as spatial groundwater-head distribution---that link topography--geology interactions to the drain fraction \parencite{Mahmood2023}. Coupled field--watershed models enable scenario analyses of water-table management not possible when component models are used separately, and, once tested, can evaluate environmentally sound management in cold regions \parencite{Dayyani2010}. Modeling frameworks should remain extensible---for example, modifying loading-estimation approaches to incorporate additional processes and pathways and the distribution of planting dates across a watershed \parencite{Basu2010}, and refining and dynamically estimating proposed indices through field calibration \parencite{Tao2025}. Detailed, site-specific nutrient-management planning is recommended to handle the interactions between site hydrology and management practices \parencite{Kurz2005}. More catchment-scale research on water quality, wetland carbon sequestration, habitat quality, and cultural and educational services---and on the effects of upgrading existing drainage infrastructure rather than only new installations---is needed to fully populate these integrated frameworks \parencite{Mitchell2022}. Ultimately, tighter interaction among stakeholders, experimentalists, and modelers is identified as essential to make watershed modeling a credible basis for prioritizing drainage water management \parencite{Wang2020b,Kröger2013}.

### Material: Tile Drainage Research Gaps and Modeling Needs

\subsection{Overview}

Despite decades of field and modelling work, the literature synthesised here converges on a shared diagnosis: our capacity to predict how subsurface (tile) drainage alters water, sediment, and nutrient export remains limited by sparse field verification, incomplete knowledge of where and how drains are installed, and process representations that are too coarse for the scales at which decisions are made. The recurring research frontier therefore spans four interlocking gaps. First, watershed-scale hydrologic effects of tiling must be assessed with both models and field observations, across events of all sizes and multiple landscapes, rather than inferred from single sites \parencite{Schilling2008,Sloan2017,Sloan2016}. Second, the spatial location, extent, and specifications of drainage networks are largely unknown, motivating calls for mapping, databases, and validated prediction of tile lines \parencite{Naz2009,Zhou2025,Valayamkunnath2020}. Third, existing agricultural models (APEX, DRAINMOD, SWAT, MIKE~SHE, and others) require improved tile, water-table, macropore, and phosphorus routines, together with careful treatment of parameter uncertainty when drainage specifications are unknown at calibration \parencite{Williams2016,Bauwe2016,Guo2018b,Nelson2018}. Fourth, key transport processes---preferential and macropore flow, phosphorus source partitioning, sediment storage and exhaustion, and cold-season dynamics---remain underquantified and must be constrained with longer-term, higher-resolution monitoring \parencite{King2015,Grangeon2021,Trentman2020}. The subsections below organise the identified gaps, hypotheses, and methodological needs around these themes.

\subsection{Watershed-Scale Hydrology and Cross-Scale Integration}

A dominant line of argument is that the hydrologic effects of tile drainage remain inadequately characterised at the watershed scale and must be pursued through combined modelling and field verification. \textcite{Schilling2008} argue that more in-depth research is needed to assess tile drainage effects on watershed hydrology using both models and field data, with specific attention to streamflow recessions and how those recessions differ seasonally between tiled and non-tiled watersheds. Extending this, \textcite{Schilling2012} call for work that quantifies the specific hydrologic effects beneath patterned tiling systems at the watershed scale. \textcite{Sloan2017} frame the outstanding questions in terms of scope, recommending that the influence of tile drains be explored for events of all sizes and at all scales, alongside more comprehensive analyses across different landscapes.

Integrating field-scale responses into basin behaviour is itself an open problem. \textcite{Sloan2016} conclude that hydrologic models must include routing between spatially variable depressional stores, and that a detailed modelling study is needed to understand flow accumulation and attenuation when responses from multiple tiled fields are integrated across a river network and their combined role in large-scale watershed flooding. Related scaling questions concern how the collective drainage network shapes flood behaviour: \textcite{Fonley2021} recommend a comprehensive study of the statistical improvement in flood prediction offered by their model, further study of how the flow duration curve evolves as the tile population evolves, and note that such work could ultimately inform recommendations for where artificial drainage is applied on the landscape. At the regional scale, \textcite{BolandBrien2014} identify a need to decouple the effects of row-crop cover from artificial drainage and to separate the contributions of surface versus subsurface drainage in altering hydrologic response; they hypothesise that bypass-flow networks contribute to hydrologic homogenisation and argue that simple models for tiled landscapes should be developed, in part because long-term data are sparse.

Several studies emphasise interactions between drains and the receiving channel network as a frontier. \textcite{Grangeon2021} suggest future investigation of the interactions between rivers/ditches and tile-drain flows, while \textcite{Clement2017} call for research on the fate and transport of tile-drain effluent after it reaches the surface drainage system. \textcite{Zhou2025} argue for field-scale monitoring of the direct impact of tile flow on downstream water discharge, stream power, and channel geomorphology across multiple locations within watersheds to provide higher-resolution ground-truth measurements. Efforts to extend field-scale drainage models to catchment and watershed scales are ongoing, as with the application of MIKE~SHE to Midwestern tile-drained lands \parencite{Zhou2013}.

\subsection{Locating, Mapping, and Cataloguing Drainage Networks}

A cross-cutting obstacle is that the physical drainage network is often unknown, which propagates uncertainty into every downstream model. At the field scale, \textcite{Amado2018} note that having actual tile locations, and measured tile-flow discharge at the site, would improve model accuracy and calibration, and that additional field data and delineation of the subsurface drainage network are needed to refine the model (alongside a grid-sensitivity study). Predictive mapping of tile lines from imagery is promising but immature: \textcite{Naz2009} call for further validation of predicted tile maps against observed locations from GPS survey, and for methods that minimise the misidentification of image noise as tile lines and that connect broken segments erroneously split during detection. \textcite{Schilling2019} likewise identify improving the ability to locate field tile systems in agricultural watersheds as an ongoing research focus.

At larger scales, \textcite{Valayamkunnath2020} anticipate updating their tile-drainage map repository as new data become available. \textcite{Zhou2025} set out the most explicit data agenda: future studies should report information on subsurface tiles beyond areal extent---particularly tile diameter, depth, and spacing, as well as the mains that collect water from pattern tiles---and a tile-drainage database should be constructed through joint efforts of government agencies, researchers, private landowners, and drainage contractors, with pipe networks mapped using remote sensing and ground-based surveys where possible.

\subsection{Model Structure, Routines, and Parameterisation}

\subsubsection{Improved and New Tile, Water-Table, and Crop Routines}

Many authors call for better process routines within existing agricultural models. \textcite{Williams2016} argue that incorporating identified flow pathways and processes into models such as APEX, DRAINMOD, and SWAT is critical for improving estimates of phosphorus transport in tile-drained landscapes. Within SWAT and its variants, \textcite{Schilling2019} recommend testing new tile-drainage algorithms that give a more physically based representation of tile function and can directly account for pattern-tile configurations; \textcite{Guo2018b} see an opportunity to improve representation of tile systems---especially individual tiles---and functionality at watershed scales, including improving water-table-depth calculation during long dry periods and testing improved routines and curve-number methods on additional individual tiles and watersheds. \textcite{Moriasi2012} call for follow-up studies comparing dynamic and constant surface-depression storage ($S_d$) approaches for tile-flow and streamflow simulation, and for additional testing of the Modified SWAT's tile-flow and water-quality simulation against measured tile flow and tile-flow water-quality data. In APEX, \textcite{Nelson2018} note that improvements in tile-drainage, water-table, and crop-growth routines may be needed, and that new routines for water-table fluctuation and subsurface tile drainage were in development but not yet implemented. For rangeland modelling, \textcite{Chen2016} conclude that a tile-drainage module is needed in the GPFARM-Range model to predict drainage from subsurface-drained pastureland on shorter time scales and in a timely manner. \textcite{Boles2015} report that their new routine's parameter values provide a basis for fine-tuning tile parameterisation, suggest that modellers vary LATKSATF on a hydrologic-response-unit rather than watershed basis, and identify additional studies to build understanding of the routine's effects.

\subsubsection{Parameter Uncertainty When Drainage Specifications Are Unknown}

A distinct methodological caution concerns calibration under unknown drainage specifications. \textcite{Bauwe2016} stress that much care should be taken when calibrating the depth-to-impervious-layer parameter (DEP\_IMP) because it is critical in dividing subsurface flow into tile flow and groundwater flow, that the choice of infiltration model matters when flow components are relevant, and that this is especially important when drainage specifications are unknown and tile-drain parameters are used for calibration. Consistent with this, \textcite{Guo2018b} propose separating DEP\_IMP into two parameters---depth to impervious layer and a soil-permeability factor---to improve representation of the tile system.

\subsubsection{Process Formulations and Higher-Dimensional Models}

Some gaps concern the underlying physics of flow and solute movement. \textcite{Qi2018} suggest that the method of simulating subsurface water flow in the soil matrix should be revisited or replaced by a soil-matrix-potential-based Richards equation, and that the algorithm for particulate-phosphorus loss to tile drainage should be revisited. \textcite{Mohanty1998} argue that tile flow and conservative solute transport would perhaps be better interpreted and modelled with a three-dimensional model considering regional water and chemical management, and that more detailed consideration of regional hydrology and adjacent-bench irrigation/fertiliser scheduling is needed to improve prediction. \textcite{DeSchepper2015} report developing a larger-scale model to simulate seasonal variation in tile-drainage outflow with heterogeneities of clayey tills represented in greater detail, and argue that more realistic simulated water-flow patterns in heterogeneous drained fields are a cornerstone of future nutrient-transport modelling; they anticipate future work focusing on nutrient-transport modelling. \textcite{Garrison1999} propose that integrating spatial images of surface wetness (before emergence) and within-season vegetative index, coupled with final yield, will refine tile-model parameters, and that their tile-water and nitrogen-flow methods may found improved spatial representation of soil-water and nitrogen dynamics.

\subsubsection{Field Verification of Model Predictions}

Calls for direct measurements to validate models recur throughout. \textcite{Muma2017} recommend further validating the CATHY model by installing an automatic tile-drain system for flow measurement, using field measurements of evapotranspiration to validate simulated actual evapotranspiration, and installing a piezometer at the centre of the tile-drain plot to evaluate model performance against water-table elevation. \textcite{Nazari2021} suggest their approach can help inform numerical-model evaluations and assess environmental gradients across sites, and that accounting for redox and other conditions affecting sorption--desorption dynamics between events could reduce uncertainty and improve model fit (Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency).

\subsection{Preferential and Macropore Flow Processes}

Preferential and macropore flow are repeatedly named as critical, underquantified controls. \textcite{Boles2015} identify macropore-related processes as a critical area of future research. \textcite{Williams2016} call for investigation of macropore-flow contributions to tile drains during different periods of the year to clarify their role in annual water budgets, and for work on the interaction between macropores and the surrounding matrix. \textcite{King2015} frame the central questions as quantifying the development, extent, and behaviour of preferential-flow paths under different soils, management practices, and antecedent conditions, and determining what fraction of phosphorus is delivered to tile drains via preferential flow versus matrix flow. \textcite{Nazari2021} recommend that their methodology be evaluated at sites where matrix--macropore exchange forms a larger share of the tile water budget, and that it be considered in water-quality research on practices---such as drainage water management---that directly affect water-table dynamics. Methodologically, \textcite{Nielsen2015} note their staining technique should be most useful in studies of preferential flow and transport in drained soils (for example, leaching of strongly sorbing contaminants), and that longer infiltration times could increase the number of stained pathways reaching the tile lines. \textcite{Ruark2009} argue that further explanation of preferential-flow interpretation requires detailed analyses of hydrograph and chemograph behaviour, and \textcite{BACKLUND1995} call for research on contamination transported by old and new tile systems through preferential-flow paths and on means of modelling it.

\subsection{Phosphorus and Nutrient Transport}

Improving the representation and understanding of nutrient transport---especially phosphorus---is a persistent priority. \textcite{King2015} call for developing and enhancing phosphorus routines in deterministic models to better represent subsurface drainage and P transport, and for measuring phosphorus losses in surface runoff and subsurface drainage simultaneously to determine partitioning between flow paths and avoid unintended consequences. \textcite{Williams2016} stress the importance of differentiating sources of tile-drained phosphorus (e.g., soil versus fertiliser) to understand how P is transported through macropore paths. \textcite{Qi2018} encourage revisiting the particulate-P loss algorithm and suggest measuring the size distribution of soil particles that act as phosphorus carriers, comparing them with soil-matrix pore sizes to enhance understanding of particulate-P transport. \textcite{Geohring2001} note that additional research would help determine which processes most significantly reduce total-phosphorus loadings to tile drains. \textcite{Zimmer2016} recommend more intensive monitoring of discharge and P forms from tile-drained areas, and considering retention of phosphorus at tile-drain outlets to prevent excessive input to water bodies.

Broader nutrient questions extend to nitrogen and receiving-water impacts. \textcite{Clement2017} call for research on P retention and uptake lengths in reaches with and without best management practices, on sampling tile drains from different areas of the watershed, on the potential effects of nitrogen given its prevalence in inorganic and organic fertilisers, and on the role of internal P loading from lake sediments in driving algal blooms. \textcite{King2018} argue that additional work is needed to understand phosphorus losses in subsurface tile-drained landscapes and to mitigate harmful and nuisance algal blooms in receiving waters, examining both individual and stacked nutrient-management practices and the processes controlling P transport across fields with varying characteristics. \textcite{Ruark2009} note that both the nature and drivers of dissolved-organic-carbon dynamics in relation to effluent applications require further investigation, and that the ecological implications of DOC from tile drainage remain largely unknown.

\subsection{Sediment Dynamics and Storage}

Sediment transfer through drains is highlighted as an emerging area requiring longer records and targeted tracers. \textcite{Grangeon2021} recommend monitoring tile-drained catchments over longer periods to gain insight into sediment storage and exhaustion, further quantifying the factors controlling sediment storage from the cultivated topsoil to the drains during ephemeral flow, using short-lived fallout radionuclides or alternative low-cost measurements to quantify sediment storage and transfer, and further quantifying the impacts of these transfers on sediment and contaminant exports from drained catchments. \textcite{Gaillot2023b} argue that the influence of cropping practices on soil-surface characteristics and tile-drain connectivity---and on the temporal dynamics of suspended sediment---should be explored further; they recommend combining high-temporal-resolution acquisition of hydrosedimentary fluxes with the dynamic evolution of soil structure at the surface, in the topsoil, and in the subsoil, and suggest that a suspended-sediment tracer allowing event-scale discrimination could clarify sediment dynamics. At the reach scale, \textcite{Ahiablame2010} call for investigations accounting for manure application, soil types, and nutrient loadings from individual tile outlets to examine the impact of tile inputs on sediments across different reach scales and ditch conditions (vegetated, non-vegetated, dredged, and non-dredged).

\subsection{Management Practices, Trade-offs, and Mitigation}

A further frontier concerns rigorous, holistic evaluation of management and treatment practices. \textcite{King2015} call for holistic evaluations of management and treatment practices---including cumulative effects and trade-offs between surface runoff and subsurface drainage---and for exploring practices that target phosphorus in drainage water, such as tillage directly over tile lines and withholding phosphorus application over tile lines. \textcite{King2016} recommend investigating cover crops, drainage water management, and 4R nutrient management as strategies to reduce nitrogen and phosphorus transport, particularly practices targeting the non-growing season to decrease annual loads. \textcite{Williams2016} call for examining different tillage operations (moldboard plow, chisel plow) and application methods (banding or injection of fertiliser). \textcite{Kokulan2021} recommend assessing tile drainage's role in hydrochemical export together with practices such as fertilisation, tillage, and variable tile configuration to develop a complete regional picture. \textcite{Qi2018} suggest testing further off-season management and modelling practices under manure-phosphorus application and different soil and climate conditions. In the context of agricultural-waste land application, \textcite{BACKLUND1995} call for research on nutrient leaching, interception, denitrification, and fecal-coliform transport; on the interaction between cedar wood chips and iron-ocher buildup (including chemical substitutes or filter-fabric applications); on reuse of drainage water and its impact on crop production and downstream water quality; and, where contamination exceeds acceptable levels, on alternatives such as tile-outflow pumpback systems, reuse, or other outlet options. \textcite{Fortin2002} note that evaluating the effects of different soil treatments on chemical movement to tile drains under natural rainfall probably requires long-term experiments to capture climatic variability.

\subsection{Seasonality, Cold Climates, and Long-Term Monitoring}

Several gaps concern temporal coverage. \textcite{King2015} identify the need to expand research on hydrology and phosphorus transport in cold climates, especially the winter and snowmelt periods that are underrepresented in the literature. \textcite{Trentman2020} recommend prioritising seasonal sampling across a range of antecedent-precipitation conditions in light of a changing climate, note that the effect of snow on tile drainage and nutrient export is a topic of interest, and observe that soluble-reactive-phosphorus reduction estimates could be better constrained with continuous (e.g., daily) tile-drain measurements. \textcite{Millar2025} call for multiyear studies of tile-drain nutrient loads and for coverage of important periods outside the growing season, including winter, postharvest, and early spring. Longer and more intensive observation is also needed to diagnose drain performance itself: \textcite{Rimidis2003} argue that a more elaborate measuring set-up and long-term observations are required to ascertain the cause of drain malfunctioning, recommending additional piezometers close to the drain to measure approach-flow head loss and to distinguish head due to entrance resistance from backpressure within the drain.

\subsection{Data, Tracers, and Methodological Needs}

Finally, the reviewed studies point to methodological and data infrastructure requirements that cut across the themes above. Beyond drainage-network mapping and databases \parencite{Zhou2025,Valayamkunnath2020,Naz2009}, authors call for tracer- and chemistry-based approaches to partition flow components: \textcite{Frederiksen2023} suggest process-based modelling or water-chemistry studies using water isotopes or conservative tracers to estimate fast-flowing components in quick flow and the tile-flow component in baseflow, and recommend adding predictor variables such as landscape characteristics to better explain spatial variation in tile-flow generation and improve modelling accuracy and precision. \textcite{Schilling2019} propose adding less-sensitive parameters such as specific conductance to end-member mixing analysis to better account for seasonal deployment and operational variations. Collectively, these needs---higher-resolution and longer-term monitoring, event-scale tracers, three-dimensional and physically based process models, and open shared datasets on drainage-network specifications---define a research agenda in which improved field verification and improved model structure advance together rather than separately \parencite{Schilling2008,Bauwe2016,Zhou2025}.

### Material: Subsurface Drainage for Soil Salt Leaching

\subsection{Overview}

Subsurface (tile) drainage is a cornerstone technology for reclaiming and maintaining salt-affected agricultural land, yet the literature converges on a striking recognition that the salt-leaching outcomes it produces are only partially understood and, in many settings, only partially detectable with current observational programs. The recurring message across studies is temporal: meaningful salt reduction often accrues slowly, so that short experimental windows may underestimate long-term benefits, and only extended drainage periods combined with sustained monitoring can resolve the true trajectory of desalinization. \textcite{Almen2021} state plainly that additional years of extensive drainage may be needed before noticeable field-scale salt reduction appears, while \textcite{Daigh2025} argue that longer-term perspectives should be incorporated when assessing soil-management efficacy, since immediate gains may understate protective benefits against salinization. This frontier is therefore defined less by settled magnitudes than by open questions concerning (i) the temporal and mechanistic understanding of salt removal, (ii) the instrumentation and monitoring infrastructure required to close water and salt balances, (iii) sensing, mapping, and modelling tools for quantifying and forecasting salinity, (iv) design and management optimization across heterogeneous soils and climates, (v) the fate, treatment, and safe disposal of saline and contaminant-laden drainage water, and (vi) the scaling of understanding from plots to landscapes and watersheds. The evidence base spans arid and coastal reclamation zones in China, the Indian subcontinent, the Mediterranean and Middle East, Australia, northern Europe, and the drainage-intensive and evaporation-pond regions of North America, and the open questions differ in emphasis but share a common demand for longer records, better process representation, and more transferable relationships.

\subsection{Long-term monitoring and the temporal dynamics of desalinization}

A dominant research gap is the need to characterize salt removal over multi-year horizons rather than single seasons. Beyond the call by \textcite{Almen2021} for additional years of drainage before field-scale salt reduction becomes evident, several authors emphasize that the sustainability of combined saline-water irrigation and subsurface drainage systems can only be judged over the long term: \textcite{Feng2017} argue that the long-term effects of saline-water irrigation and subsurface drainage on soil salinity and crop yield require further evaluation, and \textcite{Feng2021} similarly urge that this combination receive more attention for long-term soil-salinity and grain-yield management. Desalination rates and their trends themselves remain incompletely quantified; \textcite{Guo2024b} identify both the rates and their temporal trends as requiring additional study, and further note that the suitability of such systems in arid areas with deeper groundwater levels needs exploration. \textcite{Yao2024} call for investigation of the long-term effects of specific drainage parameters on agricultural output and soil health. Controlled drainage introduces its own unresolved temporal trade-offs for salinity: \textcite{Jouni2018} state repeatedly that more research is needed on the long-term effects of controlled drainage on soil salinity and on optimizing management options to avoid salt accumulation. Soil-degradation processes under drainage likewise demand extended experiments, as \textcite{Lu2019} note, coupled with site-specific cost--benefit assessments under particular hydrological and salinity conditions. In coastal, heavy-textured saline soils, \textcite{Islam2025} identify the absence of long-term studies on nutrient loss and water pollution associated with surface and subsurface drainage as a specific deficiency. \textcite{Daigh2025} frame this collective need as a matter of landscape resilience, advocating continued case studies of how soil-management practices influence resilience to salinization and cautioning against underestimating protective benefits from short records. At the salt-balance level, \textcite{Heng2021} recommend regular sampling surveys to track intensifying salt accumulation and the formulation of salt-leaching plans keyed to salt-balance trends and salt-leaching curves.

\subsection{Instrumentation, surface-water monitoring, and closing salt balances}

Several studies argue that the field cannot advance without better measurement of what leaves the system through the drains. \textcite{Mastrocicco2013} contend that much greater attention must be paid to surface-water monitoring to assess the potential export of salts and fertilizers through subsurface drainage. \textcite{Askri2022} make the instrumentation requirement concrete for oasis-scale water and salt balances, calling for the installation of flow meters and electrical-conductivity probes to measure drainage discharge and salt concentration continuously and automatically; they further note that quantifying these balance components for recent years would reveal the long-term performance of the drainage system, that the influence of the system on subsurface water and salt dynamics is still poorly understood, and that the impact of saline drainage water on downstream phreatic groundwater quality must be evaluated to safeguard sustainability. The environmental accounting of drainage improvement is also unresolved in nutrient terms: \textcite{Turtola1995} argue that the environmental benefits or disadvantages of subsurface drainage improvement should be evaluated through the nutrient balances of recipient waters. Monitoring infrastructure for tracking salinity trends over time is another recurring recommendation; \textcite{Singh2020d} propose installing representative cluster wells within experimental fields to track salinity at two depths, and \textcite{Gafni2001} plan deep drilling and soil sampling to monitor salinity dynamics over time.

\subsection{Process understanding of salt leaching}

\subsubsection{Preferential flow, macropores, and heterogeneous media}

The physical mechanisms controlling how salts are mobilized and removed remain an active frontier. \textcite{Xin2016} highlight two open questions: how macropores affect salt leaching under transient-state conditions, and how leaching methods might be modified to avoid or minimize macropore effects. For layered or otherwise structured profiles, \textcite{Zhang2022} call for future studies to incorporate the governing factors of salt leaching in low-permeability or structured soil media. \textcite{Li2023} argue that both field experiments and numerical simulations are needed to investigate the soil-salt replacement effect between subsurface pipes under brackish-water leaching and to reconcile it with indoor simulation results.

\subsubsection{Rainfall, irrigation, and desalinization ratios}

The interaction of natural and applied water with drainage is central to leaching efficiency. \textcite{Yu2015} suggest that greater rainfall frequency, irrigation, and subsurface drainage sustained over several years may improve the homogenizing effect on soil salinity, and that increasing the intensity of rainfall and subsurface drainage could raise the desalinization ratio. \textcite{Chen2025} propose analyzing variable scenarios to derive more generally applicable relationships between the soil-desalinization process and rainfall/irrigation requirements under different drainage conditions, and extending such work to different climatic conditions and soil types. \textcite{Wang2024d} emphasize that evaporative water loss during summer waterlogging leaching must be fully accounted for when adjusting leaching-water amounts to achieve target results.

\subsubsection{Ponding strategy and leaching curves}

Where reclamation proceeds by ponding, \textcite{RAO1991} propose specific untested strategies: complete ponding followed by ponding over decreasing surface fractions (80\%, 60\%, 40\%, 20\%) to avoid unleached zones, and, conversely, reversing the sequence by starting at 20\% and increasing to complete ponding to counter re-salinization and economize water; they also suggest crop-residue mulches or cultivation techniques to control capillary rise in partially ponded situations, framing this as a rationale for future leaching experiments in drained saline soils of Haryana, India. Complementing this, \textcite{Yang2022c} stress the importance of determining soil-salinity leaching curves and generating fitting equations across different agricultural regions.

\subsubsection{Rootzone salinity, matric potential, and crop response}

Linking leaching to plant performance remains only loosely resolved. \textcite{Zhang2021} call for research to optimize irrigation depths for a given irrigation-water salinity, to quantify the effects of soil matric potential and water salinity on soil salinity and plant growth, and to determine the optimal rootzone matric-potential threshold; the authors also seek comparison of gravel-sand-layer drainage against conventional tile systems. \textcite{Heng2021} identify the link between cotton yield and soil nutrients under saline-alkali improvement, and the use of moderate deficit irrigation with subsurface pipe drainage (MDI-SPD) to reduce salinity with minimal water while raising yield, as open questions.

\subsection{Sensing, mapping, and modelling salinity}

A large cluster of frontier work concerns tools to observe and predict salinity at scale. Electromagnetic induction (EMI) methods are seen as promising but incompletely developed: \textcite{Narjary2024} propose using EMI for characterizing and mapping soil salinity, extending it to three-dimensional representation via inversion modelling (EM4soil), while cautioning that calibrated relationships must be validated and modified for other soil orders and diverse agro-climatic and hydrogeological settings. \textcite{Yu2025} argue that coupled apparent-electrical-conductivity--water-content (ECa--$\theta$) models could better estimate soil-solution salinity and refine the quantification of leaching rates and their mechanisms. \textcite{Singh2020d} envisage continuation of EM38 mapping programs through university collaborations and funding, and the use of multispectral, drone, and hyperspectral remote sensing to improve salinity assessment. Modelling frameworks require targeted extension: \textcite{Singh2020d} call for a new CSUID-1D model interface incorporating tile drains, drain depth, and drainage yield, while \textcite{Jayawardane2001} report modelling of water and salt flow through FILTER plots to explain progressive changes in soil and drainage-water salinity, and note that modelling sodicity and salinity changes can predict longer-term soil stability and the need for remedial measures such as periodic gypsum application. The most integrative vision is offered by \textcite{Zhu2025b}, who advocate developing zonal drainage-design algorithms based on the spatial variability of soil texture, quantifying the contribution of crop root-zone hydraulic enhancements to salinity redistribution, and constructing a multi-scale water-and-salt transport model coupled with remote-sensing inversion data for real-time salinity early warning and dynamic control; they add that soil-texture mapping and groundwater-salinity thresholds will remain necessary to guide precise drainage zoning while optimizing crop--drainage synergies through root-dynamic modelling.

\subsection{Design optimization and management under heterogeneity}

Translating process understanding into practice requires site- and texture-specific design that is not yet standardized. \textcite{vandenEertwegh2006} argue that changes in subsurface-drainage system redesign, surface-water-level management, and field-scale nutrient management must be integrated rather than treated in isolation. \textcite{Neha2022} call for more precise understanding of water and salt dynamics and the economic viability of cut-soiler preferential subsurface drainage (PSSD) across different textural classes of salt-affected soils, variable irrigation-water salinity, and different crop-residue types. \textcite{Wang2024d} suggest that appropriately increasing vertical porous flow pipes filled with straw or sand can enhance the salt-drainage effect of subsurface pipes, and \textcite{Yao2024} note that the effects of fully charged pipe segments on salt leaching require further investigation. \textcite{Jayawardane2001} caution that the optimum combination of practices will depend on specific site conditions, echoing the broader recognition that transferable design rules remain elusive.

\subsection{Fate, treatment, and disposal of saline and contaminant-laden drainage water}

Removing salts from the soil relocates the problem to the drainage water, and its management is among the most persistent open challenges. \textcite{Ayars2014} state simply that research is needed to find environmentally acceptable disposal methods, and \textcite{Steppuhn2006} note that additional disposal techniques may be necessary when drainage discharge volumes or dissolved-salt concentrations are large. Evaporation-pond management is a specific frontier: \textcite{BRADFORD1991} call for testing of pond-management methods under laboratory and field conditions representative of full-scale evaporation ponds, and suggest combining pond management with measures to reduce drainage volume or contamination---water conservation, salt-tolerant and drought-resistant crops, cycling water through salt-resistant vegetation, and treatment to remove contaminants. Trace-element fate compounds the disposal problem: \textcite{Gao2007} argue that the role of sediment mechanisms in the fate of arsenic in drainage disposal requires further examination of As speciation, transformation, and partitioning in sediments, while \textcite{Duff1997} propose investigating the feasibility of uranium recovery, on-site harvesting of pond algae to lower water and sediment U concentrations, and removal of the surface 10~cm of pond sediment to significantly reduce pond U levels. Emerging treatment routes are only at the demonstration stage: \textcite{Yang2023} outline a research program to verify the electrolysis characteristics of saline wastewater from subsurface pipes at different salinity levels and compositions, to perform wastewater-electrolysis experiments, to evaluate solar-energy resources for agricultural wastewater treatment, and to establish demonstration bases for large-scale application. \textcite{Pugliese2023} call for further investigation to enhance the sustainability and cost-effectiveness of sorptive filter materials---which can be recycled as soil amendment or regenerated---and stress that accurate prediction of filter performance requires testing with real drainage water rather than artificial drainage water. Blending strategies remain a longer-term goal, with \textcite{Singh2020d} aiming to customize the blend of subsurface drainage water and reverse-osmosis product water to support sustainable forage production.

\subsection{Scaling from plots to landscapes and watersheds}

A final frontier concerns the extrapolation of field observations to the regional, landscape, and watershed scales at which salinity is ultimately managed. \textcite{Yu2025} argue that future research should prioritize regional-scale planning of agri-aquaculture systems to avoid ineffective management, and that landscape-level water and salinity management will be crucial for informing best practices in coastal reclamation and other salinity-sensitive land uses. \textcite{Chen2025} frame a broader ecological dimension, proposing exploration of the ecological benefits gained from early cropping production on saline soils. At the watershed scale, \textcite{Frisbee2022} call for repeat synoptic sampling campaigns to characterize baseflow during a normal precipitation year and to identify the contribution of deep-aquifer groundwater to baseflow at downstream sites, for additional campaigns combined with groundwater-well sampling to understand seasonal variability in baseflow and salinity sources, for coupling $^{36}$Cl/Cl with other tracers to disentangle the impacts of road salt versus KCl fertilizers, and for further research on the effect of KCl fertilizers on the overall salinity budgets of agricultural watersheds. Together with the zonal, multi-scale modelling agenda of \textcite{Zhu2025b} and the arid-area suitability questions raised by \textcite{Guo2024b}, these studies define a clear trajectory: from characterizing salt removal in individual drained fields toward predicting and managing salinity across heterogeneous soils, climates, and connected hydrological landscapes.
