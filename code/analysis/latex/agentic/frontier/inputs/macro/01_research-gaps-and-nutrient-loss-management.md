### Material: Future Research and Management Practices Needed

\subsection{Overview}

Across the literature on agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage, authors converge on a consistent message: while individual practices and processes are increasingly well characterised, the field still lacks the long-term, multi-scale, and economically grounded evidence needed to design, target, and deploy cost-effective nutrient-mitigation strategies at the landscape scale. This section synthesises the research frontier around several recurring lines of inquiry. First, there is a widespread call for longer-duration, multi-year, and multi-site monitoring to smooth climatic variability and capture the true magnitude of system responses \parencite{Randall2003,Randall2005,Waring2024,Jeong2018,Cooke2012}. Second, nitrogen management remains a primary focus for refinement of application rates, timing, and decision-support tools \parencite{Christianson2015b,Bandura2025,Liu2021}. Third, phosphorus transport in subsurface drainage is repeatedly flagged as under-studied relative to its importance, particularly for dissolved and bioavailable fractions \parencite{Hertzberger2019,Yuan2022,deOliveira2022}. Fourth, a strong theme is that no single best management practice (BMP) is a "silver bullet": bundling in-field, edge-of-field, and water-management practices, and targeting them to hydrologically active critical source areas, is essential \parencite{Sunohara2016,Lenhart2016,Christianson2014,Baulch2019}. Finally, authors emphasise that biophysical understanding must be coupled with economics, incentive design, policy reform, modelling advances, and stakeholder engagement before mitigation can be scaled \parencite{Liu2021,Kvítek2023,David2015}. The subsections below organise these open questions and priorities in turn, retaining the specific quantitative targets, methods, and conditions proposed by the source studies.

\subsection{Long-term, multi-scale monitoring and experimental design}

A dominant frontier priority is the need for longer observation periods to disentangle management effects from climatic noise. \textcite{Randall2003,Randall2005} argue that long-term studies with detailed measurements are necessary to smooth year-to-year climatic variability and thereby yield more meaningful information, and stress that both the corn and soybean phases of a rotation should be evaluated when assessing nitrogen (N) management effects on nitrate losses to subsurface drainage. \textcite{Waring2024} similarly conclude that long-term studies are essential to capture a system's response to conservation practices and the true magnitude of that response, emphasising site-specific impacts. \textcite{Cooke2012} note that studies of longer duration, and many more years of yield data, are required to determine the effect of drainage water management (DWM) on yield while eliminating confounding factors. \textcite{Pittelkow2017} likewise highlight the need to evaluate N losses over multiple years on an annualised basis. Continuous and comprehensive monitoring of N application rate, timing, and method on nitrate loss and crop yield in tile-drained landscapes is advocated to give modelling a more rational setup \parencite{Jeong2018}.

Several studies extend this call to non-growing-season and multi-year hydrologic coverage. \textcite{VanEsbroeck2016} recommend collecting field data during the non-growing season when estimating phosphorus (P) loads and assessing BMP efficacy, and call for additional data across a wider range of soil textures, management practices, and hydrologic years, including wetter summers, before drawing strong conclusions on optimum P-application timing. \textcite{vanderSalm2012} argue that data spanning at least a normal, a wet, and a dry year are necessary to understand nutrient losses and transport routes. \textcite{SalcedaGonzalez2025} recommend more extended calibration and validation periods, longer treatment periods, and further data collection to obtain definitive evidence of total suspended solids reductions and more conclusive orthophosphate results under cover cropping. \textcite{Ulén2007} note that as long a period as possible is needed for trend estimation because water-quality changes may occur more slowly than the P-loss changes driven by agricultural measures. Long-term or continued monitoring is also recommended to evaluate P leaching to groundwater and nutrient removal of green-land infiltration systems \parencite{Duan2015}, to determine whether enhanced effluent-driven P losses persist \parencite{Monaghan2004}, and to confirm bioreactor and saturated-buffer effectiveness across different soils, landscapes, and weather years \parencite{Jaynes2019}.

Experimental-design refinements are proposed alongside longer records. \textcite{Schilling2013} recommend establishing a suitable correlation among paired basins across a range of climate conditions, restricting paired sampling to the March--July period when most water and nitrate flows occur, delaying post-BMP monitoring to enhance the likelihood of detecting change, and using BMPs capable of achieving at least the minimum detectable change. \textcite{Sunohara2016} argue that BMP impacts on downstream receptors should be assessed simultaneously at multiple scales, from field to watershed. \textcite{Irvine2019} call for within-field and within-plot studies to elucidate the mechanistic processes responsible for nutrient export at the sub-watershed scale, and for additional field-scale studies within high-export sub-watersheds. \textcite{Xin2016c} recommend further experiments under a wider range of conditions to test the applicability of derived effective-yield formulas and models, while \textcite{Xiong2025} call for refined $^{15}$N loss observation and larger trial areas more representative of actual production, and \textcite{Loper2024} see mesocosms as an opportunity to quantify nutrient-loss responses to management changes.

\subsection{Nitrogen management: rates, timing, tools, and loss pathways}

Optimising N application rates is expected to continue receiving primary research and regulatory focus, and accurate 4R implementation will require site-specific knowledge to balance agronomic and environmental goals \parencite{Christianson2015b}. \textcite{Bandura2025} propose concrete standards for the next generation of N-rate tools: they should be developed and refined for various application timings using the economically optimum N rate (EONR) as a benchmark, judged adequate if they predict N need within 10~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ of EONR, and considered environmentally unacceptable if they exceed EONR at an N:corn price ratio of 5.6 by more than 30~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$. \textcite{Liu2021} call for more accurate N-fertilisation recommendations that consider both environmental and economic benefits, effective local nutrient-management plans, case-by-case cost analyses, and coordinated agency action (e.g.\ USDA-NRCS with USEPA) to address the economic losses from lower fertiliser rates and to promote 4R adoption. \textcite{Banger2018} advocate additional field experiments across wider crop-management practices and soil types, particularly to identify potential yield tradeoffs from N-rate reductions, and note that if such yield-loss risks appear, greater emphasis should shift to cover crops and edge-of-field practices (bioreactors, wetlands, buffers) to meet load-reduction targets. \textcite{Gupta2023} suggest precision fertiliser application based on soil N availability as a new approach to prevent N losses via subsurface drainage.

Timing and method of application are recurring open questions. \textcite{Liang2018} propose combining timing with rate to further optimise N management and note the value of models such as WHCNS for evaluating short- and long-term drainage, N-loss, and crop-growth effects. \textcite{Hertzberger2019} call for additional coordinated field research on nutrient timing, note the continuing evolution of the MANAGE database as a research resource, and emphasise the need for greater research on P transport in subsurface drainage. \textcite{Malone2007b} highlight the need for more research to confidently quantify differences between split and single spring N applications and to check possible over-prediction of winter nitrification and N loss for fall manure on soybean. \textcite{HernandezRamirez2011} note it remains unknown whether reduced-rate fall manure could match sidedress or preplant applications environmentally, and point to nitrification inhibitors, winter cover crops, more complex rotations, and multifunctional landscapes as additional mitigation avenues, with controlled drainage as a supplementary method. \textcite{Elmi2005} stress the need to quantify all major N loss pathways simultaneously and to obtain better information on the relative merits of manure versus mineral management systems.

Local-scale N testing and decision approaches also require refinement. Late-spring nitrate-test (LSNT) programmes are framed as an initial start to be replaced or refined as new research emerges, with high-clearance application equipment to reduce delayed-sidedress risk, and cropping diversity or edge-of-field denitrification needed to reach drinking-water goals \parencite{Jaynes2004}. \textcite{Malone2010} treat their conclusions as an impetus for further research, suggesting weather-based model refinement into simple tools and improved understanding of year-to-year soil mineral-N variability to reduce soil-testing needs and increase side-dressing adoption. Grain-quality and grain-protein monitoring is recommended where LSNT is implemented, along with risk insurance or other benefits for adopters \parencite{Karlen2005}. Broader gaps include comprehensive volatilisation-loss studies to determine loss extent and controlling factors, targeted research from data-poor regions, and provision of ancillary climate, yield, and soil data to support predictive modelling and policy \parencite{Chatterjee2020b}. \textcite{Haghnazari2020} call for research comparing economic and environmental consequences of N-fertiliser use under global-warming projections and for identifying best field-management practices to reduce environmental impacts.

\subsection{Phosphorus transport processes and mitigation research gaps}

Phosphorus in subsurface drainage is repeatedly identified as under-researched relative to nitrogen. \textcite{Hertzberger2019} explicitly call for greater research effort on P transport in subsurface agricultural drainage and better understanding of nutrient-related water-quality issues. \textcite{Yuan2022} note that more research is needed on conservation-practice effectiveness in reducing dissolved-P loss from agricultural fields, particularly in subsurface flow, and on the causes of annual variability in reduction rates. \textcite{Pease2018} identify increasing understanding of the pedologic factors influencing P transport in subsurface-drained landscapes, and the effect of management on P loss, as key research needs across the Eastern Corn Belt, requiring examination of individual and combined management factors. \textcite{deOliveira2022} recommend refining P-loss frameworks through additional within-field studies, continued data collection on soil testing, site history, agronomic management, and water-quality/flow monitoring, and better understanding of the "where" and "when" of drainage P loss to sharpen conservation-practice timing and placement for disproportionate, targeted benefit.

Several studies target specific dissolved and bioavailable P questions. \textcite{Uusitalo2003} argue that directing runoff into the subsurface pathway and establishing field-margin buffers are only first steps, that mitigating bioavailable particulate P requires reducing particle mobilisation across the entire field surface, and that controlling diffuse dissolved reactive P (DRP) is a longer-term task than reducing erosion. \textcite{Uusitalo2018} state that assessing the pace of P stratification requires greater data, suggest simulation tools such as the Annual P Loss Estimator (APLE) for evaluating mixing intervals, and note it remains an open question whether no-till adoption as a eutrophication-control measure is justified at their site. \textcite{Ni2020} call for quantifying dominant mechanisms of agricultural conservation practices (ACPs) and site-specific conditions under complex scenarios, more data on other ACPs affecting surface and subsurface DRP and total P, and studies of optimal soil-test-P levels under changing climate to address STP--precipitation interactions. \textcite{Wang2018} note further study is needed to confirm whether results apply to manure-injection scenarios and to test whether pre-application or immediate post-application tillage decreases DRP losses through tile drainage. Manure-related P risk is also flagged: the risk of immediate P loss after a single manure application needs fuller understanding \parencite{Madison2014}. Modelling refinements proposed include adjusting manure-P mineralisation parameters and winter drainage, setting manure decomposition rates as adjustable parameters, and further investigating tillage effects using daily or monthly datasets \parencite{Pan2023}, alongside continued efforts to improve P-loss modelling and to represent crop stress prior to maturity \parencite{Wang2019}.

Additional P-loss frontiers concern event dynamics, ratios, and legacy sources. \textcite{Brendel2019} recommend designing BMPs that reduce P losses during frequent small events (which can drive extreme P and suspended-solids losses), understanding relationships between flow, precipitation, transport pathway, and P fraction at the catchment scale, and using these to target BMP implementation. \textcite{Schilling2017b} call for refining organic-P/total-P concentration and load ratios for Iowa and the US Midwest, evaluating their sensitivity to load-estimation method and seasonality, quantifying wastewater-treatment-plant contributions, and identifying specific sources of variability such as manure applications and animal densities. \textcite{Jarvie2017} ask whether changing climate patterns contribute to increased soluble reactive P delivery, note it is unknown how much of a 40\% reduction target could be achieved by 4R practices alone, and warn that drawing down legacy P and disconnecting tile-flow P loads at times of maximum receiving-water sensitivity may be increasingly costly and intrusive. \textcite{Ulén2007} add that knowledge of the availability of eroded P to algae is still limited. Cost-effective P-mitigation hierarchies are proposed, prioritising decreasing soil Olsen P and targeting alum applications to critical source areas that lose the most bioavailable P from a small part of the farm \parencite{McDowell2015}, and P removal via regular grass harvesting under continuous-grass tile-drained fields \parencite{Zhang2015b}.

\subsection{Bundling, sequencing, and edge-of-field practices}

A pervasive conclusion is that no single BMP suffices and that combinations must be studied, sequenced, and optimised. \textcite{Sunohara2016} state plainly that no one BMP is a silver bullet and that multiple BMPs are likely required to complement controlled tile drainage. \textcite{Lenhart2016} argue that multiple BMPs in series are needed to achieve nutrient-reduction goals, that combinations of in-field and edge-of-field practices show great promise but have been little studied, and that wetlands and other water-retention BMPs must be developed to more effectively control eutrophication in sensitive downstream waters, with vegetation establishment encouraged before diverting water into a wetland. \textcite{Christianson2014} conclude that attaining water-quality goals will require a combination of practices implemented at a high level and in a targeted manner. \textcite{Makhtoumi2025} recommend combining conservation tillage with cover crops (and harrowing the top ${\sim}10$~cm to disturb leaching pathways), pairing in-field with edge-of-field conservation to maximise nutrient removal, and incorporating legacy effects to assess the real potential of practices. \textcite{Kreig2019} call for examining synergistic effects among conservation practices, formalising optimisation studies, and addressing farmer concerns about 2-tier versus 3-tier riparian buffers. \textcite{McLellan2015} emphasise better understanding of practice interaction, greater scientific understanding of the nitrogen removal capacity (NRC) of various practices, more data from stream-restoration projects over longer periods, and improved mapping of tile-drained and ditched land to evaluate available land area for N-removal practices.

Region-specific bundling agendas reinforce this. \textcite{Baulch2019} lay out a broad cold-region research programme: investigating drainage effects on nutrient export under diverse weather while comparing surface, tile, and undrained fields; studying controlled drainage, channel design, and maintenance through wet--dry cycles; assessing rotational and spring tillage with altered nutrient management in reduced-till systems; determining net effects of perennial-forage conversion (stand age, composition, snowmelt infiltration); quantifying nutrient retention and remobilisation in reservoirs, wetlands, ponds, and buffers; refining nutrient management by minimising late-fall and winter applications and targeting hydrologically connected hotspots; and developing tile-drainage mitigation (controlled drainage, bioreactors, physico-chemical amendments) suited to snowmelt hydrology. \textcite{Gupta2023} call for more robust, cost-effective BMPs to aid cover-crop benefits and for robust edge-of-field BMPs to mitigate climate warming. \textcite{Husk2024} suggest wider application of agricultural bioreactors, P reactors, controlled drainage, and municipal low-impact development, potentially alongside internal-loading management such as sediment dredging, P inactivation, biomanipulation, aeration, and nutrient precipitation. \textcite{HernandezRamirez2011} and \textcite{GAYNOR1995b} point to intercrops and management that enhances crop nutrient uptake and alters watershed hydrology as promising for P-transport reduction, with tillage options beyond ridge and zero tillage still to be investigated. Edge-of-field structure research is also called for: quantifying gains from individual components of saturated riparian buffers, installing monitoring wells and backflow valves, and clarifying suspended-solids sources \parencite{Singh2024b}, and confirming that saturated-buffer N removal increases as buffer vegetation matures \parencite{Jaynes2019}. \textcite{Spiese2023} call for novel BMPs to address both nitrate and DRP simultaneously.

\subsection{Spatial targeting, hot spots, and critical source areas}

Closely related is the frontier of targeting practices to the parts of the landscape that matter most. \textcite{Melland2008} recommend identifying hydrologically active parts of the landscape and managing them as priorities for mitigating surface-runoff P, avoiding higher stocking rates and fertiliser inputs in waterlogged runoff-generating areas. \textcite{Irvine2019} describe ongoing work to determine whether elevated loads originate from numerous low-intensity hot spots or a few key hot spots, seeking to identify land-management-specific hot spots and targeted BMPs. \textcite{Speir2022} find that targeted cover-crop implementation may be required to reduce soluble reactive P export, that regional saturation of cover crops on all fallow ground will likely be needed for consistent large annual reductions in nitrate-N and SRP, and recommend subwatershed-based optimisation. \textcite{Baulch2019} and \textcite{McDowell2015} likewise stress targeting critical source areas and hydrologically connected hotspots. \textcite{Brendel2019} note their results assist in targeting areas for BMP implementation and remediation. \textcite{Makhtoumi2025} emphasise that further progress in prioritisation promises to improve return on investment in conservation practices. \textcite{Kvítek2023} prioritise automating spatial-targeting principles into an easily applicable GIS tool, projecting results into river-basin management plans, and recommend whole-basin surveys with SWOT and cost-benefit analyses and stakeholder engagement.

\subsection{Cropping systems, cover crops, perennials, and land use change}

Improved cropping practices are widely proposed as under-explored levers. \textcite{Elli2023} recommend exploring genetic-trait impacts on water-table depth, yields, and soil-N dynamics; investigating how trait changes affect water tables and reduce climate-projection uncertainty; and examining additional strategies including irrigation, cultivar maturity groups, further cropping systems, off-season cover crops, perennial systems, green manure, and stacked multi-management strategies extended to further environments. Cover-crop optimisation is a recurring need: \textcite{deOliveira2022} call for optimising cereal-rye management (early planting for fall growth, targeting biomass accumulation) to assess DRP-loss reductions and minimise P-loss tradeoffs, while \textcite{PrezaFontes2023} suggest growing cover crops longer in spring to prevent increases in N$_2$O emissions and adjusting incentives to include enhanced-efficiency N sources. \textcite{McNeill2026} note that information on different winter-cover types is needed to inform BMPs for the increasingly hydrologically active non-growing season, that "win-win" BMPs benefiting both soil and water are needed, and that because over-winter BMP effects differed for N versus P, practices targeting individual nutrient loss pathways must be identified. \textcite{Krueger2013} recommend conservation practices beyond rye cover cropping because subsurface nitrate remained high and rye had no effect on DRP or drainage volume, suggesting French drains to replace surface inlets and applying part of crop nutrients via irrigation water. \textcite{Mitchell2000} note cover cropping requires innovative management before soil-quality benefits are expressed in productivity, along with better understanding of emergence impedance and stubble-reinforced crusts.

Perennial and land-use-change options feature prominently. \textcite{Huang2023} suggest that gaining additional soil organic carbon may require purposeful management, that poorly drained depressions may be good candidates for conversion to perennial bioenergy crops such as \textit{Miscanthus $\times$ giganteus}, or restoration to wetlands by obstructing tile drains and planting native species. \textcite{Norberg2022} conclude that mitigating N losses is most effectively achieved by keeping soil covered year-round with a growing crop such as perennial ley, while the focus for P should be on decreasing surface runoff. \textcite{Arrueta2025} call for additional data on alfalfa's effect on subsurface discharge during large events, across diverse management and geographies, and better understanding of how nutrient and transport factors couple to govern temporal variability in export. \textcite{Djiemon2019} argue for better cropping practices to ensure long-term subsoiling returns, integrating cover crops and autumn cereals to dry soils in spring, and controlling machinery traffic and weight. \textcite{Woodbury2018} call for research on the value and payments for other externalities (P and sediment reduction, greenhouse-gas mitigation, biodiversity), which may further highlight switchgrass benefits when replacing maize. \textcite{Siksnane2017} list a suite of measures warranting evaluation: fertilisation planning and nutrient balancing, converting arable land to grassland, intercultural cultivation, winter crop farming, controlled drainage, organic farming, buffer-zone creation, and artificial wetlands with phytoremediation. \textcite{GARDNER1992} recommend greater emphasis on soil types beyond red-brown earths, increased subsoil root growth, deep ripping and deep placement of nutrients and gypsum, crop species that open subsoils, and lucerne in dryland systems with rotational grazing. \textcite{Oquist2007} note more research across regions is needed to determine whether conversion to alternative farming practices improves water quality elsewhere.

\subsection{Water-table and drainage-water management}

The operation and siting of controlled drainage, DWM, and subirrigation constitute a further frontier. \textcite{Jaynes2012} are unaware of research optimising DWM operation for the soils, climate, and crops of central Iowa, and call for identifying locations where a greater field area falls under control-structure influence and for incentive programmes that share installation and management costs to increase adoption. \textcite{Adeuya2012} note more experience is required to determine optimum dormant-season management depth. \textcite{Cooke2012} require longer studies to determine DWM's effect on yield. \textcite{Gunn2015} recommend better on-site monitoring plans, long initial calibration periods, anti-seep collars on new and retrofitted systems to minimise seepage around control structures, and educational programmes to help farmers understand management-plan recommendations. \textcite{Gunn2019} call for extending subirrigation studies to more diverse regions with in-depth economic assessment, assessing variable subirrigation strategies across locations, and evaluating the importance of decreased yield uncertainty. \textcite{Baker2004} note more research is needed on whether soil-aggregate property differences affect the long-term sustainability of water-table management by subirrigation. Nutrient-trading programmes are identified as needed to increase adoption of shallow drains \parencite{Ghane2021b}, and \textcite{HernandezRamirez2011} note controlled drainage could in principle be implemented as a supplementary method for reducing drainage flow and nutrient losses. In paddy and rice systems, new water-management options may change the whole agronomy and require new infrastructure and better farm management \parencite{DarziNaftchali2014}, and integrated water-and-nutrient management systems with advancing precision agriculture are encouraged to support open-field smart agriculture \parencite{Jung2024}.

\subsection{Models, indices, tools, and data infrastructure}

Numerous studies frame the frontier in terms of improved models, indices, and shared data. \textcite{Buczko2007} outline development of a phosphorus index for Germany, starting regionally (e.g.\ Mecklenburg-Vorpommern), incorporating German conditions onto a Pennsylvania-style backbone, using factors such as wind erosion, subsurface drainage and soil leaching, groundwater transport in peat soils, and stream-network connectivity, and possibly combining the P index with an N index for concentrated animal operations. \textcite{Pärn2012} argue nutrient-index schemes should be improved by including wetland and riparian-buffer removal capacity, incorporating buffer N-removal efficiency or catchment soil wetness into both N and P index models. \textcite{Negm2016} note that regression-based tools for nutrient-credit trading via DWM can be developed for other US and international regions, that Midwest equations are underway, and that user-friendly web interfaces and educational material will be needed. \textcite{Wang2016} discuss using steeper stress s-curves in critical periods and milder transformations elsewhere, noting aeration-stress effects on yield may be more easily identified at plot scale against field-collected yield data. \textcite{Boivin2006} call for focus on hydraulic-conductivity and shrink-swell parameters and their seasonal time-dependence, direct in-field measurement methods, more comprehensive long-term datasets, and delineation of seasonal preferential-flow processes. \textcite{ROBINSON1992} note yield-loss coefficients can be adjusted with regional data and that their methodology is applicable to other regions with similar terrain. \textcite{Adeuya2012} note their statistical analysis could apply to similar time-series data. Data-resource development is emphasised through the evolving MANAGE database \parencite{Hertzberger2019}, provision of raw and ancillary data \parencite{Chatterjee2020b}, and coupling models with tools such as INVEST to assess synergies and tradeoffs among water quality and other ecosystem services \parencite{McLellan2015}.

\subsection{Economics, incentives, policy, adoption, and stakeholder engagement}

A consistent frontier is that biophysical understanding alone is insufficient; economic evaluation, incentive design, policy reform, and farmer engagement are decisive for adoption. \textcite{Liu2021} call for advanced case-by-case cost analyses to guide policymakers, farm owners, and researchers, and for inter-agency action to promote 4R implementation. \textcite{Yuan2022} note more data are needed on the cost-effectiveness and ecological benefits of ACPs. \textcite{McLellan2015} plan to incorporate practice costs and cost-effectiveness into future work. \textcite{Christianson2014} recommend continued positive incentives such as CREP funding for wetlands (given high public and lower perceived private benefit), more information and educational programmes to increase adoption (especially for crop rotation), and greater consideration of broader-scale, non-excludable benefits in multifunctional landscapes. \textcite{David2015} argue that incentivising on-farm research and collaboration among farmers, programmes that generate a collective sense of conservation need, and scaling individual efforts to meso- and macro-scale policy are essential. \textcite{Jaynes2012} and \textcite{Karlen2005} both call for cost-share and risk-insurance mechanisms to raise adoption. \textcite{Kvítek2023} call for reassessment of legislation and revised incentives for the 2024+ period to remove barriers to natural-water-retention-measure adoption. \textcite{Marja2013} recommend national policy change to retain open ditch-and-field networks where subsurface drainage prevails, noting that removing state support for ditch replacement may suffice, with targeted agri-environmental support as an additional incentive in high-revenue regions. \textcite{Ghane2021b} identify nutrient-trading programmes as needed to increase shallow-drain adoption.

Adoption, capacity, and social dimensions are stressed across contexts. \textcite{Okwany2016} call for detailed cost-benefit analyses, technical assessment of the most cost-effective designs, economic and social studies of upscaling potential, joint farmer--researcher experimentation, and greater information dissemination and capacity building, noting adoption depends on farmers' long-term cost-benefit perspective. \textcite{Ghumman2011} emphasise creating know-how and farmer confidence before government project execution, with farmer participation playing a very important role. \textcite{Ward2018} identify key practice-use issues: where different BMPs should be applied, availability of tools to assist producers, how best to bundle BMPs, and transferability of research knowledge to commercial farms, and note farmers need additional education on soil ecology and biology. \textcite{Cuevas2019} stress that proposed measures must consider stakeholders' willingness to adopt. \textcite{Saikkonen2014} argue urgently for research integrating ecological, pollution-related, and economic impacts of alternative land uses to develop environmentally sustainable yet economically profitable agriculture, and for models addressing optimal policy design. In salinity-affected and irrigation contexts, \textcite{Kotb2000} propose a package spanning reduced and consolidated rice plantings, increased irrigation staff, soil-fertility monitoring, farmer compensation, flexible cost-recovery for drainage projects, public-awareness programmes, and gradual subsidy liberalisation. \textcite{Sellars2022} conclude that improved feasibility of drainage-water-recycling requires markedly higher, less volatile irrigated yields and lower adoption costs, that producers may prefer alternative adaptations such as drought-resistant seeds or precision technology, and that internalising large welfare benefits would positively influence adoption.

\subsection{Greenhouse gases, emerging contaminants, and multi-objective tradeoffs}

An expanding frontier integrates greenhouse-gas (GHG) and contaminant considerations into drainage-mitigation assessment. \textcite{PrezaFontes2023} call for evaluating net GHG emissions in longer-term experiments. \textcite{Carstensen2021} argue that the fate of waterborne methane leaving via the riparian zone warrants further research and that GHGs should be included alongside phosphorus balances when comparing and selecting mitigation measures. \textcite{Burton2024} note more study is needed to understand the magnitude and fate of nitrous-oxide losses. \textcite{HernandezRamirez2011} call for comparative life-cycle assessment of agroecosystems based on complete N balances encompassing all ecosystem services and environmental impacts. Emerging contaminants and biological interactions are also flagged: \textcite{Spiese2023} call for better constraining the mechanism and lifetime of glyphosate in soil and water and long-term DRP datasets across land-use types to elucidate glyphosate's effect. \textcite{Schaafsma2019} argue that planter modifications must be investigated to reduce exposure of aquatic non-targets to neonicotinoid and other seed-applied pesticides. \textcite{Knorr2023} call for nutrient-mitigation strategies readily implemented without adding time or cost burdens on producers and effective across seasons, and for expanded research on how watershed microbial communities respond to and control nutrient fluxes. \textcite{Fraindová2022} call for studies of water-quality changes during different rainfall-runoff events in relation to landscape characteristics and anthropogenic activities to identify compound sources and protect water quality.

\subsection{Salinity, reclamation, and irrigation-return management}

In arid and salinity-affected drainage systems, distinct research and management needs emerge. \textcite{Sharma2004} suggest sequential reuse across cereals, grasses, forest plantations, and halophytes for future research, and innovative, cost-effective evaporation-pond designs for effective containment of salt and toxic elements. \textcite{MANN1982} caution that only small amounts of gypsum should be applied during early reclamation to avoid excessive losses, while \textcite{He2015} recommend chemical amendments (gypsum, elemental sulphur, possibly agricultural lime) in long-term management of problem soils and consideration of more environmental factors for field assessment. \textcite{Cuevas2019} foresee increased use of tolerant rootstocks and varieties, screening of halophyte germplasm for recovering salinised lands, and transgenic crops as proposed solutions. \textcite{Christen2010} propose combining land-treatment systems with pond treatment, restricting hydraulic loading to allow soil drying, storing excess wastewater for off-season application, using larger land areas with longer irrigation intervals, providing better pre- or post-treatment to reduce biochemical oxygen demand, growing and harvesting potassium-accumulating herbages, and reducing sodium in the waste stream. \textcite{Duan2015} call for pre-treatment (dilution or nutrient removal) before wastewater application and for controls to minimise N leaching by enhancing plant uptake and preventing preferential flow. \textcite{Mitchell2000} note detailed economic evaluations of integrated reuse systems are needed. \textcite{Purkey2001} raise questions about the ultimate use and suitability of retired land overlying a shallow water table surrounded by irrigated fields. \textcite{Bjorneberg2020} prioritise continued reduction of sediment loss (especially from furrow-irrigated fields), improved sediment removal in water-quality ponds, improved irrigation management to reduce return flow, and irrigation and nutrient management to limit nitrogen leaching. \textcite{Tian2023b} recommend selecting biochar with good amelioration, low contaminant content, and low environmental risk, and urgently assessing its long-term positive and negative impacts on soil quality and crop yield. \textcite{Zhang2015} call for determining optimum rates of organic soil conditioners to minimise adverse water-quality impacts while improving soil quality and productivity.

\subsection{Emerging pressures, downstream endpoints, and remaining infrastructure questions}

Finally, several studies point to under-studied downstream endpoints, climate-driven hydrologic shifts, and physical drainage-technology questions. \textcite{Apsite2017} note that projected shifts of maximum discharge from spring to winter must be considered in future flood planning, that discharge changes may alter seasonal water supply for hydropower, and that further effects will occur. \textcite{Husk2024} note further long-term nutrient drawdown and internal-loading management may be needed to achieve additional trophic improvement in receiving lakes. \textcite{Winikoff2020} call for identifying and testing novel management practices to control hybrid cattail and reed canary grass while protecting native perennials, with targeted control needed to maintain excavation benefits. \textcite{PALKO1994} suggest mass-balance studies for further insight and direct liming of ecologically important watercourses. \textcite{Lucci2014} propose developing Bayesian networks to explore trade-offs of different wintering strategies and note stock access to waterways as a complex P source outside current scope. On drainage-material and infrastructure design, \textcite{Zhang2025b} plan to explore relationships between perforation characteristics, drainage performance, and compressive performance, noting further field validation is needed, while \textcite{ASGHAR1995} call for comparing permeameter results across Pakistan, Belgium, and Canada, standardising the number of drain-plate perforations, and investigating the effect of perforation size. \textcite{Zhang2022b} note that future field experiments should include N loss among essential observations. \textcite{Eastman2010} report ongoing efforts to demonstrate total-P transport mechanisms under different BMP scenarios. \textcite{Grenon2023} call for further long-term comparative field studies with more hydrologic and climatic data to identify the long-term environmental effects of water-management systems on P, alongside simultaneous implementation of improved fertiliser management, precision agriculture, and vegetated buffer strips. \textcite{Hashemi2020} recommend special management strategies in heavy-textured subsurface-drained soils, adoption of water-table and fertilisation management for sustainability, and assessment of new technologies before introduction to paddy fields. Collectively, these frontiers underscore that advancing tile-drainage water-quality management will require sustained, multi-scale, and interdisciplinary research linking process understanding, targeted and bundled practices, robust tools, and economically and socially viable pathways to adoption.

### Material: Future Research Needs for Crop-Drainage Systems

\subsection{Overview}

The literature on agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage converges on a shared meta-conclusion: despite decades of field and modeling work, the interactions among crop yield, nitrogen and phosphorus losses, and the field water balance remain insufficiently characterized across the full range of soils, climates, cropping systems, and drainage management practices that occur in tile-drained agriculture. Authors repeatedly frame future needs around \emph{extending scope} --- to more sites, soils, climates, crops, and longer observation periods --- and around \emph{sharpening the tools} used to synthesize and extrapolate, namely process-based models, monitoring networks, remote sensing, and integrated economic frameworks. \textcite{Ma2025} distil this program broadly, arguing that more research is needed to comprehensively understand the impacts of conservation practices on agricultural productivity, nutrient-loss reduction, ecosystem services, and their tradeoffs in tile-drained agricultural ecosystems. The strongest recurring lines of evidence pointing forward are: (i) the geographic and pedoclimatic narrowness of existing field trials --- concentrated in the U.S. Midwest --- which limits transferability \parencite{Zhao2016,Birru2023}; (ii) persistent structural weaknesses in the crop, cover-crop, drainage, and nutrient modules of biophysical models \parencite{Dietzel2016,Chatterjee2025,Feyereisen2006}; (iii) the need for long-term, multi-season datasets to resolve slow soil-health and climate-interaction signals \parencite{Kaspar2012,Bawa2023,Saha2024}; and (iv) the still-immature integration of biophysical, economic, and policy dimensions needed to guide adoption \parencite{Ferin2023,Malone2023,Qin2025}. This section synthesizes these open questions and research priorities rather than settled results.

\subsection{Broadening the empirical base: sites, soils, climates, and long-term observation}

A dominant theme is that existing evidence is drawn from too few sites and too short a window. \textcite{Birru2023} call for research that extends both the scope and geographic area to test and quantify the impacts of multiple cover-crop species under diverse management and biophysical conditions, and for studies spread over more sites with varied environmental conditions and management practices so that cover-crop benefits can be optimized without compromising crop-production goals. \textcite{Zhao2016} note the unequal geographic distribution of historical drainage studies and call for additional research across other states of the U.S. Midwest. Similar calls to extend testing to additional soils, climates, and management arise from \textcite{Grewal2025} (across different soil types and rainfall levels), \textcite{Negm2014} (different climatic conditions, soil types, crop rotations, farming practices, and water-management scenarios), \textcite{Saha2024} (longer periods and additional soils in the Northern Great Plains to identify systems that benefit both productivity and environmental health under a more extreme climate), \textcite{Takeshima2023} (different rainfall environments to confirm the interaction between subsurface drainage and planting pattern), and \textcite{Liu2025} (extending the experimental period and validating pipe burial depth effects on irrigation water productivity across diverse soil properties and climates, plus exploring multiple rotation systems). \textcite{BENOIT1967} likewise stress that more complete quantification of soil-water/plant-survival relationships is needed for extrapolation to other soils.

The call for \emph{long-term} experimentation is equally strong. \textcite{Kaspar2012} identify a need for additional long-term studies of the effectiveness of winter cover crops over time, and \textcite{Bawa2023} argue that a longer-term study is needed to observe the positive influence of a rye cover crop on soil-health parameters. \textcite{MartinezFeria2016} seek understanding of the long-term consequences of including rye cover crops in Midwestern systems and of the biotic and abiotic mechanisms by which rye affects yield and environmental performance. \textcite{Kumar2014b} note that long-term monitoring of fluxes under different crop rotations is needed because of seasonal climatic fluctuations. Several teams describe active or planned long-term programs: \textcite{Qi2025b} report ongoing rice--wheat and rice--oil rotation experiments to further assess subsurface-drainage performance over multiple seasons and climates; \textcite{Liu2025b} and \textcite{Liu2025c} call for long-term monitoring of subsurface pipe drainage and seepage-drainage effects across diverse environmental conditions and cropping systems; and \textcite{Thoma2005} caution that four years of data are insufficient to resolve whether higher residue cover minimizes pollutant losses and affects grain yield over time. \textcite{Acharya2019b} similarly call for a more detailed and longer-term experiment to understand climate--crop-management interactions.

\subsection{Cover crops: agronomic, environmental, and modeling frontiers}

Cover crops --- especially winter rye --- attract the largest concentration of open questions. On the agronomic side, \textcite{Waring2020} prioritize tools to promote rye growth in colder regions and study of long-term no-till in that climate, while \textcite{Bawa2023} highlight the need for management strategies to establish rye under unusually high or low temperature and precipitation and limited growing degree days. \textcite{Dougherty2020} flag that observed yield drag may reflect poor planter performance through cover-crop residue, warranting investigation. \textcite{Kaspar2007} suggest improving rye management by evaluating more cultivars, adjusting timing relative to planting, and improving establishment methods to reduce year-to-year variability and yield impacts, and note that establishment costs, logistical conflicts, and seed availability must be overcome before widespread adoption; permanent vegetation strips might likewise be improved through different species, wider strips, or altered management. \textcite{Dinnes2002} call for cultural and plant-breeding research to improve cover-crop compatibility, establishment, management, and reduced negative impacts on subsequent row crops, and \textcite{Liu2019} echo the need to improve establishment and cold hardiness. \textcite{Qi2011b} and \textcite{Qi2011} identify kura clover as requiring better agronomic management (living mulch with corn) to avoid corn yield loss under Iowa conditions.

On the environmental-mechanism side, \textcite{Lacey2015} call for research on the release of cover-crop-scavenged nitrogen in spring and on synchronizing N release from residue with the growth stages of corn and soybean. \textcite{Roth2018} identify determining the nitrogen content of grass cover crops through a producer-adoptable method as a research priority, and note that seeding rates could be adjusted while maintaining environmental benefits and that cover-crop and nutrient-management strategies could be developed to maintain cash-crop yields. \textcite{Askar2023} call for understanding the combined effects of cover crops and manure-rate application on subsurface dissolved reactive phosphorus loads and concentrations, evaluating climatic, soil, topographic, management, and crop-species factors. \textcite{Meyer2019} highlight the need to optimize trade-offs between the services and disservices of cover crops for water balance and to better understand the factors conditioning cover-crop effects on drainage. \textcite{Liu2019} more broadly call for coordinated common experiments across geophysical conditions to quantify winter crop-cover phosphorus contributions to runoff or leaching, to identify reliable plant and soil P analytes, to understand plant--root--soil--management--climate interactions on P losses, to evaluate water-quality tradeoffs under field settings, and to predict future-climate influences.

Cover-crop \emph{modeling} is a distinct frontier. \textcite{Feyereisen2006} enumerate needed advances: additional validation of the rye growth model, field verification of climate and soil-fertility effects on the ratio of rye N uptake to biomass, adjustment of radiation-use efficiency for atmospheric conditions, and better algorithms for plant N uptake. \textcite{Malone2007c} note that determining correct parameter values for winter cover crops requires thorough testing with several years of field-specific data, and that a more realistic N-uptake routine may improve corn-yield predictions. \textcite{Malone2014b} state that modeling germination and establishment of winter rye needs further research and that further analysis is the next step to assess regional cover-crop impacts. \textcite{Chatterjee2025} report that calibration of soil-organic-matter and drained-upper-limit parameters is critical and that a dedicated crop module for simulating winter rye could improve prediction of aboveground biomass and its consequences for cash-crop yield and water and N flux. \textcite{Dietzel2016} identify weaknesses in APSIM related to cover-crop effects on following-corn yields, winter soil temperatures under frozen conditions, and subsurface drainage in soybeans during flooding. \textcite{Gupta2022} propose developing a chemical-termination routine to enhance cover-crop impact studies, and \textcite{Gupta2023b} call for improving the drainage and soil-N mineralization/immobilization modules and studying extreme-event effects on cover-crop impacts to plan field-scale adaptive strategies. \textcite{Strock2004} report research in progress to predict cover-crop establishment and growth in Minnesota. \textcite{Singh2023} suggest investigating additional cover crops such as radish and legumes in hydrology and water-quality simulations.

\subsection{Drainage system design, water-table management, and materials}

Optimizing drainage design for the joint goals of crop yield and water quality is a persistent open problem. \textcite{Kladivko2021} argue that more field studies coupled with modeling are needed to develop site-specific recommendations and to optimize drainage design for both crop yield and water-quality purposes, calling continued efforts on systems approaches "urgently needed." \textcite{Zhao2016} suggest research into alternative drainage spacing aimed at balancing crop production against decreased drainage losses. \textcite{Singh2006b,Singh2007b} recommend field experiments in Iowa and the Upper Midwest to characterize how shallow and controlled drainage affect the complete water balance and crop production, including potential water-movement pathways, and note that a flexible scheme managing the control structure by soil-moisture status might reduce controlled-drainage impacts on relative yields and surface runoff. \textcite{Nash2015} state that more research is needed to determine whether managed drainage can consistently produce greater corn yield than free drainage, with benefits on claypan soils perhaps concentrated in higher-yield-potential years. \textcite{Nelson2011} call for further evaluation of drainage water management effects on high-yielding soybean cultivars, insect and disease management, cost-effectiveness in corn--soybean rotations, and grain-quality differences on claypan soils. \textcite{Dinnes2002} prioritize low-cost drainage-control and water-table-management designs that can be retrofitted to existing tile lines, and research on wetlands, buffers, and bioreactors to address residence time, carbon sources, land area, and cost limitations.

Cold-climate and peatland drainage raise specific needs: \textcite{Li2025} call for greater attention to optimizing winter subsurface-drainage and water-table management for croplands as temperatures warm, and \textcite{Läpikivi2025} identify needs to evaluate subsurface irrigation for maintaining shallow water-table depth in fields drained to current standards, to study water availability in catchments shared by multiple peatlands, and to map the distribution of cultivated peatlands and their water-table depths for catchment-wide application. \textcite{STEINHARDT1974} caution that under severe frost, subsurface drainage might irreversibly affect ploughed-soil structure and that other criteria (no-traffic systems, aeration-sensitive crops) may sometimes rule.

Drainage \emph{materials and installation} also feature. \textcite{Ghane2022} recommend long-term field investigations of knitted-sock envelope clogging across soil types to inform drainage design. \textcite{Qi2025b} report developing an integrated excavation-and-burial machine to reduce labor costs and call for investigating alternative rot-resistant straw-bundling techniques and burial methods to improve drainage longevity and waterlogging mitigation, while \textcite{Lu2018} plan to study the long-term impact of crop straw as subsurface-drainage material on drainage-water quality and soil properties under different irrigation schedules. \textcite{Ellafi2021} call for evaluating drainage designs' impacts on crop production, salinity control, and waterlogging using DRAINMOD, and for calculating predicted income across cropping systems. \textcite{Takeshima2023} recommend developing countermeasures against unpredictable rainfall, such as combining subsurface drainage with raised-bed planting.

\subsection{Nitrogen and nutrient management priorities}

Refining nitrogen management to reduce drainage losses without sacrificing yield is a core research need. \textcite{Jaynes2013} call for further field research or crop-modeling to pinpoint where between the V12 and V16 growth stages sidedress N ceases to be beneficial, and \textcite{Jaynes2015} note that sidedressing can be combined with soil tests or crop N-deficiency sensing for real-time N-rate adjustment. \textcite{Maas2022} recommend comprehensive tools integrating reliable weather forecasts, historical land management, local field and soil parameters, and crop-variety information to estimate optimum N rates, propose regional long-term agronomic-optimum-N-rate (AONR) means derived from fertilizer type, yield, and drainage status via surveys or census data, and call for study of the planting-date/agronomic-efficiency/AONR relationship and of the drivers of uncertainty and variability in agricultural systems. \textcite{Dinnes2002} prioritize better crediting of non-fertilizer N sources through soil tests, improved in-season soil-N monitoring for split applications (including earlier-season tests and models using soil respiration and meteorology), and reliable variable-rate N models incorporating remote sensing and hyperspectral approaches. \textcite{Cassman2025} propose low-cost proxy variables for monitoring soil N and water supply --- yield without applied N as a field-scale estimate of soil N-supply capacity, and partial factor productivity of applied N and rainfall as proxies for apparent N-use efficiency and economic return per unit rainfall.

At larger scales, \textcite{Zhao2016} call for constraining the magnitude and variability of crop-rotation impacts on yield-scaled N loss and for accounting for N losses on a rotation rather than annual basis. \textcite{Raymond2012} call for additional experimental and modeling work to resolve crop-management/N-export relationships, a more spatially intense monitoring system of riverine outflow and net anthropogenic nitrogen inputs coupled with detailed crop-management (particularly tile-drainage) information, and exploration of alternative drainage approaches, cover crops, and crops with more active rooting and transpiration. \textcite{Pasley2021} note very little research on more desirable rotation crops in simple Midwestern two-crop rotations from a leaching perspective. \textcite{Deng2011} plan to apply revised DNDC to more watersheds, improve parameterization for N-loss prediction, test the variance of surface-cover and management factors across crop-growth stages, and schedule aquatic biogeochemistry to address the fate of N in streams, rivers, lakes, reservoirs, and estuaries. \textcite{Kanwar1997} reinforce the need for studies on chemical placement, rate, and timing for various tillage practices to reduce tile-drainage chemical losses, incorporated into at least a two-year corn--soybean rotation to ensure economic and environmental viability. \textcite{Bakhsh2004} note that overprediction of soybean yields may require refining N-cycling algorithms for N\textsubscript{2} fixation and plant N-uptake. \textcite{Malone2018} call for field studies to evaluate fertilized/harvested rye cover crop, which if confirmed could offer a strategy to raise producer revenue and net energy production while reducing drainage N loss.

\subsection{Irrigation, salinity, and water reuse in arid and saline systems}

A parallel body of work addresses drainage in irrigated, arid, and saline settings. \textcite{Yao2017} call for long-term geohydrological and crop data --- including intensive electromagnetic-induction surveys, groundwater-dynamics time series, and crop biomass --- to validate simulations across irrigation/drainage systems and agronomic patterns. \textcite{Alzraiee2013} argue that future modeling must systematically evaluate alternative field-scale irrigation and drainage strategies to manage waterlogging and high soil salinity under ambiguity, that a statistical notion of the likelihood of achieving environmental and crop-production goals field-by-field would guide riskier watershed-scale plans, and that considering multiple irrigation seasons might reveal different spatial distributions of yield impact. \textcite{Bethune2008} recommend that, where tubewell drainage manages deep percolation, land-use planning steer irrigation development toward low-salinity, high-yielding aquifers, encourage sprinkler irrigation, and move new development to areas where salinity-management costs are low. \textcite{Qadir2001} suggest incorporating short-duration and salt-tolerant crops (e.g., Sesbania, Bermuda or Kallar grass) into reclamation rotations, and \textcite{Castanheira2010} call for quantifying rainfall amounts and irrigation-efficiency gains with respect to salt-removal capacity and reduced salt load.

Water-productivity and technology questions also recur. \textcite{Feng2019} propose using their outputs to design irrigation schedules under different hydrological years and soil water-salt environments and using the coupled HYDRUS--EPIC model to evaluate the future sustainability of saline-water irrigation. \textcite{Jia2017} recommend measuring water-table changes and their contribution to storage and evapotranspiration, recomputing water-application efficiency per day between soil samplings to account for water-table rise, reformulating the efficiency equation, and using longer irrigation durations or slower flow rates to improve uniformity. \textcite{Qin2025} call for scalable data-management infrastructures for long-term field deployment (energy-efficient edge computing, low-power IoT protocols), life-cycle cost and return-on-investment assessment under varying market and climate conditions, integration of agronomic models with environmental and economic frameworks in a unified precision-agriculture platform, and low-temperature crystallization to treat brine toward near-zero discharge. \textcite{Abdelkrim2021} note that specific irrigation dates and amounts should be scheduled using criteria beyond modeling, such as farmers' expert knowledge and sensors. \textcite{DINAR1989} call for a more complete economic evaluation of shifting irrigation technology including associated management variables, and \textcite{Qian2025} highlight the need to study solute transport during cultivation from a components perspective and to test applicability under drip irrigation. \textcite{ElHassanin2022} suggest that established thresholds could seed large-scale modeling or the development of water/drainage-management measures to raise field water-use fraction, crop-water productivity, and wheat production, and \textcite{RADSTAKE1988} recommend continued monitoring of crop yield and water-quality change to improve forecasts of groundwater suitability for irrigation in the Nile Valley. \textcite{Ayars2006} note that extending their method to other crops will require additional studies, and \textcite{Karegoudar2022} advise that policymakers may proceed under all three water-availability scenarios. Forage-water-use comparisons remain open: \textcite{Mundy2006} judge a comparison of the water-use efficiency of forages under flood versus spray irrigation worthwhile, and \textcite{Greenwood2006} defer forage yield and plant--soil--water responses to companion papers.

\subsection{Advancing process-based and biophysical models}

Beyond cover-crop and drainage modules, authors call broadly for stronger, better-validated, and more transferable models. \textcite{SKAGGS1994} state that much remains to be learned about the mechanisms governing pollutant losses from drained soils, that research must improve process understanding before quantification is possible, that methods are needed to quantify how alternative designs and operations affect crop yields and environmental impacts while considering interdependent cultural, fertility, and pest-management practices, and that better datasets are needed to test models across wide ranges of soil, crop, and climate. \textcite{Negm2014} call for more intense field measurements to validate their model and additional testing across conditions, noting its potential for simulating long-term climate-change impacts. \textcite{Grewal2025} prioritize improving prediction of high-load peak events, refining the concentration component of the model, further testing the fertilizer-effect hypothesis under ponding, and empirical data to validate predictions for cover-crop and controlled-drainage practices. \textcite{Gunn2018} report that DRAINMOD is being coupled with a crop model (DSSAT) to capture other factors influencing maize relative yield under subirrigation. \textcite{Tian2012} call for field verification of soil-physical-property changes, investigation of early-successional plant growth and its hydrological effects, verification of temporal stomatal-conductance changes against field measurements, and cautious evaluation of adding understory-growth and prescribed-burning modules. \textcite{Morrison2014} seek greater sensitivity to low flows, yearly crop-information inputs for calibration/validation consistency, and a macropore-flow algorithm to capture poorly draining soils. \textcite{Smith2008} argue that a validated process-based rather than empirical model should be applied to capture complex variable interactions. \textcite{Ndulue2024} recommend incorporating CO\textsubscript{2} effects, diseases, nutrient availability, cultivar type, and planting date for greater confidence in climate-change results. \textcite{Ma2025} argue that systematic data collection on water fluxes, solute-nutrient fluxes, greenhouse-gas emissions, root development, and aboveground biomass would facilitate model validation and refine mechanisms and parameterization. \textcite{Li2025} note that winter-crop-growth simulations need additional crop-genetic-parameter calibration and must account for elevated CO\textsubscript{2} and alternative management, and that simpler, faster biophysical models may be viable for large-scale winter-cropland simulation. \textcite{Dietzel2016} additionally call for improved subsurface-drainage simulation within soybean in APSIM. \textcite{Qi2010b} report a field-scale experiment being conducted in parallel to a non-weighing lysimeter study to validate rye cover-crop effects on soil-water dynamics and nitrate leaching, and \textcite{Malone2007c} note that per-plot soil-parameter calibration would improve drainage predictions.

\subsection{Climate change, extremes, and system resilience}

Climate adaptation frames many priorities. \textcite{Saha2024} stress continuing to identify practices and combinations that increase the resilience of agricultural systems to climate extremes. \textcite{Malone2020} lay out a modeling agenda addressing additional simulation periods, other representative concentration pathways, management options (reduced fertilizer rates; earlier cash- and cover-crop harvest, termination, and planting), cash-crop cultivars, cover-crop types, and additional soils and locations. \textcite{Ferin2023} recommend approaches allowing within-county variation in fertilizer application rate, crop-specific manure applications as data become available, multiple global-climate-model forcings from CMIP5 or CMIP6, and attention to co-benefits of perennial grasses such as carbon sequestration and lower N\textsubscript{2}O loss. \textcite{Dietzel2016} argue that efforts to raise water-use efficiency should design cropping systems that function under both drought and flood, diversify whole-farm designs to include drought- and flood-tolerant plants, and pursue engineering solutions such as capturing excess floodwater for later use in drought. \textcite{PeltonenSainio2021} call for more information on the costs and benefits of adaptation measures for changing precipitation, a thorough assessment of the payback period for irrigation investment in Finland, stronger knowledge-sharing on carbon farming and soil-carbon maintenance, and additional funding and decision tools for both irrigation and drainage investment. \textcite{Morton2015} identify a need for increased localized understanding of climate patterns and how farmers incorporate actual and perceived experiences of excess precipitation into decisions, more accurate downscaled climate information and local forecasts beyond 3--5 days, and better understanding of the relationships among practices and adoption timescales. \textcite{Gupta2023b} likewise call for studying extreme-event effects on cover-crop impacts to plan field-scale adaptation.

\subsection{Remote sensing, precision tools, and decision support}

Emerging sensing and data technologies are widely seen as enabling the next phase of research. \textcite{Khanal2020} outline a broad agenda: determining the optimal field conditions and image quality for detecting tile-drainage patterns and advanced processing for automatic extraction of tile-drain lines; downscaling techniques combining multiple data sources for finer-resolution soil moisture; integrating remote sensing with machine learning to assess soil properties and compaction; coupling crop growth stages with forecasted weather for supply forecasting; leveraging high-resolution remote sensing with crop modeling to understand within-field nitrogen-stress variability and variable-rate N benefits; mapping crop genotypes and their role in grain quality; developing databases for early crop-disease detection; applying semi-supervised learning and generative adversarial networks to weed identification; and site-specific residue management. \textcite{Cassman2025} propose a robust spatial framework combining climate and soil type with geo-referenced farmer-reported rotation, yield, and management data, tailoring a Climate Zone and Technology Extrapolation Domain framework to delineate conditions under which soil-health-improving-practice adoption yields similar impacts, and using remote-sensing, satellite-imagery, and on-farm databases to estimate field-specific benefits. \textcite{Reid2018} call for validating individual components and aggregate edge-of-field losses for Canadian conditions, a large effort to quantify edge-of-field-to-surface-water delivery ratios, improved input-data collection with possible remote sensing of topography, soil moisture, crop area, and soil cover, and intensity-based indicators of phosphorus loss per unit production developed with commodity organizations. \textcite{Julien2023} envision a soil-wetness-index approach forming part of a decision-support tool integrated into marketing-authorization recommendations, while noting that assumptions about drainage-season insensitivity to winter crop type and site-dependent thresholds need testing across soils.

\subsection{Cropping-system diversification: perennials, bioenergy, and rotations}

Diversified and perennial systems raise distinctive research needs. \textcite{Arrueta2022} call for establishing a clear causal link between perennial-crop implementation and nutrient loads across fields with varying physical and chemical soil characteristics, and note that government policies encouraging landscape diversification and rewarding environmental services may be needed to overcome adoption barriers. \textcite{Wilson2023} call for robust observed water-quality datasets for perennialized cropping systems to understand their regional water-quality impact, supporting watershed-scale learning and collective action around novel crops. \textcite{Guo2018} propose large-scale assessment of bioenergy-crop scenarios in the Mississippi River system and other regions (e.g., China's marginal lands), quantification of biofeedstock production and its water-quantity/quality impacts in mildly sloped watersheds, investigation of pasture loss during bioenergy planting, field experiments to quantify water-quality impacts, and monitoring of nutrient-reduction efficiencies with cost/benefit assessment to alleviate Gulf of Mexico hypoxia. \textcite{Gentry2025} report future work refining management within a diverse cropping system, documenting economic viability against the conventional two-year rotation, nested microplot studies of wheat varieties and seeding rates for double-crop soybean, and planned water-infiltration studies of earthworm influence on tile flow and nitrate loss. \textcite{Dinnes2002} call for perennial cash-cropping systems that maintain economic returns while reducing nitrate leaching. \textcite{Malone2018} highlight double-cropping fertilized rye as a promising revenue, net-energy, and drainage-N strategy pending field confirmation, and \textcite{HUNDAL1976} report ongoing studies of meadow cropping effects on soil conditions and grain yields.

\subsection{Economics, policy, adoption, and the human dimension}

Bridging biophysical findings to on-farm and policy decisions is a recurrent frontier. \textcite{Waring2020} prioritize the economics of cover crops. \textcite{Roth2018} note that quantifying soil-health impacts, surface and subsurface phosphorus losses, and denitrification changes could increase cost recovery, and that a long-term average of total cover-crop cost recovery could guide policymakers on subsidies. \textcite{Mourtzinis2021} observe that a long-term cumulative yield-and-profit assessment of drainage installation would require installation cost, yield responses in other rotation crops, land value, and other farm benefits, and could quantify drainage adoption's contribution to soybean yield gain. \textcite{Malone2023} call for information on producer participation, pilot programs in key locations, field trials and modeling clarifying how primary-crop yield, rye yield, and N losses vary with soil, climate, and management, and additional studies to advance policies optimizing environmental quality, bioenergy, carbon, yields, and profits. \textcite{Ferin2023} argue that future efforts should ultimately include the multitude of biophysical factors and socioeconomic nuances through integrated biophysical--economic modeling at the field scale. \textcite{Kröger2012} urge committing significant resources to replicable studies in the Lower Mississippi Alluvial Valley to estimate best-management-practice effectiveness under realistic conditions, plus additional field-based monitoring to generate efficiency estimates for conservation-program and policy analysis. \textcite{Bakhsh2007b} argue that landscape/hydrologic spatial zones should be managed with suitable tillage and cropping systems for watershed sustainability and that decision tools should account for soil and landscape effects on yields. \textcite{Nelson2011} include cost-effectiveness in corn--soybean rotations among evaluation needs, and \textcite{Jaynes2001} conclude that dramatic changes in management on artificially drained soils will be required before corn production can be considered sustainable.

\subsection{Soil health, biology, and physical processes}

Slower-acting soil and biological processes are flagged as understudied. \textcite{Han2017} note that the link between \emph{Fusarium} abundance and soybean root rot needs examination and that long-term drainage may affect field \emph{Fusarium} populations and, under certain conditions, soybean-root health and growth. \textcite{Kumar2014b} call for measuring biogeochemical processes involved in methane production and oxidation and modeling these fluxes from poorly drained soils. \textcite{Shipitalo2004} suggest that disrupting the linkage of cracks to earthworm burrows through the plow pan --- via conservation tillage as an alternative to moldboard plowing and lightweight tractors to reduce compaction and prevent plow-pan reformation --- might reduce sediment and contaminant movement to subdrains, noting no-till has reduced sediment losses in crack-prone clayey soils. \textcite{Daigh2014} call for precisely quantifying evapotranspiration to explain drain-flow differences among cropping systems, and \textcite{Tian2012} note early-successional growth and soil-physical-property changes need verification. \textcite{DarziNaftchali2017b} argue that the drought tolerance and phenological traits of rice cultivars should guide selection of drainage systems and drying periods, timed to cultivar sensitivity to water deficit. \textcite{Nash2017} advise farmers to test forage sorghum for toxic nitrate levels before grazing regardless of perceived drought. \textcite{Manninen2018} call for understanding the reactions and role of agriculturally derived dissolved organic matter in marine systems over time, and \textcite{GiménezValero2024} anticipate complementing metabolomic fruit analysis with studies of cultivation-technique effects on citrus fruit quality and size.

\subsection{Watershed-scale synthesis, monitoring networks, and data infrastructure}

Finally, several authors emphasize scaling from field to watershed and building the data infrastructure to support it. \textcite{Baffaut2020} propose modifying the Soil Vulnerability Index to differentiate surface from subsurface artificial drainage (adjusting the risk class of the runoff or leaching component), to consider the leaching component both with and without artificial drainage to distinguish permeable-soil from drainage causes, to account for irrigation impacts on surface-water quality and drainage, to incorporate rainfall intensity (possibly via the R-factor), and to determine exact soil components where complex soils or land leveling complicate classification. \textcite{Singh2023} suggest calibrating and validating models for multiple sub-watersheds and comparing modeled versus observed crop yield at the sub-watershed level for more reliable estimates. \textcite{Smith2008} recommend focusing future conservation-assessment efforts on managing potholes in cropped fields --- especially corn fields where potholes drain directly to ditches --- and their effects on tile-drainage water. \textcite{Guo2018} extend this to large-scale bioenergy assessment across river systems. \textcite{Ferin2023} and \textcite{Deng2011} both point toward multi-forcing, multi-watershed application. Underlying all of these is the data imperative articulated by \textcite{Ma2025} and \textcite{SKAGGS1994}: better, longer, and more spatially representative datasets --- spanning water and solute fluxes, greenhouse-gas emissions, root and biomass development, and management history --- are the foundational requirement for validating models, resolving tradeoffs, and delivering the site-specific, climate-resilient drainage-management guidance the field ultimately seeks.

### Material: Reducing Nitrate Losses via Management

\subsection{Overview}
Reducing nitrate--nitrogen (NO$_3$-N) losses to subsurface (tile) drainage and the underlying groundwater remains one of the least settled objectives in drainage science: although a wide portfolio of management levers has been proposed, the field is still dominated by open questions about which practices work, under what conditions, at what cost, and with what unintended tradeoffs. Across sites from Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Minnesota, and the Dakotas in the U.S.\ Midwest to Canada, and across Europe (Finland, Denmark, Sweden, Norway, France, Germany, Switzerland, Italy, Ireland, Latvia) and beyond, authors repeatedly frame their contributions less as definitive answers than as calls for further field experiments, better process understanding, and improved decision-support tools. \textcite{ROSSI1991} state the underlying urgency plainly: management directed at substantial nitrate reductions is needed, together with careful evaluation of the effects of fertilizer timing, rate, form, and placement on NO$_3^-$ loss via subsurface drainage.

Several broad lines of evidence and research need recur throughout this theme. First, \emph{fertilizer N management}---rate, timing, form, placement, and crediting of residual and mineralizable N---is widely seen as the primary but incompletely optimized lever. Second, \emph{cropping-system change}, especially cover crops, perennials, and alternative rotations, is promoted but still under-quantified across soils and climates. Third, \emph{drainage water management} (controlled drainage, shallow drainage, water-table control) and \emph{edge-of-field treatment} (bioreactors, saturated riparian buffers, wetlands) are promising but require optimization and long-term verification. Fourth, \emph{process-based modeling} is expected to carry much of the predictive burden as concerns about nitrate export grow, yet substantial algorithmic gaps remain---particularly for winter, soil C--N cycling, and groundwater interactions. Fifth, \emph{monitoring, tracers, and scaling} methods must improve to apportion sources and extend site findings to landscapes. Finally, cutting across all of these are unresolved \emph{tradeoffs}---between nitrate reduction and yield, greenhouse-gas emissions, phosphorus loss, and economic risk---that the field has not yet adequately characterized. \textcite{Du2017} anticipate that models will be relied upon extensively to estimate management effects as concern over nitrate export increases, and can enhance understanding of how N management affects water quality.

\subsection{Fertilizer nitrogen management: rate, timing, form, and crediting}
The most consistently identified priority is refining fertilizer N management. \textcite{Zhao2000} argue that efforts should concentrate on identifying N management strategies that increase N-use efficiency in corn production, pointing specifically to optimal rate, timing, and form as targets for reducing nitrate levels in the Minnesota and upper Mississippi rivers. \textcite{Randall2001} similarly call to fine-tune fertilizer N management by applying the correct rate at the optimum time and crediting previous legume crops and animal-manure applications, and to improve animal-manure management by knowing nutrient content and application rate, spreading uniformly, and incorporating in a timely manner. \textcite{Emmett2022} note that research determining optimal rates and timing of N fertilization can help reduce N$_2$O emissions and minimize tradeoffs between yield and environmental performance. At the watershed scale, \textcite{Dattamudi2020} conclude that N application schemes need reevaluation for better N management and environmental sustainability, suggesting lower fertilizer rates for corn, precision agriculture, use of soil N with high-yielding varieties, split fall/summer application, and proper timing. \textcite{Elmi2002} add that reductions may be possible by carefully managing fertilization rates and timing to match crop uptake.

\subsubsection{Crediting residual, mineralizable, and legacy nitrogen}
A recurring, more specific gap is the crediting of N supplied by soil organic matter, prior legumes, manure, and land-use legacies when setting application rates. \textcite{Bakhsh2007} state a need to assess proper N credit due to mineralization from the soil organic-matter pool and soybean residue when deciding N application rates from organic or inorganic sources, and \textcite{Bakhsh2005} likewise call for assessing proper N credit to soybean and the storage of mineralizable N in the root zone before applications, to reduce NO$_3$-N leaching potential with tile flows. \textcite{Randall2001} extend this to a methodological need: improved soil-N testing methods to estimate mineralizable N and carryover N, especially after dry years, legumes, or manure applications. In systems transitioning from perennial cover, \textcite{Huggins2001} propose adjusting N recommendations to include second-year credits for N mineralization following perennial-grass conversion to row crops, note that N strategies for corn must be developed to efficiently use the release of mineralized N from CRP land, and observe that conservation tillage during CRP/alfalfa conversion would likely slow mineralization and alter residual soil N buildup and NO$_3$-N losses. \textcite{Weed1996} recommend minimizing NH$_3$ fertilizer applied to continuous- and rotation-corn plots by using late-spring soil-nitrate tests. On land plowed out of alfalfa, \textcite{ROBBINS1980} counsel care in determining N rates and timing and note that improved timing and amount of applied water could decrease required N rates while making more N available to the crop.

For irrigated systems on vulnerable soils, \textcite{Derby2009} enumerate management options---reducing N application during the first years after irrigation initiation, setting reasonable yield goals, giving proper credit for fall residual soil NO$_3$-N, and adjusting applications for adverse weather or poor soil properties---and emphasize that field-scale studies spanning soil, vadose-zone, and groundwater levels across multiple management periods are necessary to understand water quality under irrigated agriculture. \textcite{Hurtado2011} report that lower N rates in cotton could reduce losses during the cotton season and the beginning of the following one. \textcite{Ale2012b} caution that model-predicted excess soil-N reserves at the studied N rates must be verified, while noting the potential to further reduce fertilizer from the lowest rates given soil-N budget surpluses, which may further cut NO$_3$-N losses toward discharge-reduction goals. The persistence and role of \emph{legacy} N is itself flagged as underexplored: \textcite{Williams2022} argue that a clearer definition of legacy nutrients is urgently needed to better apportion losses and develop mitigation strategies, and \textcite{Hu2024} call for research into the coupled biogeochemical and hydrological processes that regulate the formation of legacy N stores and their impact on NO$_3^-$ export in tile-drained agroecosystems.

\subsubsection{Enhanced-efficiency fertilizers, stabilizers, and application method}
Beyond rate and timing, several authors point to product- and method-level questions. \textcite{Kaur2024c} recommend that future research address enhanced-efficiency fertilizer technology---spring-applied anhydrous ammonia with a nitrification inhibitor---in conjunction with enhanced drainage water management in corn--soybean rotations. \textcite{Vincent2025} note that management of NH$_4^+$-N specifically can be achieved through N stabilizers applied alongside fertilizers, and that the effects of cover crops on field- and watershed-scale NH$_4^+$-N dynamics deserve further exploration. Regarding manure delivery, \textcite{Gamble2018} report that drainage NO$_3$-N concentrations from fertigated silage corn need further study to determine whether they can be reduced to acceptable levels without impacting silage yield, and stress that tradeoffs of fertigation---crop injury, disease, pathogen transport to waterways, and alternative N-loss pathways relative to slurry injection---require further exploration. \textcite{Bechmann2014} recommend developing a strategy to even out manure application within catchments of high livestock density, and that mitigation in cereal areas should focus on reducing soil mineral N in spring and autumn.

\subsection{Timing of loss: the early-spring and seasonal window}
A distinctive and repeatedly emphasized frontier is the temporal concentration of losses, particularly in early spring, and the need for management targeted to that window. \textcite{Bakhsh2007} state that strategies need to be developed to reduce early-spring NO$_3$-N leaching losses, and \textcite{Bakhsh2010} extend this to reducing off-site nitrate transport during the March-through-June period, adding that the temporal distribution of rainfall during the growing season needs further attention when evaluating and managing nitrate leaching to drainage water. \textcite{Nance2007} argue that assessment of soil NO$_3$-N status, tile flow, and NO$_3$-N loss during April, May, and June warrants further study. \textcite{Gentry2024} suggest that a reliable, winter-hardy cover crop ahead of corn may help reduce tile nitrate in corn--soybean rotations, and that moving fall N application to spring while using overwintering grass cover crops may be the best way to decrease N loss in intensively tile-drained watersheds. For drainage-outlet management, \textcite{Skaggs1995} recommend selectively managing drainage outlets to reduce peak NO$_3$-N losses, especially in years following drought-reduced yields, and both \textcite{Elmi2004} and \textcite{Elmi2002} note that further reductions could be achieved if controlled drainage is kept operational during early spring (and late fall), when NO$_3$-N losses are most severe and drainage is not needed for crop production.

\subsection{Cropping systems: cover crops, perennials, and rotations}
Cropping-system change is widely promoted but consistently flagged as needing more field evidence, especially across the diversity of soils and climates. \textcite{Kladivko2014} call directly for further research, development, and technical assistance to overcome practical obstacles to cover-crop adoption in the Midwest, for additional field experiments measuring cover-crop effects on NO$_3$ losses in drainage water on multiple soil types across the region, and for using the new data to calibrate and validate RZWQM for additional soils and improve regional estimates of cover-crop NO$_3$ reductions. \textcite{Oliveira2025} identify multiple open questions: how variations in cover-crop biomass, termination timing, and rooting depth interact with drainage dynamics to further affect NO$_3^-$ and dissolved N$_2$O losses, and how variable drainage intensity, fluctuating water tables, and differences in cover-crop biomass and species composition influence dissolved CH$_4$. \textcite{Lagzdins2015} list appropriate mitigation measures needing implementation---annual winter crops, winter cover crops where spring crops are the main crop, and no autumn tillage---to reduce nitrate-N losses to drainage water.

Novel and perennial-based options are also proposed for study. \textcite{Nance2007} highlight the potential of intercropping triticale with red clover for minimizing soil NO$_3$-N and urge that research and policy encourage development of winter triticale to reduce soil NO$_3$-N loss from U.S.\ cropping systems. \textcite{Emmett2022} note that improvements in productivity and land-use efficiency in camelina--soybean relay- and double-cropping systems will be essential to the viability of that sustainable-intensification strategy, and that efforts to improve fall establishment and reduce N surpluses may enable desired reductions in nitrate leaching. \textcite{Randall2001} advocate developing alternative cropping systems containing perennial crops to reduce nitrate losses, and both \textcite{Gowda2007} and \textcite{Nangia2008} suggest that attaining nitrate TMDLs or larger reductions may require alternatives beyond fertilizer management---such as partial conversion of cropland to pasture, changes in tile-drain spacing and/or depth, fall cover crops, or wetland restoration. \textcite{Kaur2024c} add that under variable climate, crop rotations may need modification, or cover crops planted in the winter fallow season to minimize N losses and capture unused soil-profile N. \textcite{Ma2007b} recommend explicitly evaluating controlled drainage, crop rotation, cover crops, and N management for reducing NO$_3$-N loss in drain flow, and \textcite{Malone2019} propose field studies (and paired model testing) of N loss to subsurface drainage with and without corn-stover harvest.

\subsection{Drainage water management and water-table control}
Controlled drainage, shallow drainage, and other water-table management approaches are seen as promising mitigation but require optimization, verification under varied climates, and clearer partitioning of their mechanisms. \textcite{Fang2012} stress that careful management of the timing and depth of gate operation is very important to optimize reduced N loss using controlled drainage. \textcite{Wesström2001} argue that further investigation of controlled drainage/water-table (CWT) system management is needed to optimize both environmental and agricultural benefits, and that water balances for conventional and controlled plots must be established before the reasons behind differing outflow patterns can be separated and quantified. \textcite{Smith2011b} note that continued investigation in years with normal precipitation may clarify whether controlled drainage offers advantages over conventional systems for water conservation, and that further work using shallow drainage under various climatic conditions may reveal its NO$_3$-N reduction potential. \textcite{Madramootoo2024} suggest that controlled-drainage adoption may become more relevant under wetter, warmer climates, and highlight the need for practical water-table control-structure management during wetter springs to mitigate NO$_3$-N concentrations while allowing timely field operations. \textcite{Bakhsh2007} additionally suggest variable-rate controlled drainage may be helpful. \textcite{Schott2017b} identify economic and agronomic questions: whether drainage treatment affects yields (via SEW$_{30}$) during springs with frequent, heavy precipitation, how such conditions affect planting dates, and how the economic risk of reducing NO$_3$ loss with drainage water management can be better evaluated. \textcite{Miller2022} argue that more work is needed to understand the collective impacts of automated drainage water management (ADWM) on the coupled timing of flows and composition of water leaving fields, and how slowing tile flows affects nutrient export, before its full efficacy as a BMP can be assessed. \textcite{Barkle2021} suggest water-table management and edge-of-field bioreactors as mitigation options depending on site conditions.

\subsection{Edge-of-field and in-field treatment technologies}
A distinct research frontier concerns engineered nitrate-removal structures---bioreactors, saturated riparian buffers, constructed wetlands, and filter-based drainage---where design optimization and long-term field performance remain open. \textcite{McEachran2020} state that a key remaining challenge is to develop better ways to predict nitrate removal so that more effective saturated riparian buffers (SRBs) can be designed and implemented; they outline methodological advances including deriving an unsteady form of the governing equation by solving a one-dimensional diffusion equation for the head, computing average linear velocity, and solving nitrate transport numerically, and note that using an optimal-width approximation would help determine effective designs. \textcite{Jaynes2014} propose that if their SRB results can be replicated across landscapes, the practice could prevent substantial NO$_3$ from entering midwestern streams, and that if sequestration is an important removal pathway, biomass harvesting (perhaps as bioenergy) could maintain long-term viability. \textcite{Abdalaal2023} recommend improving how nitrate removal is represented in buffer designs, incorporating more sophisticated removal and hydrology components, coupling design with economic analysis or user objective functions targeting specific annual load-reduction goals, and first determining maximum allowable buffer length before optimizing width to meet load-removal and financial targets.

For bioreactors and combined installations, \textcite{Husk2017} call for further study of controlled drainage with and without a denitrification bioreactor to quantify each component's contribution, note that treating greater water volumes at lower efficiency could improve overall N mass removal (to be tested in performance-optimization studies), stress verification with more on-farm installations, and warn that the suitability of slowing subsurface flow through the bioreactor to filter suspended solids and colloids must be evaluated given clogging potential. \textcite{Vallée2014} encourage planting vegetation and maintaining organic matrices (straw, dead leaves) inside reactive riparian zones (RRZs) to increase effectiveness, since plants could raise efficiency through absorption and contributions to degradation processes. \textcite{Randall2001} propose constructing wetland restoration areas or denitrifying ponds to treat drainage water before it enters ditches or rivers. \textcite{Mendes2021} outline a broad research agenda for constructed treatment systems: thoroughly investigating the driving factors behind performance to support local implementation and nitrogen-removal compliance, clarifying the relationship between water-flow dynamics and nitrogen treatment, elucidating design-parameter effects, conducting long-term field-scale tests of nitrogen biogeochemical processes, investigating volatilization in surface-flow constructed wetlands under anaerobic basic conditions, and developing operations or interventions to enhance winter efficiency and stabilize year-round performance. For filter-based drainage, \textcite{Tao2022} note that over long operation, nitrification and denitrification in the soil and filter cannot be neglected, that long-term field experiments with different filter materials are needed, and that explaining adsorption, desorption, nitrification, and denitrification for different filter materials may be a new area of future exploration.

\subsection{Process-based modeling: outstanding algorithmic and evaluation gaps}
Because field trials cannot cover all combinations of practice, soil, and climate, process-based models are expected to fill the gap---yet the literature documents extensive, still-unresolved modeling needs.

\subsubsection{Soil carbon--nitrogen cycling, plant uptake, and crop growth}
Many authors identify soil N-process representation as the weakest link. \textcite{Smith2020} recommend improving biogeochemical processes such as nitrification, denitrification, fermentation, and mineralization, and using an improved hydrology framework to target denitrification at more appropriate ranges of soil water and oxygen contents. \textcite{Fang2012} attribute high errors in monthly flow-weighted nitrate-concentration predictions to the complexity of crop N uptake, soil N cycling, and transformations among C and N pools, and call for improvement there. \textcite{Hu2007} argue that improving SWAT's simulation of soil N processes, soybean N fixation, and maize-yield simulation could yield more reliable predictions of nutrient-management outcomes in tile-drained watersheds. \textcite{Malone2019} recommend modifying SWAT's nutrient-cycling algorithms to improve immobilization and other processes relevant to corn-stover removal, comparing SWAT and RZWQM at the same site with stover-harvest data, modifying RZWQM's stover-harvest routine to remove greater portions of the upper stalk, and using the modified RZWQM of Fang et al.\ (2017) for more accurate grain and stover N. \textcite{Ma2007} list needed RZWQM improvements: accounting for subsurface lateral flow below tiles between adjacent plots, simulating N fixation rather than supplying all soybean N, improving plant N uptake, incorporating surface roughness/detention storage in runoff, reducing winter nitrification so more soil NH$_4$-N remains at planting, and applying system models to long-term field studies while evaluating all components rather than one. \textcite{Ma2007b} reiterate the need for better simulation of management effects on yield and N mineralization, improved soybean N fixation and plant N uptake, and enhanced post-tillage mineralization, and recommend extending RZWQM to other tile-drained Midwest sites to evaluate hypoxia-alleviating practices. \textcite{Ma2008} add that crop responses to N-application timing need improvement, that use of RZWQM without soil microbial growth needs evaluation for other soils and climates (especially where water is limited and soil organic C differs), and that rigorous evaluation of each soil C/N process and pool against data is required before microbial growth's role can be understood. \textcite{Ma2001} propose a wide-ranging RZWQM agenda: reexamining MSEA datasets with the newest version, introducing variable cracking or effective macroporosity, adding phosphorus soil dynamics for manure management, including mobile organic-matter movement for long-term simulation, studying plant responses to water and N stresses and parameterizing new crops such as wheat, standardizing calibration and database development, evaluating responses to irrigation/fertilization/manure/pesticide management at multiple locations, further evaluating new modules (temperature, freeze/snow heat transport, overland-flow and sediment routing, gas emissions), linking RZWQM to a GIS with economic decision support, and modularizing the model. \textcite{Marjerison2016} report ongoing revision of winter processes and of crop-available-N estimation as suitable datasets emerge, plus work to improve simulation of organic-C mineralization from SOM, residue, and manures and of gaseous N losses in the PNM model. \textcite{Bechtold2007} note that a more robust approach to quantifying grass biomass and N uptake may be needed to simulate C and N dynamics in drained grassland. At the watershed scale, \textcite{Qi2025} recommend developing a more physically based model to represent NO$_3^-$ leaching through tile drainage, pairing outlet NO$_3^-$ loads with soil-moisture and N$_2$O observations to reduce uncertainty in denitrification and N-gas estimates, using ``soft data'' to simulate within-watershed dynamics rather than only outlet loads, adding crop-yield/biomass observations to improve growth-induced N dynamics, and introducing a passive humus pool to address underestimation of organic-N loss via surface runoff.

\subsubsection{Winter, frozen-soil, and snow processes}
Cold-season representation is repeatedly singled out as inadequate. \textcite{Feyereisen2006b} state that better algorithms are needed for soil freezing/thawing and spring-thaw drainage events, that more detailed mechanistic plant-N-uptake representation is needed, and that more detailed snow--freeze--thaw algorithms are still being sought for agronomic systems models. \textcite{Yang2007} call for improving DRAINMOD-N to estimate NO$_3$-N loss more accurately in freezing and thawing seasons and to include inputs such as tillage and intercropping. \textcite{Saadat2020} propose investigating such processes in drained fields to improve DRAINMOD predictions in seasonally frozen regions. \textcite{Jiang2020} note that additional winter data---snow depth, melting, and frost-affected hydraulic conductivity---may help test and improve the model. \textcite{Li2024b} suggest adapting an impedance factor for the RZ-SHAW model's frozen-soil hydraulic conductivity, including soil ice-content measurements in field experiments, implementing a calibratable snow--rain air-temperature threshold, and devoting more attention to new winter management for planting windows and nutrient loss. \textcite{Ahmed2007b} state that continued development and testing of RZWQM for winter conditions is required, along with further testing of ET and plant-water-uptake components, simulation of a wider range of crops for BMP evaluation in sensitive groundwater regions, and more field work to identify coefficient values for the OMNI nutrient sub-model. \textcite{Ahmed2007} similarly recommend improving RZWQM's snow algorithm, incorporating the McBride (1983) water-retention model and Brooks--Corey parameter adjustments for Ontario silt loam, more field work for nutrient-component coefficients, and further testing of ET and plant-water-uptake components. \textcite{Yang2021} indicate it is probably necessary to improve RZWQM2's accuracy in simulating winter runoff-N in winter-wheat cover-crop systems. \textcite{Roy2020} call for research on swelling and shrinkage during infiltration of water into frozen soil. \textcite{Motarjemi2021} require improved winter-crop parameterization, further attention to soil-surface and macropore infiltration processes, evaluation of the Daisy macropore setup where drain-connected macropores exist (with extensive parameter calibration), studies revealing N-loss trends under current practices, and improved management to lower NO$_3$-N in drainage under conventional rotations.

\subsubsection{Macropores, preferential flow, and drain configuration}
\textcite{Smith2020} recommend improving runoff simulation under surface crusting, clay cracking, preferential flow, snow dynamics, and freeze--thaw. \textcite{Stamm2002} argue that more attention should be given to actual drain configuration to complete the picture of factors determining solute transport in drained soils. \textcite{Chelil2022b} propose upgrading SIDRA-RU with a new nitrate-transport model to assimilate nitrate observations, investigating the effect of sampling interval (weekly/bi-weekly versus daily discharge), examining second-order interactions when approximating total-effect Sobol indices, integrating a crop-rotation module if data allow, using water-level, concentration, and flux data to better constrain solutions, and considering time-variant parameter estimation with hourly discharge. \textcite{Jeantet2021} suggest adding reference sites to improve SIDRA-RU robustness, treating the S$_{inter}$ parameter as a variable adjusted to prior-year meteorology and soil parameters, coupling with the PESTDRAIN module for pesticide leaching, and combining SIDRA-RU with a nitrate-leaching module to assess water pollution on French drained plots.

\subsubsection{Drain-design decision support and validation}
\textcite{Moriasi2013} propose using the Revised SWAT model to determine optimum drain depth and spacing that reduce NO$_3$-N losses while optimizing yield, but stress that validation studies of the Hooghoudt and Kirkham tile-drain equations in Revised SWAT are needed before use as a decision-support tool. \textcite{Ale2012} recommend validating a nitrate-load methodology by comparison with N models such as DRAINMOD-NII or RZWQM and by evaluation in additional watersheds where loads from both tile drains and streamflow have been measured. \textcite{Ng2000} note that NO$_3$-N leaching modeling for at least one year is required and that further calibration with consecutive multi-year data may improve prediction for free-drainage treatments. \textcite{Skaggs1995} caution that additional testing for other soils, crops, and locations is needed before routine model application. \textcite{Malone2007} argue that overcoming regression-based limitations and comprehensively quantifying yield and nitrate loading requires process-based models, that simulation results under different conditions could feed a database for economic and risk analysis, and that developing regression equations is a step toward a simple, accurate, objective method to quantify management and climate effects on nitrate loading and yield for a region.

\subsubsection{Groundwater interactions and integrated transport}
Coupling drainage models to groundwater is a distinct need. \textcite{Addab2022} recommend using SWAT-MODFLOW-RT3D for physically based, spatially distributed flow and reactive-transport modeling once a salinity module is incorporated, simulating groundwater storage, flow, and groundwater--tile-drain interactions in a physically based manner, and exploring the balance between improved crop yield in tiled areas and decreased downstream water quality. \textcite{Hoang2017} state that improving SWAT's groundwater module to simulate water-table fluctuation, soil saturation, and groundwater--surface-water exchange is necessary to extend SWAT\_LS validity and realistically represent nitrate retention and removal in riparian zones. \textcite{Hojberg2017} argue that optimal regulation requires considering both spatial variation in N leaching and variation in N reduction across groundwater and surface-water systems. \textcite{Elsayed2023} propose developing integrated mathematical models for nutrient transport in surface-water--groundwater interactions, evaluating climate-change effects, assessing crop-management influences, applying machine-learning algorithms to nutrient-transport datasets, and better understanding governing factors of in-stream sediment and nutrient transport. \textcite{Barkle2021} note that site-specific investigations establishing defensible water balances, or detailed groundwater-flow modeling, are needed to realistically estimate the split between artificial drainage and groundwater flows. \textcite{Yang2008} call for further work to improve NH$_4$-N transport and transformation modeling.

\subsection{Greenhouse-gas tradeoffs and indirect N$_2$O emissions}
A prominent frontier is the coupling between nitrate losses and greenhouse-gas emissions, especially indirect N$_2$O. \textcite{OBrien2022} suggest that continued investigation of the linkages between nitrate leaching and N$_2$O emissions may reveal different dynamics when N application and precipitation are temporally disconnected, and note the need to identify strategies that mitigate losses and adapt to changing precipitation and temperature. \textcite{Sawamoto2003} call for more studies on the NO$_3$--N$_2$O relationship in groundwater, on both direct and indirect N$_2$O emissions under typical conditions, and specifically on indirect N$_2$O emission from subsurface drainage---particularly the NO$_3$-leaching--N$_2$O-leaching relationship important for predicting indirect emission. \textcite{Minamikawa2015} state that field identification is still required and that new or revised calculation methods for indirect N$_2$O associated with leaching and runoff are needed to reflect differences in dominant microbial processes, along with evaluation of the tradeoff between indirect N$_2$O emission and N removal by heterotrophic denitrification across groundwater, surface drainage, river, and estuary. \textcite{Minamikawa2013} note further investigation is required to evaluate groundwater-induced N$_2$O emissions through the soil surface, and \textcite{Minamikawa2010} call for clarifying relations between redox conditions and greenhouse-gas production in the subsoil, for $^{15}$N isotope analysis of nitrate and N$_2$O to locate N$_2$O production, and for understanding production, consumption, and transport of greenhouse gases in profiles with shallow groundwater. \textcite{Smith2019} suggest that future studies of dissolved N$_2$O losses from drainage water may reveal whether they exceed surface emissions, and that further research is needed on the benefits of controlled drainage as a climate-adaptation option in the Atlantic region. \textcite{Oliveira2025} urge exploration of long-term impacts of management on cumulative dissolved greenhouse-gas emissions to inform climate-smart strategies. \textcite{Webb2021} recommend, for artificial-water emissions accounting, examining seasonal impacts on emission factors, developing a process-based model as a likely improvement over revising default EFs (supported by more studies), developing gas-transfer-velocity models across specific artificial-water types, exploring N$_2$O-production thresholds in high-NO$_3$ environments, investigating the DOC/NO$_3$ ratio, building a global database on artificial-water size, density, and distribution (beyond the UK), and targeting field measurements across major agricultural industries. \textcite{Fontes2019} plan to monitor both N$_2$O and NO$_3$-N leaching year-round across all experimental units for better total-N-loss estimates, encourage testing remote-sensing technologies for environmental monitoring, and stress attention to temporal and spatial methodology when evaluating both loss pathways together.

\subsection{Monitoring, tracers, and source apportionment}
Improved measurement and tracing underpin many proposed advances. \textcite{Williams2022} call for higher-frequency measurements (sub-daily to daily), further work on the role of groundwater dynamics in DRP transport (including intra- and inter-event concentration patterns and field differences), more comprehensive methods for apportioning nutrient sources to improve estimates of soil and incidental-fertilizer loads, and further exploration of groundwater dynamics' effect on nutrient delivery to drains. \textcite{Hu2024} recommend quantifying $\delta^{18}$O$_{\mathrm{NO_3,nit}}$ in soils across a broad range of environments to understand its variability under varying thermodynamic and kinetic conditions, and coupling $\delta^{18}$O$_{\mathrm{H_2O}}$ and $\delta^{18}$O$_{\mathrm{NO_3,nit}}$ measurements in evaporating soils to quantify evaporative enrichment. \textcite{Briand2017} argue it is essential to monitor spatial heterogeneity and temporal variability of nitrate concentrations and isotopic compositions in topsoil, drains, and ditches to characterize soil's contribution to river nitrate, with temporal monitoring and multiple tracers needed to explain variations. \textcite{Lagzdins2015} highlight the need for additional research on interactions between drain flow and TN/NO$_3$-N concentration variation, and for more detailed analysis of the interaction between timing and rate of mineral fertilizer and outlet TN/TP concentrations. \textcite{Danalatos2022} ask whether near-term changes in flow-weighted nitrate concentration are predictive of long-term changes in nitrate load and yield. \textcite{Turunen2013} call for modeling and measurement studies to quantify the contribution of groundwater outflow to nutrient loading of surface waters and to study how hydrological changes are reflected in nutrient losses and applied in water-protection design. \textcite{Jokinen2024} recommend that groundwater outflow be considered in future N-loss studies and that fractions other than mineral N be considered when modeling nutrient leaching. \textcite{Barkle2021} add that accurate total-N-export estimates at sites with significant groundwater recharge require measuring all relevant N forms and accounting for the redox status of the underlying groundwater.

\subsection{Scaling, spatial targeting, and thresholds}
Translating site findings to landscapes and defining actionable thresholds constitute another set of open problems. \textcite{Bakhsh2005b} conclude that delineated zones transporting higher NO$_3$-N leaching losses require site-specific management to minimize off-site losses. \textcite{Smith2023} propose finding the NO$_3$-N threshold across a range of P saturation, waterlogging vulnerability, soil textures, organic-carbon availability, and temperature to improve the ability to restrict N and P loss, note that scaling up will require multiple techniques, and suggest combining GIS with hydrological modeling and saturation risk to assess DRP and NO$_3$-N loss from intensifying land. \textcite{Kennedy2012} argue that better understanding the coupled effect of controlling factors and drainage density in agricultural catchments is required for a complete mechanistic synthesis of stream NO$_3^-$ and Cl$^-$ dynamics, and that understanding watershed solute-export responses to increased drainage density requires more quantitative understanding of soil-water and groundwater transport via tile drains. \textcite{Salo2006} suggest identifying critical precipitation periods that create runoff, rather than using annual values, and using the resulting equations to estimate N-leaching risks in, for example, life-cycle analysis.

\subsection{Standards, policy, economics, and cross-cutting tradeoffs}
Finally, several authors point to the need for standards, economic analysis, and explicit tradeoff evaluation. \textcite{Elmi2004} note that developing drain-effluent quality guidelines for NO$_3$-N concentration offers a great challenge, and \textcite{Lagzdins2015} argue that scientifically based standards for water-quality classification in agricultural areas would help define nutrient criteria for agricultural runoff separately from rivers. \textcite{Nash2015d} recommend evaluating whether split N application with managed drainage can further reduce annual NO$_3^-$-N loss through tile drainage from claypan soils, noting it may be possible to further reduce losses from tile-drained claypan soils in the midwestern U.S. Overarching tradeoffs remain unresolved: \textcite{Derby2024} state that the tradeoff of increased NO$_3$-N losses remains unclear and requires further evaluation, and \textcite{Addab2022} likewise call for exploring the balance between crop-yield gains in tiled areas and downstream water-quality losses. \textcite{Gamble2018} emphasize unresolved tradeoffs among N-loss pathways when switching to fertigation of manure. Broader research-and-verification programs are also invoked: \textcite{BAKER1975} describe an early two-part project in which nitrogen was to be applied at 100--300 kg~ha$^{-1}$ to determine the effect of fertilization rate on nitrogen losses, illustrating the long-standing nature of these questions, while \textcite{Dattamudi2020} conclude that better N-management planning must be implemented to reduce nitrate-N contamination in tile drainage. \textcite{Fontes2019} frame the ultimate goal as providing timely, accurate information to enhance the efficiency and sustainability of food production---capturing the applied orientation that unifies this research frontier.

### Material: Long-Term Organic Soil Drainage Research Gaps

\subsection{Overview}

The literature on agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage repeatedly converges on the same meta-conclusion: many of the most policy-relevant questions about nutrient loss, water balance, and soil evolution in drained landscapes---and especially in drained organic and problem soils---cannot be resolved with the short, single-site, single-nutrient datasets that dominate the field. Across a wide geographic span, authors call for (i) \emph{longer observation periods} that span climatic variability and slow soil evolution, (ii) \emph{better metrics}, particularly phosphorus (P) saturation and sorption indices that outperform standard agronomic soil tests, (iii) \emph{isotope- and tracer-based approaches} to resolve loss pathways and source attribution, (iv) \emph{multi-scale and multi-species designs} that partition water and nutrient loads among competing pathways, and (v) \emph{mechanistic and modelling advances} that couple hydrology, biogeochemistry, and human-induced soil change. \textcite{Gramlich2018} frame the headline gap directly: longer-term studies---especially those exceeding three to four years---on drained organic soils are needed with respect to water balance and nitrogen (N) losses, along with case studies that compare drained and undrained conditions directly for N, P, and plant-protection products. This section organises the field's open questions and stated priorities around these lines of evidence, emphasising what remains uncertain, which hypotheses need testing, and which methods and data are still missing.

\subsection{The recurring call for long-term, multi-climate, multi-site observation}

The single most common recommendation is simply to keep observing for longer and across more conditions. \textcite{Kokulan2019} argue that additional longer-term field studies are needed to capture climatic variability and that continued observation is required to evaluate whether and how field hydrology and soil structure evolve into the future. Similar multi-year, multi-climate appeals recur for N-fertiliser effects on yield \parencite{Kaur2024}, for high-carbon soil mineralization and management \parencite{Waring2022}, and for developing management strategies that balance economic production with environmental protection across soil types and climatic zones \parencite{Kladivko2004}. \textcite{RANDALL1995} stress that long-term studies conducted over a range of climatic conditions are essential to derive appropriate guidelines, and that detailed plant, soil, and water measurements must be gathered together if interactions among crop production, climate, and environmental impact are to be uncovered.

Long-term designs are also invoked to detect slow or subtle change. \textcite{Kumar2014} note that because tillage and drainage differences in their system were small, long-term monitoring of crop yield is important, and that a switch from continuous corn to a corn--soybean rotation may help regain soil organic carbon over long durations. \textcite{Manninen2023} recommend keeping long-term field studies running to obtain more accurate estimates of discharge-induced organic-matter export from various soil types and cultivation practices, and to clarify the processes behind soil organic carbon and N losses. \textcite{Frison2009} extend the horizon further, asking whether observed drainage-induced soil evolution will continue or stabilise over decades and what the consequences will be for agricultural and environmental soil function; they also call for measurements along drains at different slope positions and on drainage networks of different ages. Long-term operation is itself framed as a management imperative: \textcite{Singh2007} argue that fields with subsurface drainage must continue to operate the system and keep reclaimed land under cultivation to maintain soil health on a long-term basis.

\subsubsection{Sampling standardisation and consistency}

Several authors emphasise that longer records are only useful if sampling is standardised. \textcite{Sherbine2023} recommend that soil biological indicators be evaluated over longer periods and that sampling be done at consistent times each year under similar soil moisture and temperature conditions. \textcite{Li2023b} likewise argue that the timing of sampling for soil health index (SHI) determination should be standardised to enable comparable results, practically aligning with post-harvest sampling for standard soil nutrient testing. For meta-analytic comparability, \textcite{Ghirardini2019} call for a complete hydrologic description of each plot, standardised sampling and analytical methods, monitoring periods longer than 40 days (especially for dewatered sludge), and reporting of concentrations with descriptive statistics (minimum, maximum, standard deviation).

\subsection{Phosphorus: better metrics, pathways, and loss prediction}

Phosphorus dynamics attract the densest set of open questions, spanning the adequacy of soil tests, the mechanisms of desorption and transport, and the tools needed for prediction.

\subsubsection{Beyond agronomic soil-test P: saturation and sorption metrics}

A consistent theme is that agronomic soil-test P (STP) is insufficient for environmental risk. \textcite{Sharpley1995} argue that agronomic P tests may not reliably assess eutrophication potential and that environmental tests estimating bioavailable P and P sorption capacity should be used on soils flagged as ``high risk,'' while comprehensive P-management plans address soils already containing P in excess of crop requirements. \textcite{Hooda1999} similarly propose that a measure of the degree of soil P saturation (DPS) may better estimate loss potential than extractable P such as Olsen-P, and that a full accounting of all P output forms is needed for mitigation strategies. \textcite{Nguyen2002} call for establishing critical DPS values---including for New Zealand sediments---calibrated across a range of sediment P tests and equilibrium P concentrations at net-zero sorption--desorption. \textcite{MILLER1979} adds a design-stage caution: P-adsorption capacity should be considered before organic soil areas are developed for crop production. \textcite{Osterholz2020} recommend assessing additional fields in the 100--400~mg~kg\textsuperscript{-1} STP range to clarify STP--P relationships and examining more detailed soil properties, including P sorption saturation, macropore formation and stability, and mineralogy.

\subsubsection{Mechanisms of desorption, colloidal and particulate transport}

Multiple authors identify the sorption/desorption interface and particulate pathways as poorly resolved. \textcite{Mumbi2025} call for quantifying when and how varying water sources and fluxes interact with soil to desorb and adsorb P---a need considered critical to advancing prediction of subsurface dissolved reactive P (DRP) transport---supported by data across soil types and management (e.g., long-term till vs.\ no-till). \textcite{Flores2021} note the need to understand the mechanisms controlling particulate settling and P desorption. \textcite{Nguyen2002} call for investigating P release--retention characteristics of drainage sediments under prolonged flooding and alternating waterlogged--drying conditions, and the interaction of sediment P with overlying drainage water across flooding, drying, wetting, and P-loading regimes. \textcite{Jiang2010} recommend assessing P fractions after leaching to understand transformations under polyacrylamide (PAM), including its effect on different purple-soil layers and interactions among broadcasting rate, mixing procedure, and leaching losses. \textcite{Wilson2019} highlight the need to quantify the contribution of colloidal P to DRP measurements and to determine whether erosion-mitigating BMPs or nutrient management better control it, alongside clarifying P stratification, preferential flow through tile drainage, and the effectiveness and time-to-effect of subsurface P application in stratified fields. \textcite{Norgaard2013} recommend including both dissolved and colloid-associated leaching parameters in field-scale risk assessment, testing a modified Dexter concept using clay and silt as a linking indicator, and considering water-dispersible colloids and tillage events as indicators of particle release.

\subsubsection{Multi-species reporting, temporal effects, and databases}

Fragmented reporting hampers synthesis. \textcite{Christianson2016} call for increasing the number of field-scale studies documenting drainage P losses and the effects of cropping, nutrient application, soil properties, and drainage design; including multiple P species (DRP, particulate P, total P); further developing the Drain Load database to capture peak flowrate and a drainage-concentration table; and using individual-study source data to evaluate temporal effects on drainage P transport. \textcite{Macrae2019} call for examining the hydroclimatic drivers of event responses and their impact on differing P concentrations in tile drainage, determining how tillage or P placement affects such relationships, and improving understanding of small-scale hydrologic and biogeochemical processes under various flow conditions and soil types. \textcite{Tanga2025} note that controlled-drainage effects on soluble P remain variable in the literature and recommend monitoring both particulate and dissolved forms while selecting site-specific stoplog depths based on field slope, drain slope, soil type, and climate. \textcite{Grant1996} call for further study of soil properties and P transport and for determining the applicability of in-situ optical turbidimeters to estimate particulate-matter content in drainage water. \textcite{Daigh2015b} note that subsurface-drainage total reactive P concentrations and yields in bioenergy systems need further evaluation in areas prone to higher P losses.

\subsubsection{Organic (Histosol/peat) soil phosphorus}

Phosphorus in cultivated organic soils is singled out as poorly understood. \textcite{Grenon2022} call for mechanistic studies to elucidate the pathways, hot spots, and hot moments of P transfer from agricultural organic soils to adjacent water bodies, including the dynamics of residual P fractions, soil--water P dynamics through long-term water-quality study, and P-sorption dynamics to compute more accurate P balances. \textcite{Grenon2021} propose a concrete methodological agenda: assessing P geochemistry in agricultural Histosols using isotope tracing (\textsuperscript{32}P/\textsuperscript{33}P and oxygen isotope ratios in phosphates), XANES, and \textsuperscript{31}P~NMR alongside sequential fractionation; assessing microbial turnover times; conducting long-term studies of drainage water management to reduce total P loads; developing a new mathematical model accounting for geo-mechanical instability and variability in organic-soil properties; and creating P-transport indices to predict loss. \textcite{Höyhtyä2025} note that nutrient leaching from abandoned peat fields is poorly studied, calling for research into the effects of ditch blockage, energy-willow production, or forestation, and for exploring the true nature of H\textsubscript{2}SO\textsubscript{4}-P via P K-edge XANES spectroscopy.

\subsubsection{Isotope-exchange and kinetic prediction of P loss}

\textcite{McDowell2001} propose that P isotope-exchange kinetics (IEK) has the potential to explain and eventually predict P loss to overland flow and subsurface drainage across a wide range of STP concentrations, and that with further work the IEK approach may predict P loss more accurately.

\subsection{Nitrogen: mineralization, testing, and management options}

Nitrogen questions cluster around residual and mineralizable N, testing methods, and the search for management levers beyond fertiliser rate. \textcite{Waring2022} call for measuring mineralization rates from high-carbon soils and for N-management options beyond fertilisation practices to reach water-quality reduction goals. \textcite{Bosompemaa2021} recommend further quantifying the mass of N in soil during both growing and non-growing seasons. \textcite{RANDALL1995} suggest a soil N test in the spring following dry years to measure residual nitrate and credit it against fertiliser applications, while \textcite{Conrad2009} recommend measuring mineral N to evaluate leaching potential before a new crop is sown and modifying crop rotation to prevent long periods without soil cover or N uptake. \textcite{Gentry2014} argue that more accurate quantification of fertiliser-N rates and timing would sharpen estimates of fertiliser-management influence on nitrate transport, and that the appropriateness of fall N application on tile-drained land in east-central Illinois may need revisiting in light of warmer winters. \textcite{ElGhannam2023} recommend that future studies measure not only nitrate outflow but also soil nitrate and plant N status. \textcite{Saiki2020} call for assessing the effect of water flow on soil N accumulation and discharge by evaluating N balance in paddy fields. On tooling, \textcite{Masri2024} suggest resin-membrane strip technology as a future tool to characterise soil N cycling and release under variable conditions, and \textcite{Kaur2024b} point to technology that improves the efficiency of enhanced fertiliser management in tile-drained fields. \textcite{Aide2016} frame an integrated agenda: developing effective crop-nutrient-management systems to improve uptake efficiency and reduce nitrate leaching, and refining denitrification-bioreactor design (reactor size, bed packing materials, equilibrium thresholds, elimination of preferential flow paths, and pH maintenance).

\subsubsection{Predicting long-term nitrate concentrations}

\textcite{Skaggs2012} identify a distinct methodological gap in estimating long-term flow-weighted average N concentrations for drainage waters: methods are needed for benchmark soils under typical rotations; the approximate method requires improvement for years when concentrations deviate strongly from the long-term average; and additional testing across soils, crops, and climates is needed, ideally with simple methods relating concentrations to prior yields, fertilisation, and weather patterns.

\subsection{Isotopes, tracers, and pathway partitioning}

A cross-cutting methodological priority is the use of isotopic and tracer techniques to resolve otherwise ambiguous pathways and sources. \textcite{Grenon2022} argue that more frequent post-fertilisation sampling and isotope tracers would help elucidate P dynamics in cultivated organic soils, and that applying isotopes within soil--water dynamic studies could shed new light on loss pathways. \textcite{Islam2022} propose measuring the stable isotope composition of water ($\delta$D and $\delta^{18}$O) to identify plant water sources late in the season, and \textcite{YliHalla2022} recommend verifying preliminary groundwater flow-path estimates with stable water isotopes. \textcite{Komor1997} show a source-attribution application, calling for additional $\delta^{11}$B analyses from closely spaced wells around feedlots and cultivated fields to define spatial variation in manure-leachate proportions, and for further investigation of the isotopic compositions of B sources to strengthen $\delta^{11}$B as a contaminant tracer. At the pathway-partitioning level, \textcite{Flores2021} urge future drainage studies to comment explicitly on surface-inlet presence and to partition both water and nutrient loads by pathway using before-after control-impact designs, hydrograph separation, or tracers, and to report both N and P loads for each site-year (including additional N forms such as NH\textsubscript{4} and organic N). \textcite{Kokulan2019} similarly recommend evaluating the chemistry and relative contributions of overland flow (OF) versus tile flow (TF) to edge-of-field nutrient losses. \textcite{Laubel1999} advocate combining studies at different scales---intact soil columns, experimental plots, and catchments---to examine subsurface loss of particulate matter and P in relation to macroporosity, soil P content, and cultivation practice.

\subsection{Soil biology, health indicators, and human-induced soil evolution}

\subsubsection{Biological and soil-health indicators}

The soil-biology response to changing drainage hydrology is repeatedly flagged as understudied. \textcite{Sherbine2023} call for longer-term evaluation of soil biological indicators to understand how soil biology responds to gradually changing hydrology. \textcite{Yang2025} identify the interaction mechanisms between subsurface drainage (SSD) and soil physicochemical properties, and the response of soil microbial activity, as priorities. \textcite{Kaur2024b} and \textcite{Kumar2014} call for continued research on specific soil microbial properties and microbial community structure and function to explain tillage and drainage impacts. \textcite{Li2023b} identify a scalability gap for nematode measurements, recommending DNA-sequencing methods and statistical approaches that quantitatively integrate nematodes with the SHI, and provide a basis for expanding to multiyear assessments of SHI linkages with nutrient losses and crop productivity. \textcite{Nakajima2015} argue that additional long-term studies are needed on the effects of no-till and subsurface drainage on the soil quality index (SQI), that valid, reliable, sensitive, repeatable, and accessible indicators must be identified, and that a framework for overall soil-quality evaluation and a systems approach are needed. \textcite{Singh2007} suggest that soil-enzyme activities could help identify management practices to maintain soil health. \textcite{Masri2024} argue that further research is needed to prove that no-till improves soil-health functions enough to sustainably reduce nitrate leaching, and that an extended research period is needed to relate nitrate load, water-quality parameters, and soil-health functions. \textcite{Caron2025} recommend adding dynamic to static indicators to diagnose compaction (extending to deeper layers and more corn-producing areas) and investigating whether long-term forage rotation or agroforestry can restore soil physical health, including comparative long-term economic viability.

\subsubsection{Drainage-induced soil evolution over decadal scales}

A distinctive line of inquiry treats subsurface drainage as a driver of long-term, potentially chaotic soil evolution. \textcite{Montagne2009} argue that quantifying human-induced soil evolution as a function of time should be a research priority, that further research is needed to evaluate drainage's impact on clay-mineral dissolution, and that more quantitative data as a function of soil properties are needed to model impacts on soil functions. \textcite{Montagne2010} argue that soil evolution in response to climate change must be explicitly studied and included in global climate-change models. \textcite{Montagne2013} suggest that significant material losses over a few decades make human-induced perturbation a powerful tool for studying the time-scales of such chaotic soil evolution. \textcite{HOOGHOUDT1952} note the continuation of a long experiment to determine whether soil structure will eventually be affected. \textcite{Ouellet2008} recommend using variables expressing tillage practice, surface-residue coverage, years in no-till, and soil particle size for empirical prediction (considering variable interactions), suggest soil organic matter as a crude surrogate for residue cover in regional modelling, and note that predictions of earthworm activity would likely be stronger for no-till than conventional-tillage systems.

\subsection{Drainage water management, BMPs, and policy tools}

Several authors target the effectiveness and siting of best management practices (BMPs) and drainage water management (DWM). \textcite{Wilson2019} present an extensive agenda: determining the impact of DWM on surface and tile flow of water and P; mapping the spatial distribution and range of STP values (especially elevated fields); improving understanding of P cycling at in-field, edge-of-field, and in-stream scales; developing innovative policy solutions (manure cooperatives, transport incentives, market-based strategies) and assessing farmer willingness and site-specific cost-benefit relationships; creating standardised methods to track adoption; and developing easy-to-use decision-support tools and field-scale P-loss projections to guide BMP placement. \textcite{Singh2020c} likewise call for extensive long-term, field- and watershed-scale work: measuring BMP effectiveness in site-specific areas; long-term field-scale trials of BMPs serving as P sinks; studies of adsorption/desorption with P-binding amendments; investigation of the time and pathways over which flushes of available P are lost or fixed; simulation of measured P loss from bioreactors and saturated buffers supplemented with P-adsorption materials; field evaluation of the P-index and critical-source-area concepts; long-term studies on P stratification linked to water-quality impairment; field-scale evaluation of lime, gypsum, and slow-release P fertilisers; further work on P source, rate, placement, and timing; year-round natural-rainfall runoff studies; multi-year cover-crop and tillage studies; long-term monitoring of new controlled-drainage, bioreactor, and saturated-buffer designs; better-funded watershed-scale P-flux monitoring; and criteria to maintain and remove P from BMPs before retention capacity is exceeded. \textcite{Zhang2017} add that impacts of modified no-till on DRP losses need investigation and that new soil-P testing and optimised P application timing, rates, and methods are needed to reduce DRP losses under no-till. \textcite{Nash2015c} call for determining whether an observed P-loss response between managed and free drainage was due to soil type, pH, anaerobic conditions, shallow tile placement, plant uptake, and/or rainfall. \textcite{Gottschall2012} argue that field-scale studies of environmental concentrations and loading across different soil-management systems are needed to validate predicted concentrations, elucidate dominant exposure pathways, and characterise dissipation kinetics.

\subsection{Modelling, reactive transport, and specialised soils}

\subsubsection{Model development and reactive transport}

Modelling gaps span parameterisation, coupling, and validation. \textcite{Conrad2009} recommend a range of parameter values rather than single estimates, improved understanding of organic--inorganic transformations in soil and plant (including plant growth), and statistically based automatic calibration. \textcite{Kokulan2019} note that runoff models for tiled landscapes with cracking clay soils can be improved by including temporal variability in the activation of hydrological pathways and hydroclimatic drivers of activation patterns. \textcite{Dadfar2010} plan to incorporate clay-mineralogy data into a CF algorithm as it becomes available, to better define the clay-content threshold, and to complete field validation against water-quality data from several Canadian watersheds. \textcite{KARAJEH1994c} propose extending a reactive solute-transport formulation to account for lime (CaCO\textsubscript{3}) precipitation/dissolution as a salt sink/source and for the reactivity and mobility of toxic trace elements (selenium, boron, molybdenum, arsenic) to serve irrigation, drainage, and shallow-groundwater management where salinity and trace elements matter. \textcite{Singh2020c} also call for simulating and validating measured P loss from amendment-supplemented bioreactors and saturated buffers.

\subsubsection{Acid sulfate and boreal soils}

Drained acid sulfate (AS) soils raise specialised questions. \textcite{Salo2023} recommend that future model applications consider short-term hydro-meteorological effects on sulfide oxidation under different drainage setups, and that soil macroporosity, the effect of ripening on hydraulic properties, formation of secondary minerals and weathering, and nitrate and gaseous emissions be incorporated, with the effects of moisture, temperature, and soil structure on sulfide oxidation examined. \textcite{Virtanen2017} note that the role of the N cycle in sulfide oxidation is unknown in boreal cultivated AS soils and that sulfide oxidation coupled with nitrate reduction, together with alternative neutralising agents, warrants study. \textcite{YliHalla2022} call for detailed study of the peat-cover thickness required to hinder sulfidic-material oxidation. \textcite{Åström2005} call for more detailed maps of actual and potential AS soils, more knowledge of the relative contribution of anthropogenic versus organically derived acidity in forested catchments, new environmentally friendly ways of draining actual AS soils, and cessation of drainage of potential AS soils.

\subsubsection{Saline, waterlogged, and reclaimed soils}

For reclaimed saline and waterlogged Vertisols, \textcite{Mukhopadhyay2023} and \textcite{Mukhopadhyay2023b} call for studies of how SSD integrated with crop management (inorganic fertilisers, crop residues, compost, green manure) affects soil-quality improvement and carbon-sequestration potential, of N/P/K losses and field-scale budgeting across more sites and larger Vertisol areas, of efficient recycling of drained water, and of greenhouse-gas fluxes before and after SSD installation to assess climate-mitigation potential. \textcite{ElGhannam2023} call for long-term study of controlled drainage's effect on soil salinity and for site-specific salinity-management practices to avoid salinization under long-term shallow groundwater. \textcite{Colombani2016} call for investigation of salt-crust formation and the subsequent transport of dissolved species, detailed soil-physical analysis of long-term tillage, and more sustainable practices (accurate fertiliser scheduling, frequent small irrigations after fertilisation, and buffer zones to abate excess N). \textcite{SoltaniGerdefaramarzi2020} suggest investigating leaching effects on other elements such as plant-available potassium, analysing at least three soil samples at longitudinal distances from the drain and at different depths by combined sampling, and testing organic modifiers such as animal manures to improve leaching efficiency. \textcite{Qian2021} recommend monitoring soil properties such as elemental toxicities in each year of multi-year waterlogging experiments, extending research to different crops and regions, and scheduling field drainage to account for different waterlogging forms, especially during budding, flowering, and boll-forming stages. \textcite{Bahçeci2018} recommend further field research to verify long-term resistance to root growth (especially for perennial crops) and performance under other soil, hydrological, and agricultural conditions, while \textcite{Bahceci2008} note that drainable-porosity ($\mu$) values can be determined in the laboratory, in situ, or by correlation, with more reliable values obtained when soil texture is considered. \textcite{Sadhukhan2019} call for further tests at additional locations under different fertilisation (including manure), soil, climate, and crop conditions over longer periods with more observational data. \textcite{Isa2023} call for studies of additive effectiveness (soil strength, rate, cost, impact on rice, physicochemical properties, and soil interaction), land-replacement characterisation and economic evaluation, and integration of rehabilitation methods to maximise soil strength and paddy yield.

\subsection{Other emerging topics and cross-cutting data needs}

Several findings point to under-studied constituents and processes. \textcite{Sethna2022} call for analysis of particulate silicon (amorphous and biogenic) to quantify total-Si transport and cycling, study of how warming and changing precipitation affect phytolith mineralization and dissolved-Si inputs to freshwaters, and explicit studies of nutrient limitation and uptake to quantify the role of diatoms and ambient nutrient stoichiometry. \textcite{Manninen2023} recommend that future discharge monitoring focus on variations in nutrient stoichiometry to assess the fate of organic matter in aquatic ecosystems. \textcite{Ghirardini2019} call for addressing the release of accumulated compounds during intense rain and drought/wet cycles, microbial indicators in surface runoff and tile drainage across rainfall events and soil types, and the occurrence of antibiotic-resistant genes and bacteria in sludge-amended soils, alongside comparative studies of sludge types, application rates and methods, and irrigation frequencies on different soils. \textcite{Gramlich2018} additionally call for clarifying the relevance of preferential flow in loamy and sandy soils and for defining categories of relevant compounds and soil--site characteristic combinations that create high cumulative environmental risk under intensive agriculture. \textcite{DambenieceMigliniece2018} recommend detailed fertiliser-use records to confirm crop-area/nutrient-load correlations and analysis of seasonal changes in nutrient concentrations and loads. \textcite{Norberg2025} note that long-term maintenance of soil fertility will probably require additional P and K fertilisation to preserve availability. \textcite{Kladivko2004} argue that detailed analysis of individual storm hydrographs is needed to test event-scale hypotheses and that comparing low-organic-matter soils with the high-organic-matter soils of much of the U.S.\ Midwest is essential for understanding the system and designing region-appropriate management. Collectively, these priorities define a research frontier in which longer records, richer metrics, isotopic tracing, and multi-scale, multi-species monitoring are prerequisites for the mechanistic understanding and predictive models the field still lacks.
