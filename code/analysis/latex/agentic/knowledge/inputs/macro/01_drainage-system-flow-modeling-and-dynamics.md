### Material: Subsurface Drainage Model Performance Evaluation

\subsection{Overview}

The evaluation of models used to simulate and design agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage rests on several converging lines of evidence: (i) site-scale field validations that compare simulated water-table depths, drain discharge and, increasingly, salinity and solute fluxes against monitored records; (ii) benchmark comparisons of analytical or semi-analytical drain-spacing solutions against laboratory experiments, numerical codes and one another; (iii) watershed- and regional-scale studies that test how drainage is conceptualised within distributed groundwater and integrated hydrological models; and (iv) formal sensitivity, calibration and uncertainty analyses. Across this body of work several robust conclusions emerge. The field-scale water-management model \textsc{DRAINMOD} is by far the most extensively tested and is repeatedly judged a reliable design and evaluation tool for poorly and artificially drained lands \parencite{Singh2019b,Gowda2012}. Model skill is strongly scale-dependent in time: monthly and annual drainage volumes and cumulative water balances are typically reproduced with high accuracy, whereas daily and sub-daily peak flows, low continuous dry-season discharge, and snowmelt events are the persistent weak points \parencite{Gowda2012,Dayyani2009,Morrison2014,Varvaris2018}. Saturated flow-based formulations can substantially over-predict drainage relative to vadose-zone-aware analyses, sometimes by an order of magnitude, so capillary and unsaturated-zone storage cannot be neglected \parencite{Xin2016b}. Saturated hydraulic conductivity and the method used to estimate evapotranspiration dominate model sensitivity and prediction uncertainty \parencite{NabiSichani2012,Malakshahi2020,Wang2006}. At the catchment scale, the way tile drains and subsurface heterogeneity are represented can shift the simulated drain contribution to streamflow by a factor of two or more \parencite{Karan2021,Boico2022}. This section synthesises the quantified performance, mechanisms and design implications established by these studies.

\subsection{DRAINMOD as the Benchmark Field-Scale Model}

\textsc{DRAINMOD} is described as one of the first widely adopted and most extensively used models for assessing drainage performance and simulating subsurface water management in tile-drained fields \parencite{Singh2019b}. A recurring, well-supported finding is that its process-based structure, with independently measurable inputs, allows good description of drainage hydrology across a wide range of climates when soil hydraulic properties are adequately characterised \parencite{Gowda2012}.

\subsubsection{Water Table, Drain Discharge and Temporal Scale}

Across humid and temperate sites, \textsc{DRAINMOD} reproduces water-table depth and drainage discharge with generally close agreement, but its accuracy improves markedly from the daily to the monthly and annual scale. In North Carolina, validation Nash--Sutcliffe efficiencies (EF) for water-table depth were 0.68--0.72, daily drainage EF fell to 0.49--0.73, monthly drainage EF rose to 0.77--0.87, and predicted annual drainage volumes lay within 3 cm (5\%) of observations with a two-year cumulative difference of just 1 cm (1\%) \parencite{Gowda2012}. Similar behaviour was found in Iowa, where predicted and observed monthly subsurface drainage matched closely and cumulative 1990--2003 drainage was within 2\% (WEBS\_CC) to 10\% (CANI\_CS) of measured values, with a slight tendency to over-predict in low-drainage years \parencite{Singh2006b}. Two-year evaluations in eastern Canada reported simulated annual drainage about 30 mm below observed while capturing timing and magnitude of events \parencite{Dayyani2009}. Repeated across studies is the tendency to underestimate daily peak flows even where general trends are well captured \parencite{Morrison2014,Singh1996}. Uncertainty analysis in Indiana confirmed this scale dependence: 90\% confidence-interval widths for drain flow grew from 0--0.6 cm day$^{-1}$ (daily) and 0--3.1 cm month$^{-1}$ (monthly) to 7.6--12.4 cm year$^{-1}$ (yearly), yet annual DRAINMOD predictions fell well within the bounds \parencite{Wang2006}.

\subsubsection{Performance in Cold and Semi-Arid Climates}

\textsc{DRAINMOD} has been validated well beyond its original humid setting. In cold regions of eastern Canada it accurately simulated the timing and magnitude of subsurface drainage and water-table fluctuations, including winter months and spring snowmelt, with calibration/validation $R^2$ up to 0.93 for water-table depth \parencite{Dayyani2009,Morrison2014}. Calibration substantially improved monthly performance, although surface runoff was predicted more poorly than subsurface drainage and daily peaks were underestimated, with better performance where soils were uniform than where cracking and preferential flow occurred \parencite{Morrison2014}. Under semi-arid Indian conditions, measured and predicted midspan water-table heights generally agreed (average absolute deviations of roughly 14--24 cm), though simulated water tables responded faster to rainfall and the model over-estimated depths in dry periods and under-estimated them in wet periods; the authors nonetheless judged the model satisfactory for drainage design in such climates \parencite{GUPTA1993}. On Illinois claypan soils, measured and simulated weekly cumulative SEW30 agreed with a coefficient of determination of 0.91, and subsurface drainage was shown to affect trafficability, dry days and SEW30 far more than surface drainage \parencite{MOSTAGHIMI1989}. Additional acceptable-to-good calibrations (e.g. $R^2=0.89$--0.93, NSE $=0.84$--0.89) have been reported for groundwater-table prediction in further settings \parencite{Abduljaleel2023,Awad2022b}.

\subsubsection{Documented Limitations and Failure Modes}

The literature also delineates conditions under which \textsc{DRAINMOD} fails. At an Israeli site it showed very large deviations, with water-table differences of 0.3--1.7 m in four of five replicates and reasonable agreement in only one plot; a detailed piezometric study attributed the disagreement to a river boundary condition, permeable sandy layers below drain depth, and deep plus lateral seepage exceeding 50\% of annual irrigation plus rainfall \parencite{Sinai2006}. A structural drawback is the inability to represent time-variable surface depression storage: with a fixed depression-capacity input the model assumed excess water infiltrated, producing an unrealistic water fate and a marked observed--predicted gap during a mid-summer paddy drainage period, which may compromise nutrient-fate predictions \parencite{Abduljaleel2023}. The model tends to over-predict drainage during low-drainage years \parencite{Singh2006b} and, being built around parallel-drain geometry, requires plot-by-plot calibration when adapted to landscapes without a regular drain network, where equivalent drain-spacing parameters ranged from about 1700 to 4000 cm and porous organic layers strongly influenced water tables \parencite{He2002}.

\subsubsection{Parameterisation, Pedotransfer Functions and Enhancements}

Because site-specific hydraulic data are often lacking, several studies established that pedotransfer estimation combined with automated calibration can drive \textsc{DRAINMOD} reliably. Coupling ROSETTA-derived properties with the PEST estimator produced calibrated, validated Iowa applications \parencite{Singh2006b}, and three levels of soil hydraulic parameter sets (including ROSETTA with a soil survey database) all gave acceptable annual and monthly drainage, with only subtle differences among them, although calibrated parameters that were best in calibration performed worst in validation \parencite{Qi2015}. In South Africa, van Genuchten fits to retention data ($R^2>0.90$) supported validation NSE of 0.86 (water-table depth) and 0.81 (discharge) \parencite{Malota2024,Malota2015}. Enhancements have targeted the daily-peak weakness: incorporating macropore flow into \textsc{DRAINMOD} improved daily drainage-peak prediction, captured about 75\% of 60 peak events, partitioned matrix and macropore flow (macropore flow on 98 days), and predicted cumulative drainage within 8\% (65.9 vs 71.5 cm), though surface runoff was strongly over-predicted (51.9 vs 23.7 cm) and annual budgets closed only to about 6.4 cm \parencite{Askar2020}. A neural-network surrogate (\textsc{DRAINMOD-ANN}) successfully cloned daily groundwater-table predictions across drainage layouts, with GRNN outperforming multilayer feed-forward training, offering a way to enhance performance without altering source code \parencite{Awad2022,Awad2022b}. Sensitivity to the evapotranspiration algorithm is pronounced: replacing the default Thornthwaite method with Rohwer or pan-evaporation-based estimates substantially improved water-table prediction in northern Iranian paddies, and the best ET-estimation methods were not necessarily the best for water-table simulation \parencite{Malakshahi2020}. Likewise, moving ET$_0$ computation from a daily to an hourly time step shifted the simulated water fate, increasing cumulative runoff by 4.8\% and decreasing drainage and infiltration by 3.1\% and 1\% \parencite{Awad2022b}.

\subsection{Other Field-Scale Physically Based Models}

\subsubsection{Richards-Equation and Variably Saturated Models}

The HYDRUS (2D/3D) code is consistently found capable of predicting drainage fluxes and water-table depths under contrasting subsurface systems, with strong calibration and validation skill (EF from 0.91 to 1; RMSE 0.2--6.4 cm) in Iranian paddy fields, where it is recommended for identifying optimal drainage layouts \parencite{Karandish2018,DarziNaftchali2018}. It reproduced field soil-water content in Canadian studies (accounting for 67--76\% of the variance) and clarified drainage mechanics, showing for compacted histosols that matric-potential drawdown was on average 58\% faster below than above a compact layer, so that raising saturated conductivity of that layer (by up to a factor of 50) rather than narrowing spacing or deepening drains is the effective remedy \parencite{Mante2018,Guedessou2021}. HYDRUS-2D simulated higher drainage rates than the Hooghoudt and Murashima--Ogino equations for paddies and highlighted the strong control of drain spacing, drain depth and cracks on drainage rate and midpoint head \parencite{Ebrahimian2015}. Related variably saturated codes include the CSUID 3D finite-difference model, which reproduced 1D infiltration, 2D water-table and 2D drainage benchmarks ($R^2=0.96$--0.99) and a conservative-transport analytical solution ($R^2=0.95$) while quantifying how parameter uncertainty propagates to root-zone salinity and crop yield \parencite{Alzraiee2013b,Alzraiee2013}, and Richards- or Darcy-based simulations of sheetpipe systems showing strong textural control of drawdown and cumulative seepage \parencite{Saptomo2021,Li2021}.

\subsubsection{Comparative and Multi-Model Evaluations}

Head-to-head comparisons generally find several field models perform comparably well. In the Netherlands, WAVE, SWAP and \textsc{DRAINMOD} predicted lateral drainage flux equally reliably, with \textsc{DRAINMOD} giving the most accurate discharge estimate \parencite{ElSadek2001}. \textsc{DRAINMOD} and SWACROP both predicted midspan water tables and drain outflow close to measurements and to each other in Atlantic Canada without calibration, \textsc{DRAINMOD} being slightly better, though both over-predicted outflow after dry spells \parencite{Prasher1996}. The SWAP model reproduced water-table behaviour and cumulative drain discharge in India with small absolute deviations (0.17--0.18 m water table; 7.8--39.4 mm cumulative discharge) and was endorsed as a simple hydraulic-performance tool \parencite{Verma2014}. \textsc{WaSim} predicted water-table depth well ($R^2=0.86$, NSE $=0.98$) but discharge less confidently ($R^2=0.57$) when driven by Rosetta-estimated conductivity, which compromised its use as a design tool \parencite{Malota2022}; independent Indian and South African applications nonetheless judged it a workable design and reclamation tool \parencite{Hirekhan2007,Malota2024}. Comparisons among distributed models show DRAINMOD tending to over-predict and CATHY to under-predict tile flow, both performing poorly at low observed flow but well during wet periods, with the soil layer below the drains identified as the most influential \parencite{Muma2017}. For salinity, a saturated--unsaturated ``SD'' approach outperformed \textsc{DRAINMOD-S} on water-table, drainage-salinity and groundwater-salinity dynamics, the latter systematically over-estimating short-term and under-estimating long-term salinity \parencite{Nozari2021}; \textsc{DRAINMOD-S} itself calibrated satisfactorily for water table, discharge and salinity in coastal China \parencite{Feng2018}.

\subsubsection{Erosion, Solute and Specialised Field Models}

Broader agricultural models have been adapted to tile drainage with mixed but generally acceptable results. \textsc{RZWQM}/\textsc{RZWQM2} simulated weekly drain flow moderately well (NSE 0.57--0.64) and reproduced atrazine transport to drains under different tillage systems, with macropore number and atrazine partitioning the key inputs \parencite{Malone2014,Singh1996}; a modified \textsc{RZWQM2} that let redistribution and tile drainage proceed simultaneously with rainfall improved daily drainage at Iowa and Ontario sites, matching cumulative drainage to within about 6--7\% \parencite{Xian2017}. \textsc{WEPP} enhancements improved uncalibrated drainage (daily NSE 0.27--0.47, rising to 0.59--0.73 after calibration) and reproduced storm and peak runoff before and after drainage installation \parencite{RevueltaAcosta2021,SAVABI1993}. \textsc{ADAPT} calibration reduced two-year cumulative-drainage under-prediction from 14\% to 7\% and performed reasonably in cold Minnesota conditions \parencite{Mendez2004}. \textsc{GPFARM-Range} simulated annual drainage, soil-water storage and content acceptably (drainage NSE $=0.76$) in subsurface-drained pastureland \parencite{Chen2016}. The \textsc{SIDRA} family, based on few parameters, has over three decades demonstrated predictive capacity for peak flows, midspan water tables and, when coupled (\textsc{SIDRA-RU}, \textsc{SIDRA-MAGE}), for catchment routing and solute transfer across French sites, with KGE $>0.81$ (calibration) and drain-flow-start median error of 5 days \parencite{Zimmer2023,Henine2022,LESAFFRE1988,Giraud1997}. Data-acquisition and diagnostic studies underpin these evaluations: automated logger--flowmeter systems measured drain flow to within 2\% of manual readings while resolving rapid event dynamics \parencite{Kanwar1999}, and combined pressure-plus-discharge measurement was shown to diagnose functional decline (a tenfold drop in potential vertical flow) in aged systems \parencite{Nishida2020,Gurklys2008}.

\subsection{Saturated versus Unsaturated Flow and Vertical Flow Resistance}

A central mechanistic finding is that treating drainage as purely saturated flow overstates discharge. A saturated flow-based model over-predicted seepage and drainage time by roughly an order of magnitude relative to experiment, an analytical solution and the SUTRA Richards-equation model, which agreed to within 20\% (seepage) and 40\% (drainage time); a vadose zone retaining substantial water above the phreatic surface persisted even at steady state, and performance depended strongly on capillary-fringe thickness, capillary rise, saturated conductivity and drain elevation \parencite{Xin2016b}. Consistent with this, drainage under saturated--unsaturated conditions is governed by gravitational, pressure, hysteresis, capillary-barrier and air-pressure potentials, so saturated conductivity alone is an inadequate design basis; water-holding capacity, permeability and aeration jointly control efficiency, and preferential drainage can become discontinuous and inefficient \parencite{Li2021}. Field diagnosis in Japanese rotational paddies showed that vertical flow resistance, not horizontal resistance, limits the maximum drainage rate: potential vertical flow (2.1--5.7 mm h$^{-1}$) was far below potential horizontal flow (6.1 to over 79 mm h$^{-1}$) \parencite{Nishida2020}. Accounting for vertical head loss through a modified procedure improved water-table prediction in paddy fields, differing from field profiles by 9--13\% versus 23--75\% for the conventional 2D solution \parencite{Talukolaee2022}. Neglecting unsaturated-zone buffering also severely underestimates response times: an analytical model that retained it matched a numerical model closely (steady heads within $0.6\pm2.0$ cm; real-case water levels NSE $=0.80$, discharge NSE $=0.89$), whereas ignoring the buffer distorted transients \parencite{VandeCraats2021}. Equivalent-medium and layered representations reproduce integrated outflow and mean water level well but can misrepresent internal dynamics, being least satisfactory where high unsaturated-zone conductivity permits fast vertical flow to the drains \parencite{Carlier2007}.

\subsection{Analytical and Semi-Analytical Solutions for Drain Spacing and Water-Table Dynamics}

\subsubsection{Steady-State versus Unsteady-State Spacing}

A robust result is that unsteady-state (transient) drain-spacing equations yield markedly narrower spacings than steady-state equations, and are preferred where extreme short-duration water-table depths occur in high-storage systems \parencite{OOSTERBAAN1988}. In Iranian paddies, average computed spacing was 30 m for unsteady-state versus 55 m for steady-state equations (26 m Glover--Dumm to 63 m Ernst), the unsteady solutions being judged more suitable given highly variable rainfall \parencite{DarziNaftchally2014}. Steady Hooghoudt-based estimates did not match observed spacings well in several settings \parencite{Moshayedi2020}, and among transient options the Glover--Dumm and van Schilfgaarde models were optimal for different rainfall durations \parencite{Moshayedi2020}. Design-criterion choice matters: for a 25 cm drop in 2 days a newly developed equation estimated spacing closest to the actual 50 m (3.76\% deviation), whereas for longer drainage periods the Glover equation was superior \parencite{Pali2014}. Nondimensional analyses show discharge following a common S-shaped curve against drain-spacing ratio, with the median at a spacing-to-depth ratio of 2--5 \parencite{Nédélec2010}.

\subsubsection{Evapotranspiration, Recharge and Sloping or Layered Aquifers}

Incorporating evapotranspiration and unsaturated-zone flow generally permits wider, more economical spacing. Analytical solutions accounting for ET lower the water table faster and allow spacing increases of about 9--18\% (constant $K=3$ m day$^{-1}$, ET $=8$ mm day$^{-1}$), applicable even to layered profiles \parencite{NIKAM1992,Singh2010,Upadhyaya2000}. Accounting for evaporation from bare soil widened calculated spacings by 23--74\% depending on texture (e.g. clay from 22.5 to 28 m; silty clay loam from 23 to 40 m) \parencite{AbdelFattah2005}. A spacing equation including horizontal unsaturated-zone flow above the water table gave up to 30\% wider spacing for coarse soils near an impervious layer, diminishing to under 5\% when that layer lay 3 m below drain level, and agreed with Hydrus-2D for non-submerged drains \parencite{Yousfi2014}. Bi-level drainage solutions establish that the deep drain discharges more than the shallow one, that including ET increases spacing, and that spacing is only slightly smaller than an equivalent conventional level system \parencite{Verma1998,Upadhyaya2000,Kankarej2023}. For sloping aquifers, water-table profiles become asymmetric with the divide shifting toward the lower drain, and hybrid finite-analytic solutions yield 12--17\% larger spacing than analytical solutions \parencite{Singh2010,Upadhyaya2013,Hartani2001}. Dual-depth systems always remove water more effectively than single-depth systems, with relative discharge reaching roughly 160--270\% depending on the depth of added tubes, so retrofitting intermediate drains can raise effectiveness by 50\% or more \parencite{Kirkham1997}.

\subsubsection{Layered Soils, Ponded Ditch Drainage and Benchmark Verification}

Analytical solutions for multi-layered and ponded-ditch systems have been verified against experiments, prior analytical work and MODFLOW with close agreement, establishing that ponded drainage is strongly three-dimensional near the drains and highly sensitive to directional conductivities and anisotropy \parencite{Chakrabarti2020,Sarmah2015,SHARMA1991,Afruzi2014}. A plow-sole layer greatly restricts seepage and lengthens particle travel times, which can be reduced by staggered ponding distributions and lower ditch levels \parencite{Chakrabarti2020,Sarmah2015}. The conductivity of even a thin lower layer significantly affects spacing \parencite{SHARMA1991}. Time-stepping linearisation of the Boussinesq equation was reported to outperform earlier Werner and Baumann techniques \parencite{Lin2025}, and iteration schemes for non-homogeneous soils reproduced field water-table responses adequately \parencite{CHU1977}. On slopes up to about 0.15 m m$^{-1}$, flat-land drainage theory remains applicable for interior parallel drains, with deviations appearing only at steeper slopes \parencite{FIPPS1989}. Cross-model verifications (e.g. SIDRA versus SLOP; analytical versus finite-element) generally show water-table shape-factor differences below 4\% for flat lands, with numerical instability arising for steep slopes \parencite{LORRE1994,Nédélec2010}. Critiques note, however, that a single linear-diffusion model cannot describe all stages of transient drawdown, since flow is radial near the tubes at early time and quasi-horizontal at late time \parencite{Kacimov2000}.

\subsection{Model Structure and Preferential-Flow Representation}

At field to hillslope scale, the representation of macropores and preferential flow is decisive for reproducing rapid drainage. Dual-permeability and dual-porosity models reproduced drainage hydrographs well (calibration $R^2=0.84$), the dual-permeability variant giving the best groundwater-level match ($r=0.98$) and internal flow representation, while a single-porosity model achieved acceptable accuracy with far fewer parameters; all approaches failed to match low continuous dry-season discharge \parencite{Varvaris2018}. Dual-permeability formulations enable use of low matrix conductivities while still reproducing fast measured drainage events through macropore flow, and are well suited to complex topography and structured soils \parencite{Warsta2014,Salo2017}. In clay-dominated hillslopes, evapotranspiration and macropore flow to the drains dominate the water balance while Darcian matrix flow is negligible, though models can struggle to dry the profile in late summer \parencite{Koivusalo1999}. Adding macropore flow in a MIKE SHE application did not change total drainage but improved peak-flow simulation, with the drainage time constant (best value 5.6 h) the most critical parameter \parencite{Zhou2013}. Fully coupled surface--subsurface--tile models are recommended for field-scale dynamics, with discrete pipes most relevant for drain-flow dynamics and dual-permeability meshes reserved for detailed contaminant-transport studies \parencite{DeSchepper2015}. Representing the drain network as an equivalent porous medium or via seepage nodes can reproduce outflow almost exactly even when internal heads differ, offering a practical simplification for watershed integration \parencite{DeSchepper2015,Carlier2007,Boico2022}. Simply demonstrated is that letting drainage occur simultaneously with rainfall and redistribution improves daily and peak-timing skill \parencite{Xian2017}, and that CFD analysis of corrugated-pipe geometry (outside diameter the dominant control) matched sand-tank experiments to within 6\% \parencite{Wu2022}.

\subsection{Watershed- and Regional-Scale Drainage Representation}

\subsubsection{Sensitivity of Streamflow Partitioning to Drain Conceptualisation}

How drains and subsurface heterogeneity are represented strongly controls simulated catchment behaviour. Two grid conceptualisations of the same Danish catchment produced drain contributions to streamflow of 13\% (regular grid) versus 31\% (irregular grid), with the irregular model better capturing the lowest measured groundwater--surface-water exchange fluxes despite both matching total streamflow within 0.1\% \parencite{Karan2021}. Comparing conceptual models built on seepage nodes, soil heterogeneity affected drainage-area simulations more than tile-drain conceptualisation, while the reverse held at subcatchment scale and both mattered comparably at catchment scale; seepage-node models simulated similar discharges and, being about 35\% faster, are better candidates for automated calibration and for cases where drain locations are unknown \parencite{Boico2022}. Distributed HydroGeoSphere models reproduced observed stream hydrographs and flashy heads better than MIKE SHE, which produced slowly varying heads and lower peak frequencies \parencite{DeSchepper2017}. Machine-learning work established that streamflow signatures, especially high-flow event duration, predict simulated drain fraction across 73 Danish sub-catchments (test MAE $=0.126$), and that including signatures in calibration improved fits without major trade-offs \parencite{Schneider2022}. Novel strategies that embed artificial drainage in fully distributed integrated hydrological models reproduced streamflow without site-specific calibration, outperforming a calibrated SWAT model \parencite{Rathore2024}. At watershed outlets, 3D CATHY simulations showed subsurface drainage raising cumulative outlet flow (by 3--22\% across scenarios), lowering peak and raising minimum flows, with exfiltration the dominant outlet component \parencite{Muma2016}. Watershed-scale water balances also quantify drain contributions: subsurface drainage was the dominant source (about 68\% of total drainage) in a Chinese plain irrigation district \parencite{Zheng2025b}, and drainage represented major outflow fractions of precipitation across Nordic monitored catchments \parencite{Nousiainen2015,Turunen2013}.

\subsubsection{MODFLOW-Based Regional Drainage Design}

Distributed groundwater models, principally MODFLOW, are established tools for regional drainage assessment, salinity-hazard mapping and design optimisation. Calibrated MODFLOW models matched observed heads closely (e.g. 92\% correlation; standard errors of order 0.1--0.2 m) and were used to design interceptor and additional-drain systems, quantify drawdown (average 0.53 m over three years) and predict discharge for candidate designs \parencite{Ghorbani2016,Mirlas2012,Mirlas2009,Mirlas2013,Mirlas2021,AbdElaziz2025}. Sensitivity analyses repeatedly showed these regional models to be relatively insensitive to specific storage ($\pm15\%$) and aquifer conductivity ($\pm50\%$) but responsive to the conductivity of aquitard/relative-aquiclude layers \parencite{Mirlas2012,Mirlas2009,Mirlas2013}. Water-balance analyses attributed distinct fractions of flow to drainage, evaporation and seepage and revealed that existing drains were sometimes partly ineffective, intercepting as little as 9--30\% of groundwater flow \parencite{Mirlas2009,Mirlas2012}. Simulation--optimisation coupled to MODFLOW achieved equal or better water-table control with about 20\% less pumping and fewer wells \parencite{Rana2008}, and drainage-orientation studies identified a 90$^{\circ}$ alignment to groundwater flow as optimal \parencite{AbdElaziz2025}. Coastal-aquifer applications quantified how sea-level rise advances seawater intrusion and raises drain discharge by 12.5--43.4\% under future scenarios \parencite{AbdElaty2019}. New MODFLOW subsurface-drainage (SDR) packages based on the Hooghoudt equation markedly outperform the conventional DRN package: SDR reproduced steady-state and experimental drainage (RRMSE $<5\%$; relative errors mostly $<6\%$) by calibrating only conductivity, whereas fixed DRN conductance caused large errors when layout or conductivity changed, and simulated 16.9--55.7 times more drainage water than DRN \parencite{Yang2022b,Han2025}. A further modification introducing an entrance-resistance coefficient corrected large over-predictions for synthetic-envelope pipes (NRMSE $<3.18\%$; NSE and KGE $>0.9$) \parencite{Qian2025b}. Such frameworks also quantified salt dynamics, showing regional salt-discharge increases of 38--54\% and vadose-zone salt reductions exceeding 90\% after subsurface drainage, and a critical drainage-to-irrigation ratio near 10--11\% to avoid salt accumulation \parencite{Yang2022b,Han2025}. Grid resolution matters: a 1 m grid was appropriate for the SDR package, with relative errors below 4.8\% \parencite{Han2025}. Regional nitrate-reduction mapping further showed that high-drainage areas tend to have moderate-to-low groundwater reduction because drainage bypasses reducing subsurface zones \parencite{Hojberg2017,Hansen2019}.

\subsection{Sensitivity, Calibration and Uncertainty}

Formal analyses converge on saturated hydraulic conductivity and evapotranspiration estimation as the dominant sources of sensitivity and uncertainty. \textsc{DRAINMOD} was most sensitive to vertical conductivity of the restrictive layer and lateral conductivity of the deepest soil layer, with parameter interactions revealed only when likelihood weighting was applied \parencite{Wang2006}. Conductivity estimation method produced ``tangible'' differences in simulated water-table fluctuations, the direct method giving best agreement \parencite{NabiSichani2012}, and the van Genuchten shape parameter $n$, lateral conductivity and depth to the impermeable layer ranked as the leading sensitive parameters in HYDRUS-based inverse modelling \parencite{Sedaghatdoost2018}. Inverse modelling with a genetic algorithm robustly estimated hydraulic parameters, and monitoring drainage discharge together with water-table depth was found sufficient (NSE up to 0.83--0.95) \parencite{Sedaghatdoost2018}. Uncertainty frameworks combining GLUE, extended FAST and \textsc{DRAINMOD} showed annual predictions falling within confidence bounds and exposed interaction effects invisible to one-at-a-time analysis \parencite{Wang2006}. Multi-objective sensitivity analysis of 17 hydrologic parameters found daily drainage flow and relative corn yield most sensitive to drain spacing and drain depth, with soil-temperature parameters affecting only winter water balance, underscoring the need to calibrate the most sensitive parameters carefully \parencite{Timalsina2023}. For drainage-water-recycling design, the soil-water control volume was the most influential input, guiding interface simplification \parencite{Reinhart2020}. Parameter uncertainty was shown to propagate materially into predicted root-zone salinity, water-table depth and relative crop yield, motivating probabilistic rather than deterministic design \parencite{Alzraiee2013,Alzraiee2013b}. Consistent with this, the review literature concludes that no universal model exists, that automatic calibration is preferable because manual calibration neither guarantees global optima nor supports uncertainty estimation, and that integrated multi-model use is often more practical than single-model use \parencite{Singh2019b}.

\subsection{Design Applications, Field Outcomes and Supporting Measurements}

The validated models have been applied extensively to quantify design trade-offs. Narrower spacing and shallower drains improve water-table control and aeration: SEW30 fell sharply from 3461 to 450 cm day across configurations as drains were made shallower and closer in Iranian paddies, and a 0.65 m depth with 30 m spacing provided desired wet-season control \parencite{DarziNaftchali2013,DarziNaftchally2014}. Design analyses balanced aeration against drainage volume, showing SEW30 rising and drainage volume falling with wider spacing, and mild-to-severe aeration risk at 75 m but not at 25 m spacing \parencite{Verma2014,Hirekhan2007}. Optimum spacings for trafficability and crop protection on Illinois claypan soils were 10--13 m \parencite{MOSTAGHIMI1989}, and in South Africa target mean water tables of 1.0--1.5 m required 25--40 m spacing in clay versus 55--70 m in clay loam, confirming the closer-spacing-for-lower-$K_{sat}$ design rule \parencite{Malota2015}. Shallow and controlled drainage were shown to reduce drainage volume: shallow drainage cut Iowa subsurface drainage by 8.4\% \parencite{Zhou2013,Mendez2004}, and controlled drainage starting 1 March raised groundwater tables by 19--35 cm and reduced annual outflow by 35--52 mm and nitrate losses by 15--22 kg ha$^{-1}$ across climate years \parencite{Kesicka2023}, while lateral seepage explained 96\% of the drainage reduction under controlled drainage in North Carolina, so that ignoring seepage severely over-predicts outflow \parencite{Youssef2021,Saadat2018,Österholm2015}. Climate-change simulations consistently project higher future subsurface drainage (increases of about 14--80\% depending on model and precipitation change) concentrated in winter, with implications for nitrate loss, while recommended designs still maintained relative yields above 80\% \parencite{Singh2009,Chen2016,Feng2018}. Supporting field studies established the reliability of measurement and property-estimation methods underpinning all this modelling: drain-derived hydraulic conductivity and drainable porosity agreed with independent methods \parencite{BUCKLAND1986}, Rosetta and RETC reproduced measured conductivity and retention accurately \parencite{Malota2022,Malota2024}, cover crops reduce drainage by a robust $\approx30$ mm \parencite{Meyer2019}, and long-term drainage intensity measurably alters soil physical properties, carbon accumulation and workability, with $\le12$ m spacing protecting subsoil from compaction \parencite{Omonode2025,Mante2022,Mante2018,Frison2009,Roy2020}. Controlled-drainage research has grown rapidly, and bibliometric analysis of 462 articles (2000--2022) confirms its emergence as a leading, multidisciplinary management practice for water quality in tile-drained landscapes \parencite{Kesicka2023b}. Emerging model-predictive control and drainage-water-recycling tools extend these capabilities toward real-time water-table management and anticipatory drainage-base control \parencite{Ikonen2023,Reinhart2020,VandeCraats2021,Ahmadi1995,HASEGAWA1994,GALLICHAND1992b,MCCARTHY1992,PalaciosVélez2004,Khan2007,Prathapar1996,Ghorbani2015,Skaggs2017,Singh2006,Singh1999,Gaj2022}.

### Material: Tile Drainage Influence on Streamflow Dynamics

\subsection{Overview}

Agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage exerts a pervasive and quantifiable influence on streamflow, reshaping how water is partitioned among pathways and how the resulting hydrograph behaves across both event and non-event periods. The evidence base assembled here spans edge-of-field and plot monitoring, isotopic and geochemical tracer studies, hydrograph-separation and recession analyses, physically based and analytical modeling, regional data synthesis, and a global meta-analysis, and it converges on several well-supported conclusions. First, tile drainage constitutes a major component of watershed water yield, frequently supplying on the order of 40--65\% of streamflow during much of the year, with the largest contributions during spring and other wet, low-intensity periods \parencite{King2014,Thomas2016,Schilling2019}. Second, drainage systematically raises low flows and the baseflow fraction, dampens intermediate flows, and has comparatively little effect on the very largest floods \parencite{Sloan2017,Schilling2008,Rathore2025}. Third, artificial drainage \emph{homogenizes} landscape hydrologic response, shortening and stabilizing travel times and rendering hydrologic signatures more scale-invariant, with the distinction between tiled and non-tiled behavior fading above characteristic catchment sizes of roughly 2000~km$^2$ \parencite{BolandBrien2014,DaneshYazdi2016,FoufoulaGeorgiou2015}. Fourth, runoff generation in drained fields is governed by pathway-activation thresholds tied to antecedent moisture and rainfall, with tile flow typically the dominant and most persistent export pathway \parencite{Williams2019,Williams2018,Macrae2019}. Finally, effects on flashiness, peak flows, and streamflow drought are conditional on slope, soil, storm size, and drainage intensity, so drainage can either enhance or attenuate event response depending on setting \parencite{Zhou2025,Rathore2025,Adelsperger2024}. The subsections below synthesize these lines of evidence in turn, together with the mechanistic, tracer, and modeling work that underpins them.

\subsection{Tile drainage contributions to streamflow volume}

\subsubsection{Annual and event-scale partitioning}

A consistent body of watershed-scale work establishes that tile drains route a large and measurable share of precipitation to streams. In an Ohio watershed, 21\% of precipitation (206~mm) was recovered annually through tile drainage, which accounted for 41\% of the annual and 47\% of the monthly total watershed discharge; annual discharge averaged 498~mm, or 49\% of precipitation, split into 41\% baseflow and 59\% storm-event discharge \parencite{King2014}. Equivalent-medium modeling of an Iowa watershed in 2014 attributed 97~mm of tile drainage at the outlet---13\% of precipitation and 39\% of streamflow---within an annual water balance of $P=770$~mm, $ET=522$~mm, and $Q=248$~mm ($Q/P=0.32$) \parencite{Thomas2016}. In the Boone River (Iowa), end-member mixing analysis (EMMA) and SWAT bracketed the tile contribution at roughly 46\% to 54\% of discharge, with SWAT estimating about 53.7\% of Boone River and 65.8\% of Lyons Creek annual water yield over a 30-year simulation, and EMMA yielding 31--53\% (maximum) or 19--37\% (minimum) of streamflow over six years \parencite{Schilling2019}. Snowmelt-dominated SWAT modeling in a US--Canada watershed found tile flow equal to about 16\% of annual precipitation, or roughly 37\% of water yield, while tiling reduced surface runoff and increased subsurface flow, water yield, and drainage \parencite{Rahman2014}. Field-scale export can be even more tile-dominated: in Ohio, tile discharge accounted for $83\pm20$\% of water export where both surface runoff and tile flow were measured \parencite{Williams2018}, and most discharge from an Indiana closed depression was delivered by subsurface tile drains rather than surface flow \parencite{Williams2019}.

The partitioning between tile and total flow varies systematically with hydrologic condition and drainage area. In the Iowa equivalent-medium study, tile drainage represented on average 30\% of streamflow during precipitation events but 61\% during non-event periods; event-period $Q_T/Q$ increased with drainage area (mean slope $0.10$, mean $R^2=0.83$) whereas non-event $Q_T/Q$ decreased with area (mean slope $-0.17$, mean $R^2=0.82$), and the annual $Q_T/Q$ rose log-linearly from 0.29 at 6~km$^2$ to 0.39 at 45~km$^2$ ($R^2=0.98$) \parencite{Thomas2016}. Tile-flow magnitude also increased with contributing area, with full-pipe flow more frequent in smaller edge-of-field tiles than in larger mains \parencite{King2014}. The equivalent saturated hydraulic conductivity representing this behavior scaled with drainage density, from 164~m~d$^{-1}$ for collector drainage to 530~m~d$^{-1}$ at 10~m spacing ($R^2=0.99$) \parencite{Thomas2016}.

\subsubsection{Seasonal dynamics}

Tile contributions are strongly seasonal, peaking when soils are wet and evapotranspiration is low. Tile discharge in Ohio was seasonally variable, with median monthly summer discharge (0.9~mm) far below winter (23.4~mm), spring (10.2~mm), and fall (15.6~mm) values \parencite{King2014}. In the Boone River, tile drainage dominated flow from March through June, and October--November yields were largely groundwater plus tile discharge \parencite{Schilling2019}. DRAINMOD simulations in Iowa indicated the largest drainage effect on baseflow from March to July---more than 40\% more baseflow in drained watersheds and about 60\% more in April, May, and June \parencite{Schilling2008}. Expanded subsurface drainage was projected to decrease winter monthly flows, increase late-summer and fall flows, and have only a negligible effect on the following spring's flow volumes \parencite{Rahman2014}. In cold regions, tile flow generally occurred during snowmelt, tracking the synchrony between snow-water-equivalent depletion and drainage, with a seasonal absence of tile flow from May to September \parencite{Kompanizare2024}. Tile outflow can be a small fraction of rainfall during growing seasons except in continually wet years, reflecting how water-table position relative to tile depth gates drainage: in Iowa, mean water tables in a well-drained block lay below tile depth 72\% of the time and gradients steepened during wet periods, promoting lateral flow \parencite{Logsdon2020}.

\subsection{Modification of the flow regime}

\subsubsection{Low flows and baseflow}

A robust and repeated finding is that tile drainage elevates low flows and the baseflow fraction. Modeling and monitoring in Iowa showed drained landscapes carried far higher baseflow than undrained: average annual streamflow of 208~mm with 150~mm baseflow (72\%) at 24~m tile spacing versus 201~mm streamflow with 52~mm baseflow (25\%) with no tiles; tile drainage was concluded to primarily affect the baseflow portion of the hydrograph, though a detectable stormflow signal also appeared in tile hydrographs \parencite{Schilling2008}. Ohio simulations similarly found that tiles greatly increased low flow while decreasing higher flows to a lesser extent, raised annual average daily flow by about 5\% (via reduced evapotranspiration in the drier tiled soils), and lowered the probability of the water table entering the root zone ($<0.5$~m) from about 50\% without tiles to about 5\% with tiles \parencite{Rathore2025}. Routing analysis reinforced the general pattern that tile drains increase low flows, decrease intermediate or medium flows, and have minimal effect on the largest flows \parencite{Sloan2017}. In Latvia, drainage installation raised monthly mean and minimum river discharge, with significant increases in minimum monthly discharge in April, June, July, and August; mean runoff increased by 132--186\% during summer--autumn low-water periods (June--October) and by 53--286\% in the winter low-water period, while annual mean, minimum, and maximum discharge rose by 46\%, 158\%, and 26\%, respectively \parencite{Apsite2017}. Channelized (drained) headwater streams in Ohio likewise showed greater minimum and 10th-percentile discharge and far fewer zero-discharge days (8~d~yr$^{-1}$) than natural streams (87.5~d~yr$^{-1}$), inverting part of the accepted headwater paradigm \parencite{King2009}.

\subsubsection{Peak flows and flood response}

Effects on peak flows are consistently modest for the largest events but scale- and condition-dependent for smaller ones. Routing modeling found peakflow reductions concentrated at intermediate scales (100--1{,}000~km$^2$) and smaller at the largest scales ($>10{,}000$~km$^2$); for one event, reduction fell from 26\% at Wolf Creek watershed to 11\% at the Cedar River Basin outlet, and for another from 18\% at field scale to 10\% at the basin outlet, with negligible field-scale effect on the largest floods \parencite{Sloan2017}. The spatial placement of tiled hillslopes mattered: two watersheds with identical drained fractions could respond differently, and draining headwaters produced the greatest peak reduction because the width function clustered hillslopes there \parencite{Sloan2017}. Field-scale drained and undrained scenarios in Iowa showed no statistically significant difference in mean annual peak discharge for any of six soil types, indicating that drainage chiefly alters the flow pathway rather than the largest peaks \parencite{Sloan2016}. In Ohio ATS simulations, tiles could either enhance or attenuate event peaks depending on event size and antecedent conditions---enhancement for sub-threshold or dry-condition events, reduction for larger events---and streamflow was reduced by drainage in 23 of the 25 largest events, with a decision boundary separating positive from negative peak reduction 75\% of the time \parencite{Rathore2025}. Under expanded tiling, smaller peaks became more frequent while larger peaks declined (turning point at a 3.44-yr return period, not statistically significant), and steeper rising limbs with slightly shorter time-to-peak implied that reduced large-event peaks arose from reduced flow volumes rather than altered timing \parencite{Rahman2014}. Distributed hillslope modeling similarly found that drainage tends to decrease peak flow while increasing recession volume, reproducing observed behavior even with only 21\% of the area tiled \parencite{Fonley2021}.

\subsubsection{Flashiness}

Flashiness responds to drainage in a soil- and slope-dependent manner rather than monotonically. A global meta-analysis of 464 watersheds in 13 countries found that stream flashiness (Richards--Baker Index, RBI) was similar overall regardless of tile-drainage extent, but diverged by setting: in flat ($<2$\% slope) watersheds, medium and high drainage extents had higher RBI than low extent, whereas in sloping watersheds low extent had the highest RBI; in low-infiltration soils RBI was higher in lightly drained watersheds, while in high-infiltration soils RBI was similar across classes but the baseflow index (BFI) was higher where drainage was low \parencite{Zhou2025}. Drainage extent itself was positively associated with agricultural land use and clay content and negatively with slope and depth to water table \parencite{Zhou2025}. Field-scale Iowa simulations showed drainage decreased the flashiness index for low-conductivity soils and increased it for high-conductivity soils, thereby homogenizing response across soil types, with a nonlinear, U-shaped dependence on drain spacing and an optimal spacing near 15~m for the Webster soil \parencite{Sloan2016}. Ohio modeling found overall flashiness decreased with tiles, with a nonmonotonic minimum near 50~m spacing \parencite{Rathore2025}. Across 30 Nordic and Baltic catchments, the flashiness index was negatively related to BFI and positively related to the share of agricultural area under artificial drainage and to drainage intensity; computing flashiness from hourly rather than daily discharge increased it by nearly a factor of three on average (range 1.2--4.8) \parencite{Deelstra2014,Deelstra2014b}.

\subsubsection{Streamflow drought and long-term trends}

At the regime scale, tile drainage reshapes streamflow-drought characteristics. Across US watersheds, drainage significantly increased streamflow drought duration and intensity while reducing drought frequency, with effects comparable in magnitude to water-table depth and precipitation seasonality; each 10\% increase in tile-drained area lengthened drought duration by 0.03~d, raised intensity by 12\%, and lowered frequency by 0.10 events~yr$^{-1}$, and the result was robust to $\pm25$\% variation in the drained-area estimate \parencite{Adelsperger2024}. Regional trend analysis in Indiana found increasing low- and medium-flow trends, whereas high-flow trends were less linked to precipitation in heavily drained northern Indiana; accounting for autocorrelation and long-term persistence sharply reduced the count of significant low- and medium-flow trends, and the weakened precipitation linkage in drained areas suggested a drainage influence on streamflow trends warranting further study \parencite{Kumar2009}. A synthesis of seven Midwestern rivers concluded that inter-annual variability in streamflow, baseflow, and nitrogen load was controlled primarily by current- and previous-year precipitation rather than land cover or fertilizer, with adjusted $R^2$ up to 0.77 for baseflow in the Minnesota River, implying that recent river N-load increases are largely climate-driven and that tile-drained water will require remediation to reduce loads \parencite{Baeumler2020}.

\subsection{Scale dependence and hydrologic homogenization}

A central, well-supported theme is that artificial drainage homogenizes catchment response and reduces the scale dependence of hydrologic signatures. Data synthesis across Iowa showed that non-tiled watersheds exhibit decreasing area-normalized peak discharge and increasing baseflow ratio with scale, whereas tiled basins behave scale-invariantly; within-basin variability in recession coefficients declines as drained fraction rises, flow-duration curves show greater variability in non-tiled than tiled regions, and the tiled/non-tiled distinction disappears above roughly 2200~km$^2$, even though mean annual runoff ratio does not vary significantly with tiling \parencite{BolandBrien2014}. Travel-time analysis in Minnesota's Redwood basin found time-averaged mean travel time fell 41\%---from 77~d before land-use change to 45~d after, with the largest reduction in the growing season---while year-to-year variability was substantially reduced and travel time became independent of catchment size above about 200~km$^2$, indicating a more homogenized response under extensive tiling \parencite{DaneshYazdi2016}. Companion analysis showed streamflow dynamics became more linear after 1976 even though precipitation dynamics changed little, with strengthened dependence of streamflow on previous-day rain and more punctuated falling limbs \parencite{FoufoulaGeorgiou2015}. Isotope and transit-time work in a managed UK catchment found short mean transit times ($<1$~yr) with even faster artificial drains, and concluded that management---particularly artificial drainage---homogenized distributed catchment responses despite variation in subcatchment characteristics \parencite{DimitrovaPetrova2020}. In an Indiana tile-drained watershed, subwatersheds shared very similar spatial distributions of available water storage and hydrograph shape, so the basin behaved as an effectively homogeneous unit describable by a single exponential travel-time distribution with a 2--3~d mean \parencite{Basu2010}. Even so, tile contributions to annual discharge remained detectable as basins grew---beyond 16{,}000~km$^2$ in the Boone River---while diminishing at the largest scales \parencite{Schilling2019}.

\subsection{Runoff generation, flow pathways, and thresholds}

\subsubsection{Pathway activation and thresholds}

Runoff generation in drained fields is governed by thresholds linking antecedent moisture and rainfall to pathway activation. In an Indiana closed depression monitored over 289 rainfall events, rainfall amount alone related positively and linearly to cumulative flow with no visible threshold, but a clear threshold emerged when antecedent soil-moisture deficit (SMD) and rainfall were summed; thresholds varied by pathway and landscape position and were nearer zero at shoulder-slope than footslope sensors \parencite{Williams2019}. Subsurface tile flow generally began earlier, peaked earlier, and lasted longer than surface flow, and connected localized zones of saturation to enable rapid connectivity \parencite{Williams2019}. Isotopic work at the same site showed tile-drain celerity ($Q/P$) was largely controlled by antecedent wetness, exhibiting threshold behavior in SMD-plus-rainfall and in maximum groundwater depth relative to tile depth, with preferential flow more important under dry conditions and stored pre-event water dominating when wetter \parencite{Williams2021}. In near-level vertisolic clay in Canada, overland flow generally activated before groundwater and, in the tiled field, before tile flow in 14 of 18 events; tile-flow activation time related significantly to maximum rainfall intensity ($r^2=0.68$) and antecedent precipitation ($r^2=0.57$), improving to $r^2=0.78$ combined, and direct preferential flow into tiles was uncommon, implying a largely top-down sequence and tiles somewhat decoupled from the surface \parencite{Kokulan2019}.

Across US and Canadian sites, surface runoff was less frequent than tile flow: of 795 events, 43\% produced both surface and tile flow and 57\% produced tile flow only, and among dual-pathway events surface flow contributed a median of 12\% of total flow (range $<$1--97\%) \parencite{Macrae2019}. Surface-runoff frequency varied by texture and region (34--54\% of events on clay; 24--32\% at Ontario silt-loam sites; 48--59\% at Ohio silt-loam sites; 29\% at the clay-loam site), and event-response types (Tile, Surface, Simultaneous, Saturation) partitioned systematically by soil texture and season \parencite{Macrae2019}. In Kansas tile-outlet-terrace systems, baseflow contributed 38\% of discharge at one site, 51\% at another, and 46\% at a third, with short events dominated by quickflow and long events carrying larger baseflow components; antecedent conditions and storm size were stronger drivers of hydrologic and biogeochemical response than tile-inlet density alone \parencite{Stops2022}.

\subsubsection{Preferential versus matrix flow}

Tracer and geochemical studies partition tile flow into fast preferential and slower matrix components whose balance shifts with season and wetness. In a French field, seasonal runoff generation differed: under dry conditions rainwater transferred directly to tile drains through macropores, whereas under saturated conditions pre-event soil water dominated and rainfall contributed up to 25\% of tile-drain water; surface and tile drainage transferred 44\% and 20\% of annual effective rainfall, respectively, and 65\% of infiltrating water was not captured by tiles \parencite{Gaillot2023}. Catchment-scale monitoring there identified two soil-column transfer pathways---slow drainage of saturated soils and fast preferential flow through macropores/cracks---with the fast peak contributing about 15\% of water and sediment fluxes on average \parencite{Grangeon2021}. In Ohio, master-recession analysis distinguished two reservoirs (recession coefficients 0.9 and 0.25~d$^{-1}$; Reservoir~1 accounting for 54\% of subsurface flow), and a coupled pathway-connectivity separation apportioned annual tile discharge into 9\% quick-old, 39\% quick-new, 42\% slow-old, and 10\% slow-new, showing that new water routes through both quick- and slow-flow reservoirs and that groundwater recharge contributes importantly to tile fluxes \parencite{Nazari2021}. In Canada, preferential flow estimated from temperature and electrical conductivity agreed strongly ($r=0.83$), accounted for a higher fraction of tile flow in clay than loam soil, and correlated with phosphorus loads, indicating a disproportionate role in P export \parencite{Pluer2020}. Swedish drain-outflow modeling attributed autumn flow events to preferential flow and spring events to matrix flow under saturated conditions, with DRAINMOD-predicted cumulative infiltration explaining most variation in phosphorus concentrations \parencite{Salazar2011}. Isotopic hydrograph separation in Indiana showed groundwater was the primary source to the tile drain, followed by soil water, with minimal direct precipitation contribution and mean transit time increasing with depth (12--20~d at 10~cm to 245~d for tile drainage) \parencite{Williams2021}.

\subsubsection{Temporal concentration of flow and export}

Tile and surface flow are highly intermittent and temporally concentrated. In Ohio fields, surface runoff occurred on average $41\pm42$~d~yr$^{-1}$ and tile drainage $240\pm69$~d~yr$^{-1}$; 80\% of annual surface runoff occurred in 2.5\% of the year (9~d) and 80\% of tile discharge in 21\% (77~d), with Gini coefficients of 0.97--0.98 (surface) and 0.79--0.88 (tile) including zero-flow days \parencite{Williams2018}. Nutrient loads were similarly concentrated, and flow-duration/Lorenz analysis showed a 30\% cut in annual tile NO$_3$-N load would require treating tile flow 83\% of the time if targeting low flows but only 3\% if targeting high flows---a framework for sizing conservation practices to short windows of opportunity \parencite{Williams2018}. Managed-drainage monitoring reinforced this concentration: over a study period 287~mm of tile discharge (40.3\% of precipitation) occurred, 94\% of it when the outlet valve was open, and event discharge correlated strongly with the fraction of the event the valve was open (Pearson $r=0.86$) \parencite{Miller2022}.

\subsection{Travel times, water age, and groundwater interactions}

Modeling and tracer studies quantify how drainage shortens groundwater travel times and reorganizes the subsurface. In Iowa, groundwater travel-time distributions remained exponential across simulated drainage densities and incision depths; holding incision at 1.2~m, raising density from 0 to 0.0038~m$^{-1}$ shortened mean travel time and raised the tile contribution to baseflow to an upper bound of 37\% ($R^2=0.98$), while holding density constant and increasing incision from 0.3 to 2.7~m lowered mean travel time near-linearly (slope 1.45~yr~m$^{-1}$) and raised tile contribution from 30\% to 54\% \parencite{Schilling2012}. In Bear Creek, mean groundwater travel time fell from 5.57~yr at low drainage intensity to 1.07~yr at high intensity, far below the presettlement value of 181.6~yr, and intensive drainage short-circuited flow to riparian buffers---reducing land draining to buffers and cutting mean buffer residence time to 36.7~d or 15.5~d depending on the drainage-initiation scenario---thereby lowering buffer effectiveness for nitrate reduction \parencite{Schilling2015}. Analytical solutions confirm the mechanism: under tile drainage, groundwater levels are lower and decline faster, total groundwater discharge to the river increases, and the tile contribution to total discharge grows with tile density, though direct groundwater discharge to the river during recession is reduced because flow is diverted to the tiles \parencite{Liang2015}. Recession behavior of heavily tiled watersheds is correspondingly more linear than that of less-tiled watersheds \parencite{Schilling2008}, and differences in master recession curves were proposed as a diagnostic of tile contribution \parencite{Schilling2008}. Classical analytical treatments of transient recharge (Boussinesq solutions) provide the theoretical basis for such water-table and drain-flow dynamics \parencite{SINGH1980,Bouarfa2000}. Coexisting hydrograph-separation methods (SC-EMMA versus BFI-type baseflow programs) can nonetheless diverge substantially---bi-annual slowflow and baseflow indices differed by 5\% to 55\% across streams---underscoring method sensitivity when quantifying tile-influenced baseflow \parencite{Kronholm2015}.

\subsection{Controlled and managed drainage}

Controlled and managed drainage strongly modulate outflow volume and timing. In Sweden, controlled drainage reduced total drain outflow by 73--95\% relative to conventional drainage (e.g., 36, 14, and 120~mm versus 172, 240, and 450~mm over three years), concentrated more than 60\% of daily outflow in the 0--0.5~mm class, and produced lower retention constants and near-zero final storage; reduced storage was linked to higher peak flow, shorter lag, and shorter recession, effects that grew with groundwater level \parencite{Wesström2003}. Raising the drainage outlet significantly slowed water-table recession---by 29\% and 62\% in two site pairs---and lengthened the time for the water table to recede to 30 and 60~cm depths, whereas free-draining plots showed no such difference, confirming the effect was not seasonal \parencite{Saadat2017}. Agricultural drainage water management in Ohio was most effective at retaining water and reducing tile discharge during low-intensity events and dry antecedent conditions; the outlet valve was open on average 18.6\% of each three-day event yet passed 96\% of tile discharge, though average groundwater, soil moisture, and surface-flooding indicators did not differ significantly from free drainage \parencite{Miller2022}. In the Netherlands, subsurface water-infiltration systems significantly influenced water-table depth, reducing fluctuations at four of five sites and raising or deepening average water tables depending on applied water level and drainage base, with quadratic and delayed responses at several sites \parencite{vandeCraats2025}. Analyses of controlled-drainage and unreplicated watershed experiments highlight that method choice matters: paired-watershed, BACIP, beta/gamma-distribution, and time-series approaches can yield differing significance conclusions, with a beta distribution deemed most appropriate in one Minnesota dataset \parencite{Pagliari2021}. Cross-drain spacing further tunes recession, being most effective at 20~m spacing (reducing water-table recession by 13--32\%) and negligible by 40~m \parencite{Vaiciukynas2015}. Comparisons of drainage installation methods under Nordic conditions likewise revealed detectable performance differences: trenchless (T0) sections held groundwater levels 0.14--0.25~m higher than trench-installed (T1) sections in 60--90\% of observations, with the clearest differences when water levels rose above the 1.0~m drain depth and during autumn and winter \parencite{Salo2019}.

\subsection{Drain hydraulics, pressurization, and system design}

At the pipe scale, network pressurization introduces storage and delayed secondary peaks. In France, pressurization of the collector network during intense rainfall limited and sometimes reversed drainage discharge, temporarily storing infiltrated water as the water table rose and generating a four-stage sequence (first peak, backflow, second peak, recession) that increased the lag to the catchment outlet to 10--25~h \parencite{Henine2010}. A coupled groundwater--pipe model reproduced this behavior, with secondary peaks about 11--30~h after rainfall ended and estimated storage of 11--51~m$^3$ (38--46\% of stored recharge), the downstream network being most affected \parencite{Henine2014}. Drain flow rates were shown analytically to be governed by the system parameter $\sigma = K/(f^2 L^2)$, with maximum drain flow reaching 0.56~mm~h$^{-1}$ for $\sigma=10$~m$^{-1}$~h$^{-1}$ versus 0.025~mm~h$^{-1}$ for $\sigma=0.01$~m$^{-1}$~h$^{-1}$, and the constant-water-table-shape approximation improving as $\sigma$ increased \parencite{Bouarfa2000}. Combined tile/mole systems modeled with SEEP/W reproduced observed discharge well (e.g., $R^2=0.811$, index of agreement 0.94 in validation); a tile-only system was least effective, reducing drain discharge by 63\% and mean water-table depth by 72\% relative to the best combined systems, whose performance reflected the hydraulic conductivity above the mole channel \parencite{Tuohy2018}. Spatial patterns of drain flow were driven by topography and geology, with higher drain fractions in local depressions and amplified flow where hydraulic conductivity was higher \parencite{Mahmood2023}.

Component- and material-scale studies address inlets, media, and specialized drainage hardware relevant to flow and sediment. Surface-water-management structures such as tile risers and blind inlets altered flow duration and cumulative flow without necessarily changing discharge frequency, with direction of effect depending on the field, and blind-inlet infiltration declined from about 12--13 to 4--5~cm~h$^{-1}$ over six years, implying an 8--10-year service life under repeated tillage \parencite{Williams2020,Williams2019}. Replacing tile risers or open inlets with blind or gravel inlets substantially reduced sediment and phosphorus losses (e.g., TSS of 14.4 versus 40.4~kg~ha$^{-1}$~event$^{-1}$) \parencite{Feyereisen2015b}, and among alternative inlet designs a clear trade-off emerged between high flow capacity and sediment control \parencite{Li2017b}. In recycled concrete aggregate drainage media, permissible flow was strongly reduced by fines content (up to $\sim$73\% flow decline at 4\% fines), and filter-fabric opening size governed clogging potential \parencite{McCulloch2017}. Sheet-pipe subsurface drainage matched conventional pipe capacity and drained roughly 45\% more water with 55\% lower percolation, lowering water tables and improving aeration and rice yield \parencite{Tamura2021,Arif2021}. Numerical experiments on distribution pipes in saturated buffers showed two pipes could raise 10-day cumulative infiltration by up to $\sim$133\% relative to a single field-edge pipe, though at the cost of shorter subsurface travel distances \parencite{Jaynes2019b}. Deep-percolation and recharge estimation beneath drained lands, while uncertain, has long been recognized as part of the drainage water balance \parencite{BOOKMAN1959}.

\subsection{Coupled sediment, solute, and contaminant dynamics}

Because streamflow pathways carry material, hydrologic partitioning shapes sediment and solute export. In dual-drainage French fields, mean suspended-sediment yield was 0.79~t~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ with strong inter-annual variability (0.49 to 1.08~t~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$); 89\% originated from surface drainage, and tile drainage contributed 46\% and 21\% of runoff but only 9\% and 11\% of sediment yield in the two years, with most export during the intense drainage period \parencite{Gaillot2023b,Grangeon2021}. A spatially distributed sediment-connectivity index captured surface source, pathway, and sink areas well but did not adequately represent connectivity where subsurface tile drainage controlled vertical flow \parencite{Calsamiglia2018}. Solute behavior in tile systems tended toward chemostatic transport, with antecedent conditions and storm size---not inlet density---dominating the response \parencite{Stops2022}. Tile drainage also delivered elevated concentrations of antibiotic-resistance genes relative to surface water, with concentrations correlated to daily flow rate \parencite{Rieke2018}. Converting surface runoff to subsurface drainage did not necessarily improve water quality: an infiltration-type raingarden retained 61\% of flow and 76--81\% of pollutants, whereas a drainage-type raingarden retained only 36\% of flow and could export more phosphorus and nitrate than it received, indicating that short-circuiting runoff through subsurface pipes can lower pollutant removal \parencite{Xu2022}.

\subsection{Specialized applications: salinity, bank stability, and valley-bottom drainage}

Beyond humid croplands, subsurface drainage governs streamflow-relevant processes in salinized, erosion-prone, and valley-bottom settings. In saline Chinese soils, subsurface drains lowered surface electrical conductivity over time and suppressed resalination better than undrained controls---daily resalination rates of 0.19--1.55\% under drainage versus 1.82\% for the control at one day after irrigation---while remaining economically favorable \parencite{Yan2013,Chang2012}. Passive drains lowering the water table stabilized gully banks in Ethiopia, cutting cumulative bank retreat by 52--84\% and soil loss by about 70\% at low cost, with a drained-bank groundwater table about 20\% lower than controls \parencite{Zegeye2021}. In a Burkina Faso valley bottom, under-phreatic permeability increased downstream from about $0.10\pm0.10$ to more than $20\pm10$~cm~h$^{-1}$, so no single infiltration process described the whole toposequence---an important consideration for efficient drainage design \parencite{Keita2014}.

\subsection{Modeling tile-drainage streamflow dynamics}

The synthesis rests on a spectrum of models whose consolidated performance is itself an established result. Equivalent-medium representations reproduced explicit tile simulations within 2\% of drained-volume targets and provided calibrated equivalent properties across tile densities for watershed-scale application \parencite{Thomas2016}. Physically based cold-region modeling captured tile timing, peak magnitude, and the seasonal absence of summer flow, improving when hourly steps and capillary rise were represented, and showed the capillary fringe strongly controlled drainable water and tile flow \parencite{Kompanizare2024}. A modified ODE hillslope formulation improved watershed-scale recession and flood-threshold timing specifically at artificially drained gauges, providing generalized seepage--storage relationships with physically interpretable thresholds \parencite{Fonley2021}. SWAT was rigorously tested against daily tile flow (NSE~$=0.5$, PBIAS~$=-1.4$\%) and combined with response analysis to assess basin-scale drainage impacts \parencite{Rahman2014}. A calibration-free time-expansion (travel-time) model matched calibrated SWAT skill in an intensely drained Indiana watershed using a single exponential distribution \parencite{Basu2010}, and multiple travel-time modeling approaches of differing sophistication converged closely in Bear Creek \parencite{Schilling2015}. Coupled SC-EMMA and hydrograph-recession frameworks using inexpensive sensors improved prediction of dissolved reactive phosphorus relative to any single method \parencite{Nazari2021}, and coupled groundwater--pipe models successfully represented pressurization, temporary retention, and flooded-outlet recovery beyond design capacity \parencite{Henine2014}. Collectively, these tools demonstrate that the streamflow signature of tile drainage---elevated low flows and baseflow, damped intermediate flows, modest large-flood effects, shortened and homogenized travel times, and scale-dependent flashiness and peak response---is both mechanistically explicable and reproducible across observational and modeling lines of evidence.

### Material: Model Performance Evaluation and Comparison

\subsection{Overview}

Evaluating and comparing the accuracy of predictive tools is a recurring
requirement across the tile-drainage literature, spanning physically based
drainage simulators, analytical water-table equations, statistical and
machine-learning surrogates, geostatistical interpolators, remote-sensing
classifiers, and field measurement or sampling protocols. The evidence base
assembled here converges on several well-supported conclusions. First, the
choice of goodness-of-fit metric or objective function materially changes the
apparent performance and even the ranking of competing models, so metric
selection is itself a substantive modelling decision \parencite{Bruhn2025,STONE1995,SAVABI1995}.
Second, established physically based and analytical models---DRAINMOD, SIDRA-RU,
HYDRUS-2D, and generalisations of the Glover--Dumm and Boussinesq equations---reproduce
observed drainage discharge, water-table profiles, and effluent chemistry with
accuracy that is generally good to excellent once soil hydraulic parameters are
appropriately estimated \parencite{Salazar2008,Jeantet2021,Naderi2025,Mehdinejadiani2013,Mehdinejadiani2020}.
Third, data-driven methods---artificial neural networks (ANNs), random forests
(RF), and deep-learning surrogates---frequently match or outperform conventional
models for the specific outputs they are trained on, while requiring less input
data and computation \parencite{Sarangi2006,Yang2000,Djiemon2019,Ellafi2021,Lei2026}.
Fourth, remote sensing combined with deep learning now maps tile-drained extent
at regional to national scales with accuracies commonly exceeding 85--96\% and
totals within a few percent of agricultural-census estimates \parencite{Valayamkunnath2020,Wan2024,Redoloza2023}.
Fifth, geostatistical (kriging-family) methods deliver reliable, low-bias
estimates of spatially variable soil properties for drainage design and can
reduce sampling effort substantially \parencite{GALLICHAND1991,GALLICHAND1992,Moustafa1998b}.
The following subsections synthesise the quantified outcomes underpinning each
of these lines of evidence.

\subsection{Statistical Metrics and Objective Functions for Model Evaluation}

A central methodological finding is that different performance measures (PMs)
rank models differently, and that this behaviour is systematic rather than
incidental. Across all 14 field datasets analysed by \textcite{Bruhn2025} in
Illinois, the Kling--Gupta efficiency (KGE) consistently outperformed the
Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE): mismatches between KGE-derived outputs were
always smaller than those between NSE-derived outputs, regardless of the
reference output used, and the same overall PM behaviours recurred across every
dataset, which the authors present as evidence that the conclusions generalise
to field-scale tile-drainage modelling. The heteroscedastic maximum-likelihood
estimator (HMLE) ranked between KGE and NSE overall, but its standing depended
strongly on the underlying error structure: it performed best under scenarios~1
and~2 and worst under scenarios~5 and~6, and in scenario~6 a ranking reversal
occurred for a subset of 4 of 14 datasets (29\%), where HMLE fell to rank~3
while NSE held rank~2 \parencite{Bruhn2025}. In an accompanying DRAINMOD case
study the residuals of neither simulated output showed increasing variance,
leading to the conclusion that KGE would be the best objective function and HMLE
the worst for that situation \parencite{Bruhn2025}. That work also introduced a
quantitative ranking system to summarise PM performance across scenarios and
released open-source HMLE implementations in both R and Python
\parencite{Bruhn2025}.

The limitations of correlation-based statistics are underscored by a paired
discussion of storm-runoff model testing \parencite{STONE1995,SAVABI1995}. The
example simulations grossly overpredicted all 10 events, yet this
unacceptability was not reflected by $R^2$ or the standard error $S_e$; a
regression-based test instead found the intercept significantly different from
zero and the slope significantly different from one, so the model was deemed
unacceptable, and the two null hypotheses were rejected because both
$t$-statistics exceeded 5.041 \parencite{STONE1995,SAVABI1995}. The
corresponding RMSE and mean bias error (24.311~mm and 20.640~mm) were said to
better reflect the high overall differences between simulated and measured
runoff than the correlation measures \parencite{STONE1995,SAVABI1995}.
\textcite{SAVABI1995} note that regression analysis---including tests for
intercept~$=0$ and slope~$=1$ together with the $R^2$ and $S_e$ tests---is a
valid goodness-of-fit approach when the discrepancy between measured and
predicted values is random, and that it can indicate whether model bias is
random or systematic. The interplay of tabulated versus fitted metrics also
appears in curve-number estimation for 12 tile-drained Ohio fields, where
correlation coefficients ranged from 0.29 for the tabular method to 0.55--0.56
for the complacent--violent and $P/S>0.46$ methods, NSE ranged from $-0.26$ for
the maximum-potential-retention method to 0.55 for the complacent--violent
method, and model bias ranged from $-0.05$~mm to $-1.17$~mm; the
complacent--violent method gave the highest correlation and NSE and the best
predictions of the largest outlier events \parencite{Wessel2022}.

\subsection{Performance of Physically Based and Analytical Drainage Models}

\subsubsection{Field-scale water-balance and effluent simulators}

DRAINMOD, evaluated over four years of experiments in south-east Sweden,
achieved good monthly agreement between observed and simulated drainage rates
across five soil-information datasets (H0--H4) \parencite{Salazar2008}. Monthly
mean absolute error (MAE) ranged from 0.57--0.82~cm for conventional drainage
(CD), 0.38--0.41~cm for controlled drainage CWT1, and 0.15--0.22~cm for CWT2;
the modified coefficient of efficiency ($E'$) reached 0.62--0.74 (CD),
0.72--0.74 (CWT1), and 0.79--0.86 (CWT2); and the modified index of agreement
($d'$) reached 0.80--0.93 across treatments \parencite{Salazar2008}. Notably,
substituting ROSETTA-estimated $K_s$ for laboratory-measured values changed the
overall normalised error by less than 3\% in evapotranspiration, less than 1\%
in infiltration, and less than 15\% in drainage; controlled-drainage errors in
predicted drainage were generally under 2\% (except H3 under CWT2 at 15\%),
supporting the conclusion that ROSETTA-estimated $K_s$ can drive DRAINMOD as
accurately as measured values, at least in coarse-textured soils
\parencite{Salazar2008}.

The SIDRA-RU model likewise simulates drainage discharge accurately, especially
on silty soils, with slightly weaker but still acceptable performance on clayey
soils \parencite{Jeantet2021}. For 21 of 22 reference plots the calibration
KGE$'$ exceeded 0.5, spanning 0.54--0.83 on silty soils, 0.54--0.76 on
silty-clay soils, and 0.44--0.76 on clayey soils; annual drained water balances
lay close to the 1:1 line with $R^2>0.9$ for all three textures; low- and
mean-flow biases were near zero while high-flow biases were larger, especially
on clayey soils; and a split-sample test confirmed temporal robustness at the
national scale, enabling long-term impact studies \parencite{Jeantet2021}.
HYDRUS-2D reproduced observed nitrate concentrations in drainage water very well
under both mid-season drainage (MSD) and alternate wetting and drying (AWD) in
Iran, with RMSE of 0.25--0.99~mg~L$^{-1}$, normalised RMSE of 5.3--19.1\%,
normalised mean bias error of $-2.14$ to 10.47\%, and modelling efficiency of
0.6--1.0, with only a slight tendency to overestimate \parencite{Naderi2025}.
The calibrated model estimated the grey water footprint (GWF) far more
consistently with field-based estimates than the tier-1 method: HYDRUS-based GWF
values differed from the field-based method by only 7.0--11.9\% (MSD) and
$-9.4$ to 8.2\% (AWD), whereas the tier-1 method reached relative errors up to
3960.7\%; AWD raised rice yield by about 39.6\% but increased GWF roughly
1042\% relative to MSD \parencite{Naderi2025}.

\subsubsection{Analytical water-table and drain-flow equations}

Generalisations of classical drainage theory have been repeatedly validated
against homogeneous and heterogeneous soils. A space-fractional Boussinesq
formulation reduces to the Glover--Dumm model when the heterogeneity index
approaches 2, so in homogeneous soil the fractional and Glover--Dumm solutions
were nearly coincident with minor error, whereas in a very heterogeneous
experimental field soil (heterogeneity index 1.04) the fractional model tracked
observed water-table profiles more closely and outperformed Glover--Dumm
\parencite{Mehdinejadiani2013}. Extending this to parabolic and elliptical
initial profiles, \textcite{Mehdinejadiani2020} found all fractional models more
sensitive to the heterogeneity parameter $\alpha$ than to $\kappa_x/\mu$;
estimated $\alpha$ was near 2 for relatively homogeneous fine sand but around
1.3 and 1.1 for sandy clay loam and field soils, and the elliptical (SFBE-E) and
first-parabolic--type (SFBE-F) forms generally fit best while SFBE-P2 fit worst,
with SFBE-E substantially improving on its classical counterpart in
heterogeneous soils. A Bees-Algorithm inverse model (DIM) then provided
repeatable, reliable estimates of $\kappa$ (variance across ten runs
$\approx0$), and drainage equations using DIM-derived $\kappa$ gave the best
simulation performance across laboratory and field datasets, outperforming the
Glover--Dumm criterion (GDC), Skaggs's drainage criterion, the drainage-discharge
method, and the direct method; in a worst case at $t=110$~min the global
performance index improved almost 34-fold over the GDC and almost 9-fold over
Skaggs's criterion \parencite{Mehdinejadiani2022}. Complementing these,
\textcite{Samani2007} showed that using water-table-profile variation over time
within an inverse-problem framework let all analytical models agree well with
measured data, that treating effective porosity as a variable improved precision
in every case, that the Topp and Moody model was the most accurate, and that
calibrated $K$ and $f$ values yielded much better water-table predictions than
measured values while remaining close to them. A Green-element method (GEM)
solution of the nonlinear governing equation resolved slope effects efficiently,
matching or bettering earlier analytical results for most practical cases: for
Test~2 GEM gave a maximum error of 2.5\% at 30\% slope, and it predicted better
than Childs and Pavlovsky for a $-15$\% slope, though errors grew as slope
exceeded 0.60 \parencite{Onyejekwe1999}. Field determination of drainage
parameters is also feasible: a Turkish site yielded drainable pore space of
7.0--7.2\%, a mean drainage reaction factor of 0.599~day$^{-1}$, and
transmissivity $K_d$ near 6.7~m$^2$~day$^{-1}$, in agreement with reference
values for medium-textured (silt loam) soils, with the caveat of large
differences among published reference limits \parencite{Bahceci2008}.

\subsubsection{Comparisons of process models with data-driven surrogates}

Head-to-head comparisons show that the best model depends on the target
variable. For an Indian subsurface-drained field, a back-propagation neural
network (BPNN) with 12 hidden neurons and a learning rate of 0.02 predicted
drainage-effluent salinity more accurately than SALTMOD at both 35~m and 55~m
drain spacings, with smaller deviations, but SALTMOD was superior for root-zone
soil salinity because the ANN could not represent the soil-profile salt balance
\parencite{Sarangi2006}. The authors conclude that ANNs are simpler and faster
and operate on minimal input data---moving from a ``black box'' toward a ``gray
box'' when system-based parameters are included---while SALTMOD remains
preferable for detailed salt-balance accounting \parencite{Sarangi2006}.

\subsection{Machine Learning and Data-Driven Predictive Models}

Artificial neural networks have proven accurate and efficient for real-time
water-table management. For Canadian subsurface-drainage (SD) and
subirrigation (SDI) systems, low RMSEs confirmed strong ANN performance: 1996
real-time control gave minimum RMSEs of 73.8~mm (SD) and 60.6~mm (SDI), and
time-series simulation improved for the longer time gap ($D=3$) and with more
training data \parencite{Yang2000}. Most best-performing networks were small,
indicating that large architectures were unnecessary, and the authors emphasise
that ANNs are easy and fast to develop, require little training time, and can be
improved simply by adding recent data \parencite{Yang2000}. Where measured
saturated hydraulic conductivity is scarce, site-specific ANNs predicted $K_{sat}$
more accurately than pedotransfer functions (PTFs) in both an easy-access plain
(EAP, best $R^2=0.90$) and a hard-access plain (HAP, best $R^2=0.96$); PTF-2 was
the best PTF but still less accurate, and ANN-based drainage designs produced
drain spacing and water-table depths close to measured-data designs, whereas
PTF-based designs gave shorter spacing, deeper water tables, and higher initial
costs \parencite{Ellafi2021}. The number of $K_{sat}$ measurements needed to
train an accurate ANN could be reduced to about 50 \parencite{Ellafi2021}.

Random forests outperformed multiple linear regression (MLR) for
drainage-related agronomic prediction in Quebec. For corn plant height, RF
achieved $R^2$ of 0.97 (calibration), 0.84 (validation), and 0.98 (complete
dataset) versus 0.69 for MLR, and RF predicted mean water-table depth with
$R^2=0.95$ versus 0.28 for MLR \parencite{Djiemon2019}. Both methods identified
drain depth and drain distance as important predictors, but RF additionally
captured nonlinear effects and smaller prediction intervals, and it located an
optimal subsurface drain spacing of about 7~m and a critical mean water-table
depth greater than 0.25~m for maximising corn height, while subsoiling direction
had negligible effect \parencite{Djiemon2019}. At the frontier of surrogate
modelling, an Attention U-Net with an adaptive weighted loss (AWL) cut the
average $L_2$ error across four predicted channels by roughly 43\% relative to a
baseline U-Net with mean-squared-error loss, lowering validation loss to
$5.9\times10^{-5}$ versus $8.5\times10^{-5}$ \parencite{Lei2026}. On the test set
the model reached $R^2$ of 0.997--1 and RMSE below 0.05 for hydraulic head,
drainage water volume, salt concentration, and salt discharge; relative errors
of total drainage water and salt discharge stayed within 1.5\%; and a global
optimisation converged to a 40~m drain spacing and 1.4--1.95~m burial depth
while drastically reducing computation time compared with numerical simulation
\parencite{Lei2026}.

Clustering and discriminant analysis provide a further data-driven route to
delineating management zones. In Iowa, average annual NO$_3$-N leaching losses in
drainage water varied from a minimum mean of 6.8~kg-N~ha$^{-1}$ (1994) to a
maximum mean of 35.2~kg-N~ha$^{-1}$ (1993); cluster analysis grouped normalised
losses into high (mean 2.18, 14\% of area), medium (0.28, 50\%), and low
($-0.54$, 36\%) zones, and stepwise discriminant analysis identified elevation
and subsurface drainage as the significant discriminating variables
($p<0.01$), predicting cluster membership with about 86\% accuracy
\parencite{Bakhsh2005b}. A companion yield study formed low, medium, and high
clusters from normalised corn--soybean yields each year and found that soil,
curvature, and aspect contributed significantly in four of six years, though no
variable qualified for the discriminant model in 1997, 1998, or the pooled
1993--1998 data at $P\le0.15$ \parencite{Bakhsh2007b}.

\subsection{Remote Sensing and Deep Learning for Tile-Drainage Mapping}

\subsubsection{Regional and national tile-drainage extent products}

Two large-area products dominate the mapping evidence. AgTile-US, a 30-m
decision-tree map for the contiguous United States, agreed closely with USDA
county estimates, reporting a national tile-drained area of 22{,}340{,}442~ha
against 22{,}418{,}966~ha from USDA (a $-0.35$\% difference); validation with
16{,}000 ground-truth points gave 86.03\% overall accuracy (13.97\% omission
error), with state-level accuracy highest in the heavily drained north-central
states (72.8--100\%) \parencite{Valayamkunnath2020}. The complementary
random-forest product SEETileDrain, built through Google Earth Engine for the US
Midwest, achieved testing overall accuracy of 0.96, recall 0.96, precision 0.85,
balanced accuracy 0.96, and F1 of 0.90, with county-scale area agreeing with the
Ag~Census at $r^2=0.69$; it estimated 185{,}549~km$^2$ of tile drainage in 2017,
about 9.4\% below the USDA-NASS estimate of 204{,}842~km$^2$
\parencite{Wan2024}. A simplified random forest using only slope and soil
drainage classes performed far worse (out-of-bag error 36\%, precision 0.29, F1
0.4), and 27\% of counties with NASS-reported tile were predicted to have none,
though these had a small median reported area of about 27~km$^2$
\parencite{Wan2024}. In Denmark, a selective-ensemble machine-learning approach
predicted artificially drained areas over 49.4\% of the study region, with the
final ensemble achieving $\kappa=0.526$ and 76.5\% simple accuracy; models
trained on principal components outperformed those on raw covariates and had
shorter prediction times (mean 8.2~s in the ensemble versus 49.4~s in the
library), and evaluation performance correlated with hillclimb performance
($R^2=0.66$) \parencite{Moller2018}.

\subsubsection{Deep-learning detection of drain lines from imagery}

Convolutional segmentation networks reliably delineate visible drain lines. A
U-Net trained on imagery from the US Midwest delineated tile drains in 53
validation images across two areas---including 21 images about 200~km southwest
of the training data---exceeding 92\% accuracy and roughly 53\% balanced
accuracy (reported 93--96\% accuracy, 52--54\% balanced accuracy), with training
performance of 95\% accuracy and 63\% balanced accuracy; the model learned to
ignore roads, buildings, trees, lakes, and boundary artefacts, and drains were
most visible in spring imagery under high solar radiation, intermediate soil
water, and minimal crop cover \parencite{Redoloza2023}. In Ohio, a
deep-learning segmentation model reached minimum Dice losses of 0.28 (training)
and 0.42 (validation) over 500 epochs, an average validation Dice coefficient of
0.58 across 257 images, and 0.55 on a large-scale map, outperforming Sobel and
Canny edge detectors that produced noisier and more false detections
\parencite{Song2021}. An ensemble fully convolutional network gave accurate,
robust drainage-pipe detection on training, validation, and unseen test data,
was visually closer to the ground truth than individual models, and outperformed
Sobel edge detection and the Hough transform; performance depended on input
resolution, peaking at 400~m/256~pixels with reasonable results (Dice $>0.3$)
between 200 and 500~m/256~pixels \parencite{Woo2023}. Earlier automated
approaches were already useful: a decision-tree classification of an Indiana
watershed identified 79\% as potentially tile-drained and, after masking, an
estimated 50\% drained area with drain spacing of 17--80~m and sub-watershed
averages of 43--44~m, within the recommended 23--45~m range for local soils
\parencite{Naz2009}.

\subsubsection{Ancillary remote-sensing methods relevant to drainage}

Several studies quantify how remote sensing infers surface conditions tied to
drainage. Synthetic-aperture-radar (SAR) image simulation of undulated corn
canopy reproduced the statistical and undulation properties of a validation
parcel, with autocorrelation peaks separated by 5.3~pixels (25~m) corresponding
to distinct drainage ridges; coefficient of variation and contrast discriminated
corn fields with and without subsurface drainage, and low contrast (near-zero
plant-height differential) was associated with drained fields
\parencite{Meunier2001}. A Landsat-based soil-water-content algorithm exploited
strong SWIR absorption (Bands~5 and~7), with reflectance nearly constant below
0.20~cm$^3$~cm$^{-3}$ and rising above 0.40~cm$^3$~cm$^{-3}$; the best 15-cm
model, $Y_{15}=0.48e^{-2.64(B5-B1)}$, validated on 70 observations across nine
fields with $r=0.94$ and RMSE near 0.02~cm$^3$~cm$^{-3}$, though it
underestimated wetter conditions \parencite{Rijal2013}. For cover-crop
monitoring in an Illinois watershed, SAVI, EVI, and TVI correlated strongly with
biomass and N uptake; SAVI was selected because it matched or bettered the
others while needing only red and near-infrared bands, giving biomass estimates
of 86--107\% and N-uptake estimates of 85--98\% of ground values, and it
outperformed ordinary kriging and inverse-distance weighting where management
varied \parencite{Xu2018}. A large-aperture-scintillometer study showed that
sensible-heat-flux retrieval is sensitive to parameterisation: a rule-of-thumb
roughness length overestimated $H_{LAS}$ by 47--66\% via inflated $u^*$, but an
improved $z_0$ estimate brought $u^*$ RMSD from 0.21 to 0.08~m~s$^{-1}$ and slope
from 1.37 to 0.98, yielding LAS--eddy-covariance agreement within 32~W~m$^{-2}$
and a slope of 1.0 for the best (W71) temperature function \parencite{Zhang2010}.
A broader review documents an increasing 20-year trend in agricultural remote
sensing, a sharp post-2015 rise in unmanned aerial systems (34\% of papers from
Europe, 20\% USA, 11\% China), and notes that high-resolution thermal imagery
can detect temperature differences over drain lines and thereby the subsurface
tile network, while subsurface drainage remained comparatively under-studied
\parencite{Khanal2020}.

\subsection{Geostatistical and Spatial-Interpolation Methods for Drainage Design}

Kriging-family methods are consistently the most reliable interpolators for the
spatially variable soil properties that govern drain spacing. In the Nile Delta,
variations in hydraulic conductivity were spatially isotropic with a high nugget
and a 5.6~km range of influence; kriging cross-validation gave low estimation
errors (mean $-4.2\times10^{-4}$, variance 1.012), and kriged effective
conductivities were recommended as design values because they are the best
obtainable estimates and produce smooth surfaces delineating homogeneous blocks
\parencite{GALLICHAND1991}. Grid-optimisation work showed that a 600~m grid
retained nearly all information: average kriged point conductivity was
0.201~m~d$^{-1}$ on both complete and reduced grids, block-effective correlations
reached 0.906--0.909 with average absolute deviations of 0.028--0.031~m~d$^{-1}$
(versus 0.930 and 0.031 for the measured grid), and a preliminary 900~m survey
sufficed to fix the optimal sampling density \parencite{GALLICHAND1992}. For clay
content, no interpolation method showed important bias (estimated averages
56.19--57.07\% versus 57.13\% observed); cokriging gave the lowest mean absolute
difference (12.01) but only marginally beat kriging and inverse-distance
weighting, while the closest-neighbour method gave the highest MAD (16.44) and
the largest low-clay areas, so kriging, cokriging, and weighted moving average
($\alpha=1$) were all judged adequate \parencite{GALLICHAND1993}.

The practical value of accounting for spatial variability is quantified by
several Egyptian studies. Kriged estimates outperformed measured point values:
measured water-table depth underestimated the need for subsurface drainage by
about 40\%, measured soil salinity overestimated levels by 59\%, and using
kriged hydraulic conductivity widened calculated lateral drain spacing by about
5\% (up to 9.6\% for some unsteady-state formulas), saving 17{,}155~m of lateral
pipe while reducing the minimum sample size roughly three- to sevenfold
\parencite{Moustafa1998b}. A geostatistical correlation model for representative
$K_{sat}$ was validated on Nile Delta and Nile Valley soils and shown practically
valuable; neglecting spatial variability of $K_{sat}$ could bias design drain
spacing by $-27$\% to $+3$\% and overestimate required sample size by about 76\%
\parencite{Moustafa2000}. The covariance variogram proved superior to the
semi-variogram for clay soils, being smoother, more robust to high values, and
lower in cross-validation error, and subsurface drainage itself strongly altered
spatial variability---the spatial correlation range increased about 29\% and
relative structured heterogeneity doubled four years after installation, while
the nugget rose for all soil-water properties \parencite{Moustafa1998}. In
Turkey, ordinary point kriging was appropriate for $K_{sat}$ and soluble salts,
which showed strong depth-dependent structure (horizontal models explaining
81--84\% of variance) with horizontal correlation distances of 710--1130~m,
supporting an approximately 1130~m~$\times$~1130~m subfield and $\geq75$~cm profile
as a reference for drain-tile layout \parencite{Akis2015}. Simple regression and
geostatistical models for rapid representative $K_{sat}$ were also proposed for
large-scale drainage design, with coefficients that necessarily differ among
soils and hydrological conditions \parencite{Sepaskhah2004}.

\subsection{Calibration, Parameter Estimation, and Inverse Modelling}

The way objective functions are constructed and optimised strongly affects
parameter identifiability. In Iranian inverse modelling of soil hydraulic
parameters, a genetic algorithm (GA) generally outperformed PEST, especially for
objective functions using two or three datasets; combining drainage discharge,
water-table depth, and drainage salinity was the best strategy, though drainage
discharge with water-table depth alone already gave excellent results, and PEST
was less capable for complex objective functions \parencite{Sedaghatdoost2019}.
Variational data assimilation (VDA)---the first such implementation for
SIDRA-type models---used global adjoint sensitivity analysis to identify
$K_{sat}$ as the most influential parameter (followed by $\mu$ then $S_{inter}$),
selecting only 4 of 11 parameters for calibration; split-sample validation gave
KGE $>0.66$, NSE $>0.53$, and RMSE $<2.44$~l~s$^{-1}$, and VDA cut the five-year
cumulative-volume discrepancy to 4.8~mm ($<1$\%) versus 58.7~mm (10\%) for
PAP-GR \parencite{Chelil2022b}. Single-parameter tests showed $K_{sat}$ and
$\mu$ converged uniquely while $SSDI$ and $S_{inter}$ hit local minima, and a
hybrid background-variance approach improved robustness across periods
\parencite{Chelil2022b}. At regional scale, a single-objective calibration in
California produced identifiable parameters with small uncertainties and
predicted regional water-table variation well (RMSE 2.76~m, correlation 0.98),
while multi-objective calibration revealed clear trade-offs between water-table
and pumping predictions; drainage predictions were much more sensitive yet
largely independent of the other objectives, and prediction uncertainty was
large for water tables but small for drainage and pumping \parencite{Schoups2005}.

\subsection{Evaluation of Measurement, Sampling, and Sensor Methods}

Performance evaluation extends to the field protocols that generate the data
against which models are judged. For phosphorus-load monitoring in Michigan tile
drainage, all sampling strategies underestimated total reactive phosphorus (TRP)
load, but the magnitude depended sharply on design: time-proportional discrete
sampling worsened from $-0.2$\% at 3~h to $-51.0$\% at 14~d, and time-proportional
composites ran from $-12.4$\% (1-d) to $-43.0$\% (7-d), whereas flow-proportional
discrete sampling stayed within $-0.2$ to $-5.4$\% and flow-proportional
composites within $-0.5$ to $-7.2$\% \parencite{Dialameh2022}. Flow-proportional
strategies thus gave more accurate cumulative-load estimates with fewer samples
and lower analysis cost, and the number of aliquots did not substantially affect
accuracy \parencite{Dialameh2022}. A recording ultrasonic flow meter in a
multi-slotted U-pipe achieved very high calibration quality (mean and minimum
$R^2$ of 0.9993 and 0.993), propagated $Q(h)$ uncertainties of only 0.1--0.4\%
across slot sizes, and performed well in laboratory and field with good signal
stability using low-cost components \parencite{Enamorado2007}. For denitrifying
bioreactor tracer tests, a continuously deployed bromide sensor produced both
under- and over-reported concentrations---the best field match (test~\#7) still
underpredicted the peak by 7.5\%, with lowest error RMSE 5.0~mg~L$^{-1}$ and MAE
3.0~mg~L$^{-1}$ and a pooled slope near 1.0 (0.96); most tracer metrics fell
within $\pm10$\% of lab-based values, but recovery and peak-timing metrics could
change substantially (e.g.\ hydraulic efficiency from 0.29 to 1.8), and post-hoc
bottle-based measurement outperformed real-time deployment (pooled slope 0.97,
RMSE 3.1~mg~L$^{-1}$) \parencite{deOliveira2023b}. A non-intrusive geophysical
protocol produced ``as-built'' subsurface maps of golf-green tile, confirming
all tile spacing met the USGA specification but finding four of 12 laterals below
the minimum required downward slope (two sloping upward) and about 20\% of the
area failing root-zone thickness standards \parencite{Freeland2014}. On
drainage-efficiency measurement itself, both large-ring and small-core methods
were valuable for relative comparisons, the large ring covering a more
representative elementary volume, and infiltration and saturated conductivity
were 5--65 times larger in the trench backfill zone than midway between trenches
in Swedish clays \parencite{Messing2006}.

\subsection{Environmental Indicators and Empirical Regression Relationships}

Aggregative indicators and empirical regressions provide a complementary,
policy-facing layer of model evaluation. A global compilation of 60
hydraulic-retention-time (HRT)--nitrate-removal-efficiency (NRE) regressions
showed strong within-study linearity but wide between-study variability (slopes
0.13--18~\%/h, intercepts $-19$ to $+47$\%); slope correlated positively with
water temperature (Spearman $\rho=+0.39$) and negatively with inflow N
concentration ($\rho=-0.46$), while the intercept correlated with no compiled
parameter \parencite{Christianson2025c}. Generalised median models
were NRE~$=2.9\times\mathrm{HRT}+0.37$ for the full dataset and
$3.2\times\mathrm{HRT}+0.18$ for a subsurface-drainage ``Tile Proxy'' subset, and
study scale did not significantly alter slope or intercept, though field-scale
studies showed greater slope heterogeneity \parencite{Christianson2025c}. A soil
quality index (SQI) built by scoring-function analysis in Ohio ranged narrowly
(0.69--0.71) and was not significantly affected by tillage or subsurface drainage
but was significantly correlated with corn yield ($R=0.62$), with $K_{sat}$ the
key physical indicator and soil organic carbon the highest-weighted chemical
indicator \parencite{Nakajima2015}. At landscape scale, phosphorus-loss
indicators such as IROWC-P are framed as aggregations of multiple source $\times$
transport interactions whose dominant components depend on climate, soils,
topography, and management; the revised component structure estimates
edge-of-field loss and applies a delivery ratio, and results are expected to be
``directionally and magnitudinally correct'' against annual water-quality data
\parencite{Reid2018}. More broadly, environmental indicators are presented as
powerful tools for tracking change and informing policy that function well at
field, catchment, and local scales but need refinement for national or
international comparison, with the Phosphorus Index highlighted as a widely
adopted aggregative indicator encompassing subsurface tile-drainage pathways and
soil organic carbon as an indirect indicator of soil quality and CO$_2$ emissions
\parencite{Zhang2018}. Finally, a riparian-quality assessment of 21 tile-drained
Canadian sites produced RQI scores of 45--129 that correlated with an independent
index ($R^2=0.56$), while width alone correlated more strongly with that index
($R^2=0.79$), and partial-least-squares analysis showed nutrient and sediment
retention related differently to landscape variables by region
\parencite{Terrado2015}.

### Material: Numerical Modeling of Pipe Flow Dynamics

\subsection{Overview}

Numerical and analytical modeling of pipe flow dynamics in subsurface (tile) drainage
spans a continuum of scales and physical processes, from the movement of water toward
and into individual perforated pipes, through the hydraulics of flow within the conduit
network, to the measurement of discharge at control structures and outlets. Across this
theme, the dominant methodological signature is validation: models are consistently
tested against sand-tank and laboratory experiments, analytical benchmarks, field
observations, and independent numerical solutions to confirm accuracy in predicting
inflow/outflow dynamics, water-table behavior, and water mass balance. Several
consolidated lines of evidence emerge. First, the classical governing equations---the
Richards equation and its Boussinesq-based approximations---reproduce observed
water-table drawdown and cumulative drainage/subirrigation volumes when a nonconstant
drainable porosity is accounted for \parencite{TANG1977,Xin2016c}. Second, analytical
drain-spacing and single-tile flow solutions closely track numerical simulations and
observed heads, and newer formulations reduce reliance on the difficult-to-estimate
drainable porosity parameter \parencite{Cooke2001,KUMAR1994,SINGH1992,FathAllah2025,Chahar2010}.
Third, entrance resistance and effective radius---the parameters governing how readily
water crosses the pipe wall---have been quantified for a range of perforation geometries
and envelopes, with sock-wrapped pipes approaching the behavior of a fully open pipe
\parencite{Ghane2022,Ghane2022b,Gaj2020,Oyarce2017}. Fourth, within-conduit hydraulics,
including spatially varied flow, friction resistance, and outlet effects, have been
modeled and benchmarked against experiments with sub-5\% error in leading cases
\parencite{Bayat2011,Kouchakzadeh2011,Abdurrasheed2019,Graber2004,Nédélec2008}. Finally,
robust head--discharge relationships for V-notch and compound weirs, along with
vision-based flow detection, provide the measurement backbone for validating drainage
models and quantifying conservation-practice performance
\parencite{Christianson2019,BinShokrana2021,Katuwal2024,Napierala2023,Popek2021,Young2023}.
This section synthesizes these established results, emphasizing quantified outcomes and
the conditions under which each modeling approach has been confirmed.

\subsection{Governing Equations for Water-Table and Drainage Dynamics}

\subsubsection{Richards- and Boussinesq-Based Solutions}

The most physically complete descriptions of drainage and subirrigation solve the
Richards equation, which fully represents saturated and unsaturated water movement during
both processes \parencite{TANG1977}. In sand-tank validation, predicted water-table
drawdown from the Richards equation was somewhat slower than observed but in generally
good agreement; the profile drained slightly more rapidly than predicted, whereas for
subirrigation the predicted--observed agreement was better than for drainage
\parencite{TANG1977}. Predicted and observed inflow/outflow rates and cumulative flow
volumes agreed closely, with the predicted rate slightly lower than observed for drainage
and slightly higher than observed for subirrigation \parencite{TANG1977}. Critically,
approximate Boussinesq-based solutions were in close agreement with the Richards-equation
solutions and with experiment \emph{when a variable (nonconstant) drainable porosity was
considered} \parencite{TANG1977}. The overall conclusion is that the Richards equation is
more accurate, but Boussinesq approximations, corrected for nonconstant drainable
porosity, remain useful for design when reliable saturated hydraulic conductivity and
drainable porosity are available; by suitable modification the computer solution can also
accommodate irregular boundary/initial conditions and soil stratification
\parencite{TANG1977}.

The role of the unsaturated (vadose) zone in setting the effective drainable porosity is
developed further in the Boussinesq-equation (BE) analyses of \textcite{Xin2016c}. Using
an effective specific yield formulation to mimic the vadose-zone effect, the authors
report $S_y = 0.0512$ for cases 1 and 3 and $0.1057$ for cases 2 and 4, and derive a new
analytical solution separately for the BE model to remove large errors that arise when the
unsaturated zone is neglected \parencite{Xin2016c}. The two BE-model solutions predicted
almost identical non-dimensional cumulative water drainage over time across different
$l_1/l$ values, indicating that the new analytical solution is reasonably accurate across
scales, and for the four experimental runs the adjusted-$S_y$ BE model, the analytical
solution, and the SUTRA numerical model predicted similar total water drainage at the end
of the process---good agreement in water mass balance \parencite{Xin2016c}. The BE model
gave reasonably good agreement with the experimental data for cases 2 and 4. A recognized
limitation is that the BE model with an effective specific yield predicted a quicker
groundwater-table decline and could not capture the dynamic drainable-porosity effect
caused by vadose-zone development; for case 4 the analytical solution predicted slightly
quicker drainage than the numerical BE model, attributed to approximations (notably a
Taylor expansion of the initial boundary condition) \parencite{Xin2016c}. Overall, the
Boussinesq equation with an effective specific yield provides a reasonably good
description of the drainage process, particularly for total water drainage
\parencite{Xin2016c}.

\subsubsection{Preferential Flow and Constitutive Relationships}

At the field scale, the fidelity of tile-flow prediction depends strongly on the soil
hydraulic functions used. \textcite{Mohanty1997} introduced new piecewise-continuous,
bimodal soil-water-retention and hydraulic-conductivity functions, which predicted
preferential flow to the tile drain reasonably well following all irrigation events and
performed better than the unimodal van Genuchten--Mualem functions in describing the
observed flow regime. Predicted flow using the bimodal functions matched observed flow
rates reasonably well and far better than the unimodal case, minimized over- and
under-predictions across the different flow domains, and better captured the timing and
magnitude of the preferential-flow peaks \parencite{Mohanty1997}. This body of work
established that capturing preferential (macropore) flow in tile drains requires
constitutive relationships beyond a single unimodal curve.

The recession behavior of the water table following recharge was addressed analytically
by \textcite{VANDELEUR1959}, whose mathematical approach accorded with field-experiment
conclusions and was successfully checked by a sandbox experiment (in which radial
resistance was absent). Tail recession was described as beginning only after a period
following the cessation of vertical percolation into the saturated zone; before that, the
recession curve depends on the rates and durations of preceding replenishment
\parencite{VANDELEUR1959}. Radial resistance near the drain could appear as a modified
value of the equivalent depth $D$, suggested to be high at one site and almost absent at
another \parencite{VANDELEUR1959}.

\subsection{Analytical Drain-Spacing and Single-Tile Flow Solutions}

\subsubsection{Single-Tile and Region-of-Influence Formulations}

For flow to a single tile, \textcite{Cooke2001} proposed analytical solutions that closely
agreed with numerical (finite-element) simulations and were judged adequate for practical
description of single-tile flow. The key conceptual advance is replacing the usual drain
spacing with a variable \emph{region of influence} $L$---corresponding to points in the
finite-element domain where saturation was at least 98.5\%---which makes the equations
applicable to irregular or randomly spaced tile systems and eliminates the need to specify
a spacing parameter \parencite{Cooke2001}. Without accretion, analytical solutions using
drainable porosity derived from water-retention curves gave root-mean-squared deviations
of $0.085$ cm$^2$ per day for silty clay loam and $0.060$ cm$^2$ per day for clay;
deviations between analytical and numerical flow were greatest near the start of
simulations, when the initially flat water table was being drawn down to the tile
\parencite{Cooke2001}. The resulting equations are simple, universally applicable, and can
be incorporated into existing models without appreciable added complexity or computing
cost \parencite{Cooke2001}.

\subsubsection{Transient Water-Table and Parameter-Estimation Methods}

\textcite{SINGH1992} modified the steady-state drainage solutions of Ernst, Dagan, and van
Beers to predict the transient water table midway between drains, integrating them
alongside the Hooghoudt, Toksöz--Kirkham, and van Schilfgaarde equations. At the Mundlana,
India site these integrated equations predicted hydraulic heads reasonably close to
observed values for 50, 67, and 84 m drain spacings and agreed well with the transient
solutions of van Schilfgaarde and of Bouwer and van Schilfgaarde \parencite{SINGH1992}.
For the Nwa and Twocock data, all equations predicted larger spacings than the actual
40.23 m while maintaining the same order of performance across both experimental periods;
for the El-Mowelhi and Hermsmeier data, the predicted time for a 0.8 m water-table drop
was two to three times the actual time in the first year and four to six times in the
second year, with some equations agreeing more closely than others
\parencite{SINGH1992}.

Complementary parameter-estimation work by \textcite{KUMAR1994} in India proposed
analytical solutions that agreed closely with the Glover--Dumm solution, with reported
errors below 10\% for periods up to 15 days. A computer-simulation technique produced high
agreement between observed and predicted dimensionless hydraulic heads (correlation
coefficients of 0.9449 to 0.9931), yielded $KD/f$ estimates close to graphical-technique
means, and was concluded to be ``by far and most appropriate'' for evaluating the
transmissivity-to-drainable-pore-space ratio \parencite{KUMAR1994}. Groundwater-table
predictions using inverse-technique parameters matched observations better than those
using field-investigation parameters, and the graphical and computer-simulation techniques
were the preferred approaches for predicting $KD/f$ \parencite{KUMAR1994}. A
discharge-based inverse technique had limited applicability---the Mundlana data were not
amenable to it and the trial-and-error procedure was time consuming unless discharge
measurements were widely separated in time---and the proposed drainable-pore-space
equation generally gave lower $f$ values \parencite{KUMAR1994}. The work also proposed
practical procedures to separate transmissivity and drainable pore space once their ratio
is known, filling a gap in the available theory \parencite{KUMAR1994}.

\subsubsection{Design Equations Reducing Reliance on Drainable Porosity}

A recurring practical obstacle in unsteady-state design is the drainable porosity of soil
($f$). \textcite{FathAllah2025} addressed this with the Safaa equation, which avoids using
$f$---described as the greatest obstacle in unsteady-state design---and replaces it with a
new constant $C_s$, while adding $C_{sh}$ to represent the shape of the initial water
table. Validation with Haress pilot-area data (Nile Delta, Egypt) showed that the lateral
spacing from the Safaa equation was closer to the actual spacing than that from Glover's
equation, and the exponential curve plotted with the Safaa equation fell within the range
of the actual curves \parencite{FathAllah2025}. The Safaa spacing is greater than
Glover's, making the design more economic, and the study concludes the equation provides
more accurate and economic results for subsurface drainage design in the Delta region
\parencite{FathAllah2025}.

For minimum-cost ditch-drainage design, \textcite{Chahar2010} used nonlinear data fitting
to derive an explicit equation for optimal ditch spacing, avoiding numerical computation
of improper integrals, simultaneous solution of nonlinear equations, and formal
optimization. The fitted equation had errors within 7\% over the practical parameter
ranges: for Case 1 it gave 4.40 m versus 4.586 m from the optimization program (4.1\%
error), and for Case 2, 12.035 m versus 12.514 m (3.83\% error) \parencite{Chahar2010}.
Optimal spacing increases with the earthwork-cost parameter and decreases with the
pumping-cost parameter, and the explicit forms give close approximations across the
practical ranges considered \parencite{Chahar2010}.

\subsection{Entrance Resistance, Effective Radius, and Perforation Design}

\subsubsection{Effective Radius of Perforated and Enveloped Pipes}

The ease with which water crosses the pipe wall is characterized by the effective radius
and the entrance resistance factor. \textcite{Ghane2022b} quantified effective radii for
Michigan pipes: 3- or 4-row regular-perforated pipes ranged from 0.3 to 0.9 cm (average
0.6 cm) and 8-row regular-perforated pipes reached 1.9 cm, while 4-row sand-slot pipes
ranged from 0.1 to 0.7 cm (average 0.4 cm) and the 8-row sand-slot pipe reached 1.6 cm.
Wrapping regular-perforated pipes with a knitted-sock envelope increased the effective
radius dramatically to 5.7--6.0 cm (average 5.9 cm) and reduced entrance head loss to near
zero \parencite{Ghane2022b}. The most efficient way to increase effective radius was
increasing the number of longitudinal rows of perforations, followed by increasing
perforation length, whereas increasing perforation width had minimal effect
\parencite{Ghane2022b}. These hydraulic gains carry design and cost implications:
sock-wrapped pipes allowed wider optimum drain spacing than sand-slot pipes, but 8-row
sand-slot pipes had a lower initial system cost than sock-wrapped pipes across all factor
combinations at the same drainage rate and depth; and for soils without a sedimentation
problem, 8-row regular-perforated pipes allowed wider spacing and lower initial cost than
4-row pipes \parencite{Ghane2022b}.

A companion study \parencite{Ghane2022} experimentally measured the effective radius of a
commonly manufactured sock-wrapped pipe for the first time and found close agreement
between measured and theoretical effective radii for the sock-wrapped and sand-slot pipes,
validating the Dierickx (1999) theoretical method. The sock-wrapped pipe had much lower
entrance head loss than the 8-row and 4-row sand-slot pipes, giving it the largest
effective radius and a higher estimated drain inflow than both sand-slot pipes; its very
small entrance resistance factor meant it functioned almost as a completely open pipe
without walls \parencite{Ghane2022}. The knitted-sock envelope met the hydraulic,
anti-clogging, and retention criteria for the sand used, and under free drainage with
ponding, water entered the sock-wrapped pipe only through perforations in the bottom half
of the pipe \parencite{Ghane2022}.

\subsubsection{Entrance Resistance in Corrugated Versus Plain Pipes}

Using a numerical model calibrated with sand-tank experiments, \textcite{Gaj2020}
estimated entrance resistance ($\alpha_e$) and delivery ratio ($Q/Q_0$) for perforated
pipes and developed six nonlinear multiple-regression models to predict these from
perforation design variables---the first reported computation of entrance resistance for
corrugated pipes with circular holes. For corrugated pipes with circular holes, $\alpha_e$
was higher than for plain-wall pipes with the same perforation configuration because
streamlines converge in the corrugation valley; for the case $N=4$ and
$A_p = 60$ cm$^2$/m, $\alpha_e$ was 23\% higher and $Q/Q_0$ 20\% lower in corrugated than
in plain-wall pipes \parencite{Gaj2020}. For the same opening area and configuration,
$\alpha_e$ was 51\% higher for circular holes than for rectangular slots, while $Q/Q_0$
was marginally greater for circular holes; the drawdown at the midpoint between two
parallel drains did not differ significantly between holes and slots for $N=4$ and
$A_p = 60$ cm$^2$/m \parencite{Gaj2020}. The consolidated conclusion is that rectangular
slots are more hydraulically advantageous than circular holes in the valleys of corrugated
pipes \parencite{Gaj2020}.

\textcite{Oyarce2017} gave experimental validity to Dierickx's mathematical model through
discharge measurements in a physical prototype and developed a calculation tool to
evaluate water-entrance resistance across pipe radii, perforation densities, and screen
presence. Water extraction by a perforated pipe was proportional to extraction area, but
increasing perforation density reduced pipe support and mechanical-impact resistance
\parencite{Oyarce2017}. The entrance-resistance factor depended on perforation density and
arrangement and on the presence of an enveloping screen; a screen reduced the resistance
factor and increased the effective radius, whereas identical pipes without an envelope did
not drain the substrates because the perforations clogged almost instantly
\parencite{Oyarce2017}. For small pressure heads, discharge increased with perforation
density, but for larger heads the effect was less relevant; the optimal distance between
adjacent parallel drainpipes was smaller when entrance resistance was considered,
especially for low saturated hydraulic heads \parencite{Oyarce2017}. Overall, smaller
diameter pipes with higher perforation density and an enveloping screen provided greater
discharge capacity than larger-diameter pipes---subject to a balance between mechanical
strength and discharge capacity---identifying an economic/design advantage for smaller
pipes with few, small-width perforations combined with a screen envelope
\parencite{Oyarce2017}.

\subsubsection{Structural Response of Perforations to Traffic Loads}

The mechanical counterpart to perforation hydraulics is the structural response of
corrugated HDPE pipes to field traffic, modeled by \textcite{Gaj2021}. Vertical stress
transmission from wheel loads was largest for the loaded grain wagon and smallest for the
tractor; at depths greater than 1.6 m, $\sigma_{vi}$ was below 35 kPa for the three
wheel-load cases \parencite{Gaj2021}. A bonded interface-friction condition generally
induced larger wall stresses than a smooth condition, though not statistically
significantly across all soil types and load cases; for non-perforated pipes the loaded
(LL) cases produced greater stresses than the empty/half-loaded cases across all soil
types, with the critical case being a loaded grain wagon on silt loam \parencite{Gaj2021}.
Failure by ductile yielding of non-perforated corrugated HDPE pipes was highly unlikely
under the investigated field-traffic, soil, and water-table conditions, and vertical
deflection remained within the 5\% performance limit under all loading, although
perforations can raise the risk of ductile-yield failure before deflection becomes
limiting \parencite{Gaj2021}. Circular-hole perforations substantially increased local
wall stresses---5.6 mm-diameter holes exceeded the yield strength at burial depths
shallower than 0.9 m under the critical load case in silt loam---whereas rectangular slots
produced lower stress concentrations when the perforation width $w_{perf} \le 0.5 w_v$,
with slot length having little effect compared with slot width \parencite{Gaj2021}. Axial
perforation spacing did not materially affect stress distribution, so perforations may be
placed in every corrugation valley without direct axial-stress interaction; the simulated
stresses were used to establish perforation-dimension relationships that allow perforations
to be incorporated into the structural design of buried corrugated pipes
\parencite{Gaj2021}. Consistent with the hydraulic findings, rectangular slots also had a
lower ductile-yielding failure risk than circular holes provided $w_{perf} \le 0.5 w_v$
\parencite{Gaj2021}.

\subsubsection{Discharge to Improved Drains Under Ponding}

\textcite{Tao2019} proposed a general discharge formula for improved subsurface drainage
with ponded water in homogeneous, isotropic soil, verified by laboratory experiment, field
test, and numerical simulation. Under two boundary assumptions, Method 1 outperformed
Method 2 in the numerical simulation, with calculated values closer to the 1:1 line; the
critical boundary between saturated and unsaturated filter conditions was
$(b_0 + h_0)/d = 0.6$ for Method 1 and $h_0/d = 0.4$ for Method 2 \parencite{Tao2019}. For
formula simplification, a drain-spacing-to-impermeable-layer-depth ratio of 3 was the
critical condition separating the two simplified equations, and when the impermeable-layer
depth is unknown, a fallback equation can roughly estimate discharge \parencite{Tao2019}.

\subsection{Within-Conduit Hydraulics and Network Flow}

\subsubsection{Spatially Varied Flow and Discharge Capacity}

Flow within drainage conduits with lateral inflow is a spatially varied flow (SVF)
problem. \textcite{Bayat2011} calibrated an SVF model by an inverse method, proposing an
average Manning coefficient of 0.02 for the tested corrugated pipes and local head-loss
coefficients $K_{ud} = 1.0$ and $K_{bd} = 1.0$, supported by comparison between computed
and observed water-surface elevations. Ignoring local head loss in the manhole computation
produced underestimation with a maximum error of $-20\%$ \parencite{Bayat2011}. For the
example network, the SVF model gave a total discharge capacity of 6.41 l\,s$^{-1}$ when
$y/D$ was close to 0.94 in the main pipe, and 5.12 l\,s$^{-1}$ when no segment was allowed
to pressurize; using modified traditional relationships for the same network gave much
larger capacities of 12 and 20.81 l\,s$^{-1}$---roughly two to four times the SVF
value---indicating a large discrepancy relative to the physically based SVF method
\parencite{Bayat2011}.

The generalized capacity problem was treated by \textcite{Graber2004}, who presented a
generalized numerical solution for circular conduits with free discharge and uniform
inflow over a wide range of parameters, and a companion solution for a rectangular
horizontal channel judged of particular practical value. Manning's equation alone is not
generally adequate for subsurface-drain capacity, and Van der Beken's method is likewise
inadequate; computer-based numerical methods are recommended more generally
\parencite{Graber2004}. In the worked example, a 0.102 m pipe provided insufficient
capacity while a 0.152 m pipe met the required design capacity, and a multi-diameter
interceptor-drain design (14-, 12-, 10-, 8-, and 6-in. segments) was produced by computer
calculation \parencite{Graber2004}. Supercritical flow should usually be avoided in
subsurface drains because it uses pipe capacity uneconomically, produces relatively high
velocities, and increases turbulence through hydraulic jumps \parencite{Graber2004}.

\subsubsection{Flow Resistance in Corrugated Pipes}

Resistance to flow depends strongly on flow regime. \textcite{Kouchakzadeh2011} compared
pressurized flow, gradually varied flow (GVF), and SVF in corrugated pipes. The pressurized
setup revealed Darcy--Weisbach friction factors of 0.065 and 0.04 for the 0.115 m and
0.182 m diameter pipes, with corresponding Moody-diagram relative roughness of 0.04 and
0.012 \parencite{Kouchakzadeh2011}. Reported Manning's coefficients were 0.025 and 0.020
for GVF and 0.035 and 0.030 for SVF, for the two diameters respectively; the GVF resistance
coefficient exceeded that of the pressurized regime, and SVF increased resistance by
40--50\% relative to GVF \parencite{Kouchakzadeh2011}. These results provide Manning's $n$
values for corrugated pipes used in sewer and drainage networks \parencite{Kouchakzadeh2011}.

\subsubsection{Outlet Effects and Flow-Control Modules}

Flow behavior at conduit outlets was studied by \textcite{Nédélec2008}, extending
knowledge of free-pipe outlet flow. The conduit outlet may cause non-negligible tail-water
effects upstream when high pipe flow combines with high channel discharge capacity, and
this tail-water effect may increase with flow rates when pipe and channel flows are
concomitant and proportional \parencite{Nédélec2008}. Combined pipe slope and downstream
tail-water effects may cause a transition from free-surface to pressurized flow, and the
system can exhibit two forms of transitional cavity flow with possibly different transient
behaviors \parencite{Nédélec2008}. The work identified and illustrated ten flow patterns
in the channel and pipe; the outlet energy-loss behavior resembled junctions of two pipes
of different diameters and compared rather well with a more general junction model,
suggesting possible generalization subject to further study of friction losses
\parencite{Nédélec2008}.

The effect of an in-channel flow-control module was modeled in three dimensions using
FLOW-3D by \textcite{Abdurrasheed2019}. The numerical results closely matched laboratory
experiments in both free flow without module (FFWM) and flow with module (FWM), with
percentage error below 5\%, and the experimental results strongly agreed with the numerical
values \parencite{Abdurrasheed2019}. The REDAC module altered the flow pattern and created
resistance, increasing the downstream depth-averaged velocity from 43.50 cm/s to about
46.50 cm/s and decreasing water depth from 7.75 cm to 6.50 cm, while raising Manning's
roughness upstream relative to FFWM \parencite{Abdurrasheed2019}. Mass conservation was
maintained, with inflow and outflow both coinciding at 4750 cm$^3$/s (0.00475 m$^3$/s) in
both cases \parencite{Abdurrasheed2019}. The FLOW-3D approach modeled the module behavior
with greater certainty, visualized flows in 2D and 3D, and can characterize larger module
arrangements and different flow conditions without expensive laboratory experiments
\parencite{Abdurrasheed2019}.

\subsubsection{Network Routing Through Risers and Berms}

At the watershed scale, \textcite{Gupta2019} presented a new toolbox (CoBAGNPS, based on
the AGNPS model) for simulating water and sediment control basins (WASCoBs), which
successfully reproduced the required outputs and routed flow through WASCoBs and the
drainage network. For a single WASCoB, there was no substantial difference in flow
characteristics between HB and PI pipe risers, while a third riser type (HBS) produced a
shorter peak discharge and drained slightly more slowly; at the DFTILE outlet the three
riser types produced different peak discharges for two storm events but with overall
reasonable routing behavior \parencite{Gupta2019}. For extreme design storms, the installed
150 mm tile drain was not efficient enough; larger pipes improved drainage, and a 375 mm
pipe was recommended for 2-, 5-, and 10-year 24-h storm events, with 200 mm and 375 mm
diameters significantly enhancing efficiency and reducing ponding time behind the berms
\parencite{Gupta2019}. Direct routing from the DFTILE outlet to the watershed outlet had
only a slight effect on peak flow but increased the recession limb of the hydrograph
\parencite{Gupta2019}.

\subsection{Flow Measurement at Control Structures and Outlets}

\subsubsection{V-Notch Weir Calibrations}

Accurate discharge measurement underpins the validation of all drainage models and the
assessment of conservation practices \parencite{Christianson2019,Katuwal2024}. For a new
stainless-steel-edged 45\textdegree{} V-notch weir in AgriDrain inline water-level control
structures, \textcite{Christianson2019} found no significant differences in fitted weir
parameters across the two structure sizes (15 cm and 25 cm) or the three weir placements.
The fitted equation was $Q = 0.011 H^{2.28}$ ($Q$ in l\,s$^{-1}$, $H$ in cm), equivalently
$Q = 1.44 H^{2.28}$ in U.S. customary units, with a combined-model coefficient of
determination of 0.992 and a standard error of estimate of 0.135
\parencite{Christianson2019}. The standard deviation of measured flow for each weir-height
and structure-size combination ranged from 0.1\% to 2.9\% of the mean, and the equation was
valid only up to a maximum water depth of 16.5 cm, above which a compound weir equation
should be used \parencite{Christianson2019}.

\textcite{BinShokrana2021} developed an empirical flow equation for a metal-edge
sharp-crest V-notch weir using a top-down approach that gave a V-notch apex height of
31.7 cm (from $b = 149.8$ cm and $c = 118.1$ cm). A combination of the weighing method and
a flow meter produced the stage--discharge relation, and least-squares regression showed a
strong head--flow relationship ($R^2 = 0.999$) with the empirical equation
$Q = 0.749 H^{2.25}$, calibrated for flow rates up to 409.42 L/min at a head of 16.3 cm
\parencite{BinShokrana2021}. This equation produced consistently higher flow estimates than
the \textcite{Christianson2019} equation, whereas the USGS equation underestimated flow
across all head measurements \parencite{BinShokrana2021}.

For a PC-edge sharp-crest 22.5\textdegree{} V-notch weir, \textcite{Napierala2023} produced
empirical stage--discharge equations for downstream-face (chamfer) slopes of 30\textdegree,
45\textdegree, and 60\textdegree. The 45\textdegree{} chamfer gave the strongest fit and
lowest error ($R^2 = 0.9981$, RMSE $= 0.255$ dm$^3$\,min$^{-1}$), with the 30\textdegree{}
and 60\textdegree{} equations also fitting well ($R^2 = 0.9955$ and $0.9980$); residuals
were generally within $\pm 4\%$, and among previously reported equations White's fit the
data best for all chamfered angles \parencite{Napierala2023}. These equations can be used
for any cylindrical control structure provided flow passes through the V-notch without
submergence and can support nutrient-load-reduction estimates in subsurface drainage
studies, with the 45\textdegree{} chamfer the best choice \parencite{Napierala2023}.

\subsubsection{Compound Weirs for Overtopping Flows}

Because V-notch equations lose validity once flow overtops the notch, compound weir
formulations are required for high flows. \textcite{Katuwal2024} developed a compound weir
equation ($Q_{CW}$) that estimated overflow discharge accurately, whereas the existing
combined V-notch-plus-rectangular method ($Q_{V+R}$) consistently underestimated it. Across
three Agri Drain control-structure sizes, $Q_{CW}$ produced MAPE below 5\% and PBIAS
between $-5.4\%$ and $0.7\%$, while $Q_{V+R}$ had PBIAS above 15\% and MAPE above 13\%
\parencite{Katuwal2024}. The V-notch calibration relationships varied with structure size,
confirming that head--discharge relationships are affected by structure size; against
published USBR compound-weir data, $Q_{CW}$ gave estimates comparable to measured values
while $Q_{V+R}$ remained consistently lower \parencite{Katuwal2024}. Importantly, using
$Q_{CW}$ instead of $Q_{V+R}$ increased the estimated annual discharge and nitrate load
diverted to a saturated buffer, demonstrating that overflow underestimation can materially
bias nutrient-load estimates \parencite{Katuwal2024}.

\subsubsection{Adjustable Damming Regulators}

\textcite{Popek2021} calibrated two water-damming regulators for drains. The funnel
regulator had an effective flow of 0.17--0.25 l\,s$^{-1}$ over $H_f = 47$--71 cm, with a
linear relation between effective flow and overflow-edge height ($R^2 = 0.960$); when inflow
increased up to 5.5 l\,s$^{-1}$, the water layer above the overflow edge rose only
0.12--3.0 cm, allowing a maximum damming adjustment of up to $+3.0$ cm relative to the set
height \parencite{Popek2021}. For the hole regulator, the effective flow required for
damming was 0.009 l\,s$^{-1}$ at $H_h = 20$ cm, 0.012 l\,s$^{-1}$ at 40 cm, and
0.015 l\,s$^{-1}$ at 60 cm; flows up to 0.25 l\,s$^{-1}$ produced similar discharges among
holes and water-table elevations within about 11--15 cm above the hole edge, above which
damming height became more variable, and the reduced-flow curve was developed for
intensities up to 0.65 l\,s$^{-1}$ \parencite{Popek2021}. The funnel regulator suits
holding water at flows at or above the effective flow and ensures a stable damming ordinate
across a wide flow range, while the hole regulator suits low flow rates up to
0.25 l\,s$^{-1}$; both have uncomplicated structure and adapt to existing drainage devices
\parencite{Popek2021}.

\subsubsection{Vision-Based Outlet Monitoring}

Addressing a gap in outlet-monitoring technology, \textcite{Young2023} developed a
low-cost, vision-based method to characterize outlet flow events by occurrence, duration,
and rate from free-standing outlet points without in-situ instrumentation. The approach
correctly detected outlet flow in low, medium, and high flow test cases and did not
misclassify hand or object motions as flow when none was present; occurrence detection was
delayed relative to ground truth by an average of 1.0 s and flow duration was underestimated
by 2.23\% on average \parencite{Young2023}. Steady-state flow-rate estimates were lower than
ground truth for all three conditions, with errors of 5.9\%, 9.2\%, and 11.2\%, so flow
rates were estimated within 12\% or less of ground truth under laboratory conditions
\parencite{Young2023}. The study validated the feasibility of visually monitoring tile
outlet flow and released an open-source application to extract flow-rate data from recorded
video \parencite{Young2023}.

\subsection{Coupling Drainage Hydraulics to Buffer Performance}

Numerical modeling of pipe flow also feeds into the design of edge-of-field practices that
receive drainage water. \textcite{McEachran2020} balanced the time to remove nitrate
against the time for water to flow through a saturated riparian buffer (SRB) to derive a
quantitative expression for optimal buffer width. For all six study sites the optimal width
was smaller than the current SRB width; widths at four of the six sites exceeded the upper
limits of the optimal ranges, implying that nitrate-removal effectiveness could be about 50
to 120\% greater at those sites, while widths at two sites fell within the optimal range
with near-optimal effectiveness \parencite{McEachran2020}. Two sites had optimal widths
below the current minimum buffer width in USDA-NRCS Code 604 \parencite{McEachran2020}. The
overall uncertainty of the optimal width was large---combining all parameter uncertainties
yielded 57\% uncertainty in $L_x^*$---with the removal coefficient $\lambda$ contributing
most, followed by $K$, while $n_e$ and $\Delta h$ contributed only small amounts
\parencite{McEachran2020}. The analysis rested on first-order nitrate removal, quasi-steady-
state concentration profiles, and omission of dispersion; incorporating a site-conditioned
width into design standards would improve water quality locally and regionally
\parencite{McEachran2020}.
