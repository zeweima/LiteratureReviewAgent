### Material: Nitrate and Nutrient Loss via Drainage

\subsection{Overview}

Agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage is one of the most important
anthropogenic modifiers of the field-to-stream nitrogen and phosphorus
pathway, and a large, geographically diverse body of field, watershed,
and modelling evidence now supports a fairly consolidated picture of how
it controls nutrient export. The central, repeatedly confirmed finding
is that subsurface drainage is the dominant hydrologic and nitrogen loss
pathway in artificially drained cropland: in intensively monitored
Norwegian catchments, subsurface drainage carried $76$--$91\%$ of runoff
and $87$--$95\%$ of total-nitrogen loss, with drainage total-N
concentrations $2$--$4$ times higher than in surface runoff and over
$90\%$ of TN losses occurring through subsurface drainage
\parencite{Bechmann2014}. Nitrate-N is the predominant exported species,
and its transport is tightly coupled to discharge, so that hydrology and
fertilizer intensity jointly govern loads \parencite{Raymond2012}. Across
the literature the headline quantities recur: field- and watershed-scale
nitrate-N yields commonly reach or exceed $20$--$60$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$
in maize--soybean systems \parencite{Gentry2009,Gentry2014,Dattamudi2020,Tomer2008},
concentrations frequently approach or exceed the $10$~mg~L$^{-1}$
drinking-water limit \parencite{Dattamudi2020,Gordon2021}, and phosphorus,
though smaller in mass, moves through both tile and surface pathways at
concentrations well above eutrophication thresholds \parencite{Smith2008,Tomer2008}.

The evidence also converges on a suite of mitigation strategies with
quantified, mechanistically coherent effects. Drainage water management
(controlled drainage, subirrigation, and drainage water recycling)
reliably reduces drainage volume and nitrate load, typically in
proportion to the flow reduction, while often maintaining or increasing
yield \parencite{Skaggs2010,Youssef2018,Wu2025,Madramootoo2024}.
Edge-of-field and beyond-the-field practices, constructed wetlands,
denitrification bioreactors and walls, saturated buffers, two-stage
ditches, ditch weirs, riparian zones, and filter strips, remove nitrate
mainly by denitrification and reduce phosphorus and sediment with more
variable efficiency \parencite{Tanner2005,Jaynes2008,Hodaj2017,Strock2025b,JACOBS1985}.
In-field practices, principally cover crops and nutrient management, cut
tile nitrate loss substantially at the tile scale but show attenuated,
scale-dependent signals at the watershed outlet
\parencite{Hanrahan2018,Speir2022,Gentry2014}. This section synthesizes
these consolidated findings, organized around the magnitude and pathways
of loss, the hydrologic and land-management controls that drive them, and
the drainage-management, edge-of-field, and watershed-scale strategies
shown to abate them, together with the salinity, trace-element, and
pesticide dimensions of drainage water quality.

\subsection{Magnitude and Pathways of Nitrogen Loss}

\subsubsection{Subsurface drainage as the dominant nitrogen pathway}

Multiple independent studies establish that in tile-drained landscapes
the subsurface pathway carries the overwhelming majority of nitrogen.
In Norway, over $90\%$ of TN losses occurred through subsurface drainage,
concentrations in drainage were $2$--$4$ times those in surface runoff,
average agricultural-area TN concentrations ranged $3$--$19$~mg~L$^{-1}$,
and nitrate-N constituted $71$--$85\%$ of TN except at one site
\parencite{Bechmann2014}. New Zealand mole-pipe systems captured about
$62\%$ of surplus rainfall as drainage and routed roughly $80\%$ of
soluble N discharged in combined flows, while surface runoff dominated
P, sediment, and \emph{E. coli} transfer \parencite{Monaghan2016}. In
east-central Illinois, tile drainage losses were a major N source to
surface waters, with tile N loads of $22.7$ and $59.9$~kg~ha$^{-1}$ in
successive years and flow-weighted mean NO$_3$-N rising to $19.2$~mg~L$^{-1}$
in the wetter year \parencite{Gentry2009}. Watershed nitrate export in
Iowa exceeded $20$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ and tracked baseflow used as a
surrogate for tile flow \parencite{Tomer2008}, and long-term Illinois
budgets showed subsurface stations losing $13$--$30$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$
of nitrate-N (equal to $16$--$26\%$ of applied N) with more than $70\%$ of
subsurface samples exceeding the $10$~mg~L$^{-1}$ MCL, while surface runoff
was negligible in the flat terrain \parencite{Dattamudi2020}.

\subsubsection{Loads, concentrations, and their variability}

Reported magnitudes span a wide but coherent range. New Zealand tile
drains exported $14$--$109$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ of TN, of which
$58$--$90\%$ was nitrate-N, with annual drainage yields of $193$--$853$~mm
\parencite{Tanner2011}. Riverine and tile nitrate yields in Illinois
averaged around $30$--$32$~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ and ranged up to
$57$--$59$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ \parencite{Gentry2014}, and Indiana ditch
networks showed NO$_3$-N loads of $0.05$--$19$~kg~ha$^{-1}$ with
concentrations peaking above $16$--$20$~mg~L$^{-1}$ in wet springs
\parencite{Smith2008}. Across 29 Iowa watersheds, five-year moving-average
flow-weighted nitrate concentrations ranged $0.6$--$15.5$~mg~L$^{-1}$,
loads spanned $0.07$--$58\times10^{6}$~kg~yr$^{-1}$, and yields ranged
$3.6$--$78.7$~kg~ha$^{-1}$; crucially, interannual variability in load
greatly exceeded that of concentration, so flow-weighted concentration
served as a more robust early indicator of management change than load or
yield \parencite{Danalatos2022}. In a Czech watershed, flow-weighted
drainage nitrate rose with risk class from $9.6$ to $17.8$~mg~L$^{-1}$,
corresponding to drainage nitrate contributions of $796$--$2290$~kg~N~km$^{-2}$~yr$^{-1}$
\parencite{Kvítek2023}. German catchment analysis documented a decline in
drainage N discharge from $33$ to $10$~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ (1480 to
454~t~yr$^{-1}$) between the 1980s and 1990s, driven mainly by reduced N
surplus, with the largest area-specific losses on sandy and loess
substrates \parencite{Hirt2005b}.

\subsubsection{Fertilizer input and hydrology as joint drivers}

At regional scale, fertilizer input and watershed hydrology together
regulate export: about $34\%$ of applied fertilizer N was exported to
streams in the Mississippi basin, wetter watersheds exported a larger
fraction, and tile drainage was identified as a hydrologic modifier that
increases throughput and reduces residence time, thereby raising nitrate
loss to the Gulf of Mexico \parencite{Raymond2012}. Direct comparison of
fertilized and unfertilized Minnesota fields confirmed the causal link:
nitrate load from the unfertilized field was $58\%$ of that from the
fertilized field \parencite{Ghane2016b}. Watershed N budgets showed
fertilizer as the largest input and grain as the largest output, with
mass balances swinging from positive to strongly negative in wet years
that depleted soil N \parencite{Gentry2009}, and plant uptake dominating
outputs ($89$--$96\%$) while roughly $80\%$ of residual soil-profile N was
lost to runoff and leaching \parencite{Dattamudi2020}. Multivariate models
that combined current and prior-year water yield, prior corn yield, and
fall fertilizer sales explained $96\%$ of annual nitrate-yield variation,
and shifting fertilizer application to spring was estimated to reduce
nitrate yield by $17$--$20\%$, detectable in watersheds as large as
$481$~km$^2$ \parencite{Gentry2014}. Principal-component and regression
analyses similarly identified the hydrologic component, together with
flow, N application, and temperature, as the dominant controls on nitrate
load \parencite{Li2015}.

\subsection{Phosphorus and Sediment Loss}

While nitrogen dominates the mass balance, phosphorus and sediment loss
through drainage systems is well documented and follows distinct
controls. Total P loads are typically small ($0.4$--$0.7$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$)
yet concentrations commonly exceed $0.1$~mg~L$^{-1}$ and respond to storm
runoff more than nitrate \parencite{Tomer2008}; in Ohio tiles, mean annual
DRP and TP loads were $0.46$ and $0.57$~kg~ha$^{-1}$, with DRP and TP
concentrations higher in the growing season \parencite{King2016}. Surface
inlets exert a distinct effect on drainage nutrient transport: site-years
with surface inlets had greater median TP load ($0.40$ vs.
$0.21$~kg~ha$^{-1}$) but smaller dissolved-N load ($3.3$ vs.
$23.0$~kg~ha$^{-1}$), and a logistic model using TP and DN loads correctly
classified $89\%$ of site-years, offering a tool to infer upstream
practices \parencite{Flores2021}. Phosphorus is delivered through both
surface and subsurface routes: in flat New Zealand pastures surface runoff
carried $54\%$ of both filterable-reactive and total P
\parencite{Monaghan2016}, whereas Swedish diagnosis identified subsurface
drainage itself as the single largest probable cause of P losses, with
stream proximity second \parencite{Djodjic2002}. Legacy and macropore
processes matter: winter cover-crop tiles showed unexpectedly high TP
during rain and rain-on-snow events, with concurrent TP/TOC increases and
nitrate declines indicating bypass flow through macropores
\parencite{McNeill2026}, and Irish ditch sediments accumulated legacy P
that made farmyard-connection and outlet ditches the highest-risk P
pathways \parencite{Moloney2020,Opoku2024}. Watershed export
also concentrates in spring snowmelt "hot moments," with identifiable
spatial hot spots for DRP and TP distinct from nitrate hot spots
\parencite{Irvine2019}.

\subsection{Hydrologic and Climatic Controls}

\subsubsection{Discharge, high flows, and seasonality}

Discharge is the master variable for both N and P export. Tile discharge
was the main driver of soluble reactive phosphorus load in Indiana
watersheds, and subsurface flow--nitrate load relationships were strongly
positive ($R^2 = 0.84$--$0.86$) \parencite{Trentman2020,Dattamudi2020}.
More than $70\%$ of watershed nitrate export occurred during high-flow
events and moist conditions \parencite{Hanrahan2018}, and high flows were
identified as the major control on contaminant transport, implying that
targeting high flows could reduce loads \parencite{Ghane2016b}. Discharge
is strongly seasonal: in Ohio, $81\%$ of tile discharge occurred in the
nongrowing season, so nitrate and TN loads were greater then despite
higher growing-season concentrations under corn \parencite{King2016}.
Controlled-drainage studies similarly show winter and spring dominance of
loss, with most nitrogen loss between April and May
\parencite{Strock2025b,Grenon2023}. Nutrient concentrations frequently
peak after soil thaw and in spring following planting, especially in
wetter years \parencite{Magner2004,Smith2008,Bechmann2014}. Antecedent
moisture modulates these dynamics: wetter antecedent conditions increased
tile discharge and SRP load, while discharge declined as days since the
last $10$ or $20$~mm event increased \parencite{Trentman2020}.

\subsubsection{Climate change projections}

Modelling indicates that intensifying hydrology will increase drainage
throughput. In an Indiana watershed, annual subsurface drain flow was
projected to increase by up to $70\%$ by century's end (up to $67\%$ under
RCP~8.5), with surface runoff rising up to $131\%$; nitrate loads in tile
drains were projected to fall $25$--$75\%$ by mid-century while surface-runoff
nitrate rose $50$--$100\%$, and soluble-P yield via drains declined
$30$--$60\%$ \parencite{Mehan2019}. Ensemble analysis for an Ohio watershed
found no consensus on the direction of future nutrient change, with
climate-model choice dominating uncertainty for discharge, tile discharge,
ET, and TN, and hydrologic-model choice dominating for runoff and
phosphorus; on average tile discharge rose $18\%$ while TP fell $7\%$ and
TN changed little \parencite{Kujawa2020}. Long-term Latvian and Lithuanian
records already show rising precipitation and temperature and shifting
seasonal outflow, with significant winter increases and spring decreases
in drainage \parencite{Lagzdins2015,Povilaitis2018}. Increasing
precipitation and discharge are expected to shorten N residence time and
exacerbate export \parencite{Raymond2012}.

\subsection{In-Field Management: Cover Crops, Rotation, and Nutrient Management}

\subsubsection{Cover crops at the tile scale}

Cover crops consistently reduce nutrient loss where measured directly at
the tile. In Indiana, tiles draining cover-cropped fields had
$69$--$90\%$ lower seasonal nitrate loads in winter and spring
\parencite{Hanrahan2018}, and independent monitoring found cover crops cut
tile discharge by $28$--$65\%$ and nitrate-N loss by $27$--$72\%$ annually,
with consistent winter--spring reductions and more variable SRP reductions
of $7$--$59\%$ \parencite{Speir2022}. Spring cover-crop biomass reduced
median tile discharge by $50$--$80\%$ and SRP load by up to $54\%$, with
the effect estimated at a $15\%$ reduction in SRP over the fallow period
\parencite{Trentman2020}. In Missouri terraced fields, a paired-watershed
design attributed a $30\%$ reduction in subsurface flow and $75\%$
reduction in TP loss to cover crops \parencite{SalcedaGonzalez2025}. Winter
cover choice matters: winter-wheat fields had the highest and mixed
cover-crop fields the lowest nongrowing-season N concentrations
\parencite{McNeill2026}. Economic analysis showed environmental
co-benefits recover a large share of costs, averaging $61\%$, with the
largest contribution from cover-crop biomass N mineralization
\parencite{Roth2018}.

\subsubsection{Scale attenuation and offsetting responses}

At the watershed outlet the signal is weaker and scale-dependent. Despite
strong tile-scale effects, annual, seasonal, and monthly watershed nitrate
export did not differ significantly between pre- and post-cover-crop years,
although average annual nitrate yield was $13\%$ lower, and monitored tiles
contributed only $\sim7\%$ of watershed export \parencite{Hanrahan2018}.
Watershed-scale reductions were most consistent in spring, and consistent
annual reductions likely require broader regional or targeted
implementation \parencite{Speir2022}. Some cover-crop and relay systems
showed no drainage or nitrate benefit and higher N surplus or N$_2$O
emissions: a winter camelina relay produced no change in cumulative
nitrate load but increased nitrous oxide emissions and N surplus
\parencite{Emmett2022}, and Ohio BACI trials found cover crops reduced
nitrate flow-weighted mean concentration but not nitrate load
\parencite{Askar2023}. Grassing of drainage recharge zones reduced median
nitrate by $26$--$32\%$ locally and monthly N load by up to $47\%$
\parencite{Fucík2015,Kvítek2023}.

\subsection{Drainage Water Management: Controlled Drainage and Subirrigation}

\subsubsection{Consolidated hydrologic and nitrogen effects}

Controlled drainage (CD) is among the best-established practices. A
synthesis of prior studies found CD reduced drainage outflow by $17\%$ to
over $80\%$ depending on soil, drainage intensity, control strategy, and
location, while raising water tables and increasing ET, seepage, and
surface runoff; for many studies nitrogen-loss reduction was about the
same percentage as the drainage-volume reduction, though the N effect
depended on denitrification and seepage pathways \parencite{Skaggs2010}. A
global meta-analysis of 84 publications, 52 sites, and 1788 paired
site-years confirmed that both CD and controlled drainage with
subirrigation (CDSI) reduce subsurface discharge and subsurface nutrient
losses while modestly increasing yield; CDSI reduced subsurface nitrate
and TN more than CD, whereas CD reduced subsurface TP more, and CDSI (but
not CD) significantly increased surface runoff and surface nitrate loss
\parencite{Wu2025}. Simulations across 48 U.S. Midwest locations found CD
reduced annual drainage and N losses everywhere while increasing runoff,
ET, and seepage, with efficacy strongly climate-dependent (largest
reductions at Memphis, smallest at Fargo) \parencite{Youssef2018}.

\subsubsection{Field evidence of nitrate reductions and yield effects}

Field studies quantify these effects concretely. In Quebec, controlled
drainage with subirrigation cut tile drainage volume by $24\%$, nitrate
concentration by $25\%$ ($10.6$ to $7.9$~mg~L$^{-1}$), and nitrate loss by
$43\%$ ($25.8$ to $14.6$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$) \parencite{Ng2000}, and a
long-term Canadian study found CD reduced total nitrate loss by $\sim58\%$,
increased yield $3\%$, and cut drainage volume $17\%$ without elevated
greenhouse-gas emissions \parencite{Madramootoo2024}. In North Carolina,
nine years of CD reduced drainage outflow $33\%$ and nitrate-N export
$30\%$ (average $6.3$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$), a reduction equal to the
extra N removed in grain as CD raised corn and soybean yields $10$--$11\%$
\parencite{Poole2018}. Controlled tile drainage during the growing season
reduced daily water flux $\sim60\%$, nitrate $51\%$, ammonium $58\%$, DRP
and TP $66\%$, and \emph{E. coli} $76\%$, while boosting corn and soybean
yields \parencite{Sunohara2016}, and the same practice imposed en masse
across a watershed significantly reduced growing-season stream fluxes of
water, N, and P, with physical abatement of tile flow as the overriding
mechanism \parencite{Sunohara2015}. Iowa DWM cut tile flow $21\%$ and
nitrate mass loss $29\%$ with an $8\%$ soybean-yield increase, though
flow-weighted concentration was not significantly changed
\parencite{Jaynes2012}. Missouri controlled drainage reduced NO$_3$-N
concentrations by $6.0$--$9.6$~mg~L$^{-1}$ and cumulative nitrate loads by
$69$--$99\%$ during growing seasons, but raised dissolved reactive P
concentrations \parencite{Nash2020}.

\subsubsection{Timing, trade-offs, and phosphorus concerns}

The timing of control governs efficacy: DRAINMOD analysis identified
$1$--$15$~March as the most effective window, when CD could reduce
subsurface outflow and nitrate-N losses by up to $100\%$ and retain up to
$22$~kg~NO$_3$-N~ha$^{-1}$, whereas April starts resembled free drainage
\parencite{Kesicka2023c}. Lithuanian CD reduced monthly outflow
$55$--$80\%$, inorganic-N load $42$--$77\%$, and TP load $34$--$72\%$
\parencite{Povilaitis2018}. Modelled shallow drainage outperformed CD for
nitrogen (reducing N loss $40\%$ vs. $26\%$ long-term) with minimal yield
penalty \parencite{Craft2018}. Trade-offs are recognized: CD can increase
surface DRP and TP losses that partly offset subsurface benefits
\parencite{King2022,Wu2025}, and controlled drainage--subirrigation was
predicted to raise runoff N ($+340\%$) and sediment N ($+118\%$) even while
cutting leaching $35\%$, for a net $17.8\%$ reduction in combined
non-point N \parencite{SHIRMOHAMMADI1995}. Greenhouse-gas responses are
mixed but generally not adverse: N$_2$O fluxes under CD were $49\%$ higher
than free drainage in three years and up to $43\%$ lower in three others
\parencite{Madramootoo2024}. Drainage-management alone often fails to lower
concentrations even when it lowers loads via volume reduction
\parencite{Grenon2023,Wesström2014}. Automatic CD/subirrigation
outperformed traditional CD, reducing N losses up to $27\%$, increasing
plant uptake, and saving $28$--$77\%$ of subirrigation water
\parencite{Abdelbaki2025}. Subirrigation lysimeters in Iran cut drainage
nitrate concentrations by $74$--$84\%$ and improved yield and water-use
efficiency \parencite{Noory2009}, and Swedish controlled drainage/subirrigation
reduced NO$_3$-N, TN, PO$_4$-P, and TP losses to about $40\%$ of
conventional while raising yield and N uptake \parencite{Wesström2014}.

\subsection{Drainage Water Recycling and Reuse}

Drainage water recycling (DWR), capturing and storing drainage for
supplemental irrigation, is documented to benefit both crop production and
downstream water quality. Reported yield gains reach $61\%$ for corn and
$31\%$ for soybean relative to free drainage, with modelled Ohio benefits
rising under future climate; Canadian studies documented N-loss reductions
of $40$--$70\%$ and P-loss reductions of $12$--$36\%$, and modelling
predicted downstream N-delivery reductions of $24$--$37\%$ and P reductions
of $21$--$39\%$ in Iowa and Indiana \parencite{Hay2021}. A North Carolina
reservoir reduced TN load $47\%$, TP load $30\%$, and sediment $87\%$,
driven by nitrate ($-53\%$) and orthophosphate ($-46\%$) concentration
reductions, with hydraulic retention time the key control, though it was a
net source of particulate P \parencite{Moursi2023}. An expanded DRAINMOD
DWR model showed reservoir inflow dominated by subsurface drainage
($67\%$), and a $4\%$-area reservoir optimized corn yield while reducing
nitrate loss $\sim20\%$ ($7.7$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$)
\parencite{Moursi2022}. Multi-location synthesis of 53 site-years showed
DWR is more resilient than free drainage, protecting yield during drought
and excess precipitation, especially in the V9--R2 window, producing
$2.22$~Mg~ha$^{-1}$ higher yield overall and reducing yield variability
$22\%$ \parencite{Willison2021}. Adoption economics indicate crop insurance
is not a major barrier: real-options adoption thresholds shifted less than
$\sim20\%$ with insurance, and DWR and insurance manage different risks
\parencite{Sellars2022}. Regional DWR in Iran saved $67$~Mm$^3$ of water,
recycled $32$~Mm$^3$, and raised river flow while cutting drainage to
evaporation ponds $38\%$; optimal blending was $13$--$23\%$ recycled water,
and a $20\%$ scenario limited sugarcane yield loss to acceptable levels
\parencite{Kosari2024}. In California's San Joaquin Valley, integrated
on-farm drainage management reused all but $0.7\%$ of applied water, using
only $\sim6\%$ of project area for evaporation and reuse, though drainage
EC did not represent crop-water concentration effects
\parencite{Ayars2014}.

\subsection{Edge-of-Field and Beyond-the-Field Practices}

\subsubsection{Constructed wetlands}

Constructed wetlands treating drainage are extensively documented as
effective nitrate sinks, with denitrification the dominant mechanism.
Across drained French watersheds, nitrate removal averaged $\sim42\%$ (up
to $40$--$90\%$), dominated by denitrification ($93\%$) over plant uptake
($7\%$), and devoting $\sim1\%$ of contributing area to a wetland can
realistically achieve $50\%$ removal, with hydraulic residence time the
priority control \parencite{Tournebize2017}. Iowa wetlands reduced incoming
nitrate concentrations $49\%$ and loads $\sim61$~kg~day$^{-1}$, with
performance varying by hydrology ($23$--$65\%$) \parencite{Drake2018}; a
Minnesota wetland removed $67\%$ of tile nitrate over four years, mostly
via infiltration to shallow groundwater \parencite{Gordon2021}; and another
removed $68\%$ of nitrate and $\sim76\%$ of phosphorus, with plant harvest
the primary P-removal route \parencite{Lenhart2016}. Illinois wetlands
retained $36$--$37\%$ of nitrate and total N and over half of P fractions,
and $\sim450$~ha of wetland were estimated necessary to cut watershed N
loading $46\%$ \parencite{Kovacic2006}. New Zealand wetlands occupying
$1$--$2\%$ of catchment area removed $16$--$63\%$ of nitrate-N loads but
performed poorly for P, sometimes exporting more TP than received
\parencite{Tanner2005,Tanner2005b,Tanner2011,Tanner2003}. Wetland
performance is strongly a function of hydraulic loading rate, residence
time, temperature, and inlet concentration: a first-order areal model with
median $k_{20}=25$~m~yr$^{-1}$ and temperature coefficient $1.106$ best
represents whole-system behaviour, and no single-number design is adequate
\parencite{Kadlec2012,Kalcic2018}. A German wetland retained only $2.9\%$
of TN over four years, but retention reached $\sim80\%$ when loads were low
and residence time exceeded $20$~days at temperatures above $8\,^{\circ}$C
\parencite{Steidl2019}. Meta-analysis reported mean cropland-drainage
removals of $23.7\%$ TN and $30.3\%$ nitrate-N but negative mean removals
for ammonium and TP, underscoring variable P and ammonium behaviour
\parencite{Messer2021}.

\subsubsection{Bioreactors, denitrification walls, and stacked practices}

Woodchip-based denitrification is well supported. Denitrification walls
reduced flow-weighted nitrate concentrations by at least $55\%$ and cut
average annual nitrate mass loss $55\%$ over five years with no yield
penalty and no land taken out of production \parencite{Jaynes2008}, and a
19-year comparison found a rye cover crop and a woodchip denitrification
wall reduced N leaching $58$--$59\%$, with rye more effective in dry years
and the wall more effective in wet years \parencite{Rogovska2023}. Trench
backfill additives reduced tile nitrate by $52$--$78\%$ (woodchips best),
and among in-ditch filter media Polonite ranked highest for P removal
\parencite{Povilaitis2018}. A stacked DWM--woodchip bioreactor--saturated
riparian buffer system reduced nitrate-N export $78$--$88\%$ over five
seasons, sustaining performance even in the final year, though the system
could be a net source of suspended solids \parencite{Singh2024b}. Saturated
buffers were highlighted as offering the largest climate-mitigation
potential per unit area among conservation drainage practices, decreasing
nitrate leaching with little increase in N$_2$O and accruing SOC
\parencite{Hall2025}.

\subsubsection{Ditches, weirs, two-stage ditches, and riparian buffers}

In-channel and edge practices provide additional nitrate and P
interception. A ditch with a low-grade weir reduced discharge $51\%$,
nitrate concentration $22\%$, and nitrate load $58\%$ over seven years in a
cold climate without removing land from production \parencite{Strock2025b}.
A two-stage ditch significantly reduced TP, SRP, and TSS concentrations and
loads (TP load $-40\%$), and lowered nitrate concentrations, though not
nitrate loads \parencite{Hodaj2017}; two-stage ditches and wetlands are
regarded as promising beyond-the-field practices capturing both N and P
\parencite{Kalcic2018}. Riparian buffers are highly effective for nitrate:
in North Carolina, groundwater nitrate dropped from $7$--$8$~mg~L$^{-1}$ to
$<0.1$~mg~L$^{-1}$ within $16$--$47$~m of the drainageway, attributed to
denitrification rather than plant assimilation, and buffers $<16$~m
induced significant nitrate loss \parencite{JACOBS1985}. Iowa grass
waterways and drainageway alluvial soils reduced groundwater nitrate $70\%$
relative to cropped fields and removed $144$~kg~yr$^{-1}$
($7.8$~kg~ha$^{-1}$) of inflow nitrate \parencite{Schilling2022}. However,
where tile drains and ditches route water directly to streams they can
bypass the riparian zone, limiting attenuation \parencite{Puckett2005}.
Vegetated filter strips reduced surface-runoff nitrate, PO$_4$, and TP (up
to $70$--$75\%$ for P), but subsurface nitrate could increase through the
strip \parencite{Bhattarai2009}; regionally, filter strips were the most
effective practice for surface sediment, TN ($-57\%$), and TP ($-63\%$)
\parencite{Yuan2022}. Improved subsurface-drainage filter materials
(sand--gravel, zeolite) enhanced ammonium and phosphate removal and salt
discharge, though some configurations increased nitrate and TN
\parencite{Tao2019b,Tao2022,Tao2025b,Wang2024e}.

\subsubsection{Comparative effectiveness and cost}

Comparative syntheses provide load-reduction benchmarks. Denitrifying
bioreactors, constructed wetlands, cover crops, crop rotation, and nutrient
management each reduced subsurface nitrate loads by $23$--$40\%$
\parencite{Yuan2022}. In the Lower Mississippi Alluvial Valley, nine BMPs
delivered nutrient reductions of $15$--$100\%$, with mean TN reductions
highest for conservation tillage ($76\%$) and land-use conversion, and
subsurface drainage itself yielding TN and TP reductions of $17$ and $31\%$
\parencite{Kröger2012}. Cost-effectiveness analysis ranked wetlands most
cost-effective over both 10- and 50-year horizons ($2.04$--$2.91$~\$~kg$^{-1}$~N),
followed by two-stage ditches and cover crops, with USDA payments reducing
landowner costs $76$--$90\%$ \parencite{Roley2016}; wetland N-removal costs
across studies ranged $0.66$--$58$~\$~kg$^{-1}$~N \parencite{Messer2021}.
Farmer-facing evaluation found nutrient management most compatible and
wetlands richest in ecosystem services but least compatible, with cover
crops moderate and positive \parencite{Christianson2014}.

\subsection{Salinity, Trace Elements, Pesticides, and Contaminant Dynamics}

Beyond N and P, drainage governs salt, trace-element, and pesticide
transport. Reducing applied irrigation and the leaching fraction can cut
salt discharge by $50\%$ or more, and San Luis Drain data showed salt load
proportional to discharge at $9.2$~kg~m$^{-3}$; drainage-water reuse for
salt-tolerant crops and evaporation basins are practical pathways, though
basins accumulate extreme salinity ($4$--$390$~g~L$^{-1}$) and trace
elements \parencite{WESTCOT1988}. Selenium in San Joaquin drainage
motivated evaporation-pond hazard management, where only four of twenty
methods (removing levee vegetation and windbreaks, deepening ponds, hazing
birds) were recommended, and no single method could eliminate the hazard
\parencite{BRADFORD1991}; open-water evaporation is prohibited in
California owing to selenium, requiring rock-media systems
\parencite{Ayars2014}. Egyptian forecasts indicated pumped-groundwater TDS
would remain below $800$~ppm over 100 years with no crop water-quality
problems \parencite{RADSTAKE1988}, and Pakistani pipe-drainage salinity
showed essentially no temporal trend owing to lateral groundwater inflow
\parencite{Kelleners1998}. Israeli subsurface and bio-drainage systems
lowered water tables below $2$--$3$~m and improved the salinity--sodicity
regime on over $95\%$ of $3000$~ha \parencite{Gafni2001}. Wrapped
subsurface pipes with sand--gravel filters achieved salt-removal rates
above $95\%$ \parencite{Wang2024e}, and isotopic/hydrochemical analysis
distinguished irrigation, phreatic, and drainage water sources of salt in
saline-alkali land \parencite{Zhou2023}. Idaho irrigation return flow
carried elevated salts and $10$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ nitrate-N owing to
tunnel/tile flow, but concentrations declined significantly over
$2006$--$2018$ \parencite{Bjorneberg2020}.

Drainage of acid sulphate soils is a distinct hazard: subsurface drainage
was a major cause of stream pH declines to near or below $4.0$ (and $3.0$
in ditches), elevating Al and other metals, with controlled pipe drainage
recommended over in-stream liming \parencite{Osterholm2005,Åström2005}.
Pesticides appear in drainage within a month of application as a general
rule, with losses tied to application quantity and compound properties;
highest concentrations occur at plot scale and dilute downstream
\parencite{Tournebize2012}. Constructed wetlands and small grass-strip
wetlands remove pesticides with efficiency governed by sorption ($K_{oc}$),
degradation ($DT_{50}$), and residence time, sometimes with negative
efficiencies \parencite{Tournebize2017,Vallée2015}. Persistent degradates
such as metolachlor ESA remained in groundwater up to $18$~years but did
not migrate far, and no detections exceeded MCLs \parencite{Puckett2005};
drainage increased nitrate but decreased atrazine relative to runoff-only
conditions \parencite{SPALING1995}. Isotopic and geochemical tracers
resolve sources and pathways: combined stable isotopes and fecal markers
showed nitrate sources vary with hydrological stage and that soils play a
determinant role in nitrate formation and transfer \parencite{Briand2017},
and multi-tracer analysis distinguished shallow chloride- and nitrate-rich
groundwater from regional carbonate-aquifer water in the Wabash headwaters
\parencite{Frisbee2022}. Direct effluent bypass through mole-pipe drains
contributed disproportionately to pollutant loads (\emph{c.} $81\%$ of
\emph{E. coli}, $72\%$ of ammonium-N, $55\%$ of P) despite averaging only
$\sim0.7\%$ of applied effluent, motivating storage and slower application
\parencite{Monaghan2004,Monaghan2007}.

\subsection{Watershed-Scale Assessment, Modelling, and Reduction Strategies}

\subsubsection{Models and monitoring}

Process models are well validated for drainage hydrology and N. DRAINMOD
and its derivatives, RZWQM2, RZ-SHAW, and SWAT reproduce drainage flow,
water-table dynamics, and nitrate export across climates
\parencite{Youssef2018,Abdelbaki2025,Craft2018,Jiang2020,Tian2012,Mehan2019}.
LEACHM predicted nitrate leaching better under controlled
drainage/subirrigation than free drainage \parencite{Ng2000}, and
DRAINMOD-FOREST captured drainage and nitrate in managed pine plantations
\parencite{Tian2012}. Monitoring design strongly affects inferred loads:
high-frequency ($15$-min) sampling captured concentration ranges, diurnal
variation, and storm dynamics missed by grab sampling, which overestimated
loads \parencite{Drake2018}. Spatially distributed, event-based, and
year-round sampling is needed to resolve subwatershed heterogeneity and hot
spots \parencite{Irvine2019,Husk2024}, and long-term records are essential
because significant nitrate reductions are far more detectable in
flow-weighted concentration than in load or yield \parencite{Danalatos2022,Ghane2016b}.
Total-N concentrations decrease as spatial scale increases, implying
scale-specific water-quality criteria \parencite{Lagzdins2012}.

\subsubsection{Achieving reduction targets and residual challenges}

Watershed-planning studies show that meeting large reduction goals requires
combining in-field and edge-of-field practices. In the Mississippi basin,
nitrogen-management practices alone were insufficient (improved fertilizer
management and cover crops together cut delivered loads $30.2\%$), but a
full suite of N-management plus N-removal practices reached the $45\%$
target while converting under $6\%$, and as little as $2.5\%$, of cropland
when practices were combined and targeted \parencite{McLellan2015}.
High-resolution LiDAR-based planning identified scenarios achieving
$34$--$39\%$ nitrate reduction while removing $<5\%$ of cropland, with cover
crops and nutrient-removal wetlands common to the best scenarios
\parencite{Tomer2015}. Long-term simulation suggested DWM could plausibly
help meet Iowa's $41\%$ reduction goal \parencite{Craft2018}, and a Czech
pilot designed measures reducing drainage nitrate load $24\%$ across $38\%$
of the drained area \parencite{Kvítek2023}. Yet watershed-scale evidence
also cautions that traditional surface-water BMPs may be inadequate over
tile drainage: seven years of increased strip-till, grassed waterways, and
stream buffers produced no measurable change in nitrate, phosphorus,
sediment, or hydrology, because they did not intercept subsurface flow,
which must be a design priority \parencite{Lemke2011}. Ecosystem-service
framing shows drainage modernization can sharply reduce migratory-bird
habitat (up to $87\%$ for dabbling ducks) while paired water-quality
wetlands substantially reduce nitrate export and partially offset habitat
loss, benefiting diving ducks most \parencite{Mitchell2022,Mitchell2025}.
Managed and semi-natural ditch networks themselves provide biodiversity,
water-purification, and nutrient-retention functions that depend on
vegetation, morphology, and maintenance, with no single maintenance regime
optimizing all services \parencite{Dollinger2015,Herzon2008}. Field
observations that subsurface drainage installation did not measurably alter
soil-health metrics or soil organic carbon over two years, against strong
seasonal and interannual variation, further temper expectations of rapid
in-soil change \parencite{Sherbine2023,Huang2023}.

### Material: Soil Macroporosity and Drainage-Induced Structure

\subsection{Overview}

Subsurface (tile) drainage is not a hydraulically passive intervention: by lowering
and stabilising the water table it reorganises the soil pore system, its aeration
status, and the pathways along which water and solutes move to the drain. The
evidence assembled here establishes several well-supported propositions. First,
rapid delivery of water and surface-applied solutes to tile drains is governed by a
small volume of continuous macropores---principally earthworm burrows, shrinkage
cracks, root channels, and old installation scars---rather than by uniform matrix
flow \parencite{Shipitalo2004,Stamm2002,Villholth1998,Zehe2001,Nielsen2015,Petersen2012}.
Second, these flow-active pathways and the broader pore network vary systematically
both with depth and, more weakly, with lateral position relative to the drain line,
so that the bulk soil directly above a drain is often more conductive and more
biologically active than the inter-drain zone \parencite{Shipitalo2004,Alakukku2010,Nielsen2015,Petersen2012,Montagne2009}.
Third, sustained drainage measurably alters static structural properties---reducing
bulk density, increasing air-filled porosity and gas diffusivity, and improving
saturated hydraulic conductivity---although the magnitude of these changes is modest
and frequently smaller than that of tillage or of the passage of time since
installation \parencite{HUNDAL1976,Nakajima2014,LAL1993,Frankl2023,Caron2025}.
Fourth, mechanical structural interventions coupled to drainage---mole drainage and
subsoiling---create large, effective, but time-limited pore networks that dominate
flow in heavy clays \parencite{MULLER1988,HARRIS1993,PLAMENAC1988,JHA1995,Castanheira2010}.
Finally, over decades drainage accelerates pedogenic processes---eluviation, clay
translocation, and iron/manganese redistribution---leaving a spatially heterogeneous,
partly irreversible imprint on soil morphology \parencite{Montagne2008,Montagne2010,Montagne2013,Mercier2000,YliHalla2009}.
The lines of evidence range from field tracer and dye/smoke experiments through soil
physical sampling and micromorphology to numerical dual-domain modelling, and they
converge on a consistent mechanistic picture in which a structured, connectivity-limited
macropore system is the central control on drained-soil behaviour.

\subsection{Macropores and preferential-flow pathways to drains}

\subsubsection{Biopores as the dominant conduits}

Across contrasting soils and climates, the pathways that connect the soil surface to
buried drains are overwhelmingly biological in origin. In a Finnish clay, dye applied
to cracks moved down to the base of the plough layer and then travelled laterally
until it encountered open earthworm burrows, which acted as the only conduits to tile
depth; at 10 cm above the tile, movement to the drain occurred exclusively through
earthworm burrows, of which 106 dyed burrows m$^{-2}$ were counted, and the base of
the plough layer functioned as a hydraulic barrier \parencite{Shipitalo2004}. Mean
infiltration in the bulk soil above the tile lines was about twice that between the
lines and correlated positively with \textit{Lumbricus terrestris} numbers and
biomass; individual burrow infiltration rates were highly variable (6--1043 mL
min$^{-1}$, mean 358 mL min$^{-1}$ with the plough layer removed) and consistently
higher with the plough layer intact (mean 1080 mL min$^{-1}$)
\parencite{Shipitalo2004}. In a Swiss loam, stained structures were concentrated in
the upper profile, earthworm burrows were the most abundant stained features, and
worm burrows were the only structures present with sizable frequency at the depth of
the tile drains \parencite{Stamm2002}. At a German site, roughly 50 worm burrows
m$^{-2}$ reached continuously from the surface to 90 cm depth, where the gravel
embedding of the drains began, and the abundance of such continuous pathways was
identified as the most important (necessary but not sufficient) precondition for
preferential flow of the sorptive herbicide isoproturon \parencite{Zehe2001}.

\subsubsection{Direct visualisation of surface-to-drain connectivity}

Smoke-injection and dye studies provide direct, quantitative confirmation that
continuous air-filled macropores link drains to the surface, and that these openings
are spatially clustered above the drain line. In Denmark, smoke plumes emerged from
earthworm burrows at the surface, and all but one smoke-emitting macropore (SEM) lay
within a 1-m band centred over the drain; strong SEMs marked entrances to more
efficient pathways (4 of 7 strong SEMs traced to the drain versus 1 of 6 weak SEMs,
and 5 of 13 dye applications reached the tile line), and strong SEMs also showed the
highest water-infiltration rates \parencite{Nielsen2015}. A companion study found
smoke fans from open macropores on all observation dates---evidence of continuous
macroporous pathways from the drains to the surface---with the number of SEMs
increasing markedly as the water table dropped from about drain depth to 20--50 cm
below it; reproducible SEM distributions were obtained at air pressures of 0.38--0.93
kPa, most SEMs occurred within a 1.0-m-wide band centred above the drains, and their
distribution was significantly non-random along the drain sections
\parencite{Petersen2012}.

\subsubsection{Quantifying active macroporosity and its size structure}

The pore volume that is hydraulically active is small. In a Danish glacial-till soil,
field analysis of a chloride breakthrough indicated an active macroporosity of only
about 0.17\% of total soil volume, or roughly 0.5\% of total porosity, yet this
minute fraction produced abrupt drainage-concentration increases by factors of 20 and
400 within hours of surface application \parencite{Villholth1998}. Image analysis of
macropore area fraction (MAF) in a Canadian soil showed maximum depth-averaged total
MAF of only 0.0036--0.0087, with the minimum (\textless 0.001) occurring at tile
depth; macropores 0.5--5 mm were most abundant near the surface, 5--10 mm pores
peaked near the top of the B horizon, and where macropores existed at tile depth they
were mostly 0.5--5 mm \parencite{Frey2011}. Fractures contributed only a small share
of total macroporosity (maximum roughly 15\%, 2\% and 7\% at three plots), and
dye staining revealed that \textgreater 20-year-old tile-installation scars were
still channelling infiltrate to the drains, while at one plot macropores appeared to
terminate at a textural boundary above the tile \parencite{Frey2011}. Surface soil
and B-horizon saturated conductivities correlated strongly with MAF ($r = 0.79$ and
$r \geq 0.83$), tying the visible pore network to measured flow capacity
\parencite{Frey2011}.

\subsection{Spatial organisation of pores: depth and distance from the drain}

Two organising gradients recur. Macroporosity and biopore abundance decline sharply
with depth, whereas lateral variation relative to the drain line is real but
generally weaker. In a Finnish clay, macroporosity decreased strongly with depth but
did not differ substantially with distance from the drain; the number and area of
earthworm burrows likewise fell with depth, and root-channel numbers showed a depth
effect with little distance effect \parencite{Alakukku2010}. Nonetheless a lateral
signature was detectable: earthworm burrows were 44\% more numerous above a drain
than at 6--7 m away (an 89 burrows m$^{-2}$ difference, $P = 0.04$), and field
saturated hydraulic conductivity was 31\% lower at 2 m from the drain than on or at
6--7 m from it, while laboratory $K_{\mathrm{sat}}$ declined rapidly with depth but
did not vary clearly with drain position \parencite{Alakukku2010}. At that site slope
explained more variation than distance to the drain, as soil properties and earthworm
abundance increased downslope \parencite{Alakukku2010}. A Canadian study similarly
found no significant effect of tile position on MAF distribution, with the caveat
that one plot may have been biased by wheel traffic \parencite{Frey2011}. Reviews of
drained soils nonetheless report higher infiltration rates and lower bulk density
above drain lines than at mid-drain positions, together with a runoff reduction of
34--55\% relative to comparable undrained soils \parencite{Montagne2009}. The
smoke-injection evidence that flow-active openings cluster within a roughly 1-m band
above drains reinforces the view that drainage induces lateral heterogeneity in
near-surface connectivity even when the static pore volume is laterally uniform
\parencite{Nielsen2015,Petersen2012}.

\subsection{Static structure: bulk density, porosity, aeration, and gas diffusion}

Sustained artificial drainage consistently lowers bulk density and raises air-filled
porosity and gaseous diffusivity, improving aeration. In Ohio, subsurface drainage
produced greater hydraulic conductivity, lower unconfined compressive strength, and
lower surface-crust resistance than undrained treatments, and it decreased bulk
density and increased the volume of air-filled pores at 0.02--1.0 bar suction,
although these pore-volume effects were of smaller magnitude \parencite{HUNDAL1976}.
Drainage significantly reduced bulk density in the 0--10 and 10--20 cm layers (with
total porosity showing the opposite pattern), raised relative gas diffusion
($D_p/D_0$) and air content---especially at 0--10 cm and more so under no-till---and
these gas-diffusion gains were positively correlated with corn grain and stover yield
\parencite{Nakajima2014}. Consistent with an aeration mechanism, artificial drainage
in Georgia increased soil oxygen concentrations and lowered soil moisture, raising net
mineralisation and, more strongly, net nitrification, while denitrification potential
was greater in the wetter undrained plots \parencite{GROFFMAN1984}. Across a large
Canadian survey, low relative gas diffusivity was pervasive (over 93\% of corn fields
below the 0.03 threshold at both 15 and 30 cm), and yield correlated positively with
log$_{10}$ relative gas diffusivity and with saturated hydraulic conductivity at 15
cm; boundary-line analysis suggested maximum yield at the highest measured $D_s/D_o$
(0.16) and a major yield drop below $D_s/D_o$ of 0.0001, underscoring that dynamic
aeration indicators outperform static bulk density in explaining productivity
\parencite{Caron2025}.

Comparisons with undrained or subirrigated soils, however, show the direction of the
bulk-density effect is context-dependent. In one multi-site experiment undrained
plots had lower bulk density, higher aggregate mean weight diameter (MWD), lower
penetration resistance, and greater moisture retention than drained plots, with
overall mean bulk density essentially identical (1.44 versus 1.43 Mg m$^{-3}$) and no
significant drainage effect; the authors attributed lower undrained yields to
transient anaerobiosis rather than to permanent physical damage
\parencite{LAL1993}. In a long-term water-table-management experiment, subirrigated
soils had \textit{lower} bulk density and \textit{greater} total porosity than
continuously drained soils but also lower aggregate stability, smaller MWD and
geometric mean diameter, fewer macroaggregates, and about 11\% greater available
water capacity, while the drainage treatment showed greater penetration resistance,
notably at 30--45 cm \parencite{Baker2004}. Drainage-induced compaction of the surface
was also implicated in reduced organic-matter status: an undrained area retained
higher soil organic matter than the drained area, with drainage reducing organic
matter in the top layers and lowering soil pH overall \parencite{FAUSEY1992}, and
non-drainage increased soil-organic-carbon stock relative to tile drainage by
18--27\% across depths in a related Ohio study \parencite{Kumar2014}. The upper bound
on drainage-based aeration control is set by compaction: in the most compacted soils
all pores remain saturated up to $P = -100$ cm, so aeration control by drainage
becomes impossible and excess-water removal depends almost entirely on evaporative
drying, while plough layers impede downward flow, build perched water tables, and
prevent installed systems from exercising their beneficial effect
\parencite{SMEDEMA1993}.

\subsection{Infiltration, hydraulic conductivity, and structural evolution over time}

Drainage and associated loosening raise infiltration capacity and saturated hydraulic
conductivity, and---importantly---these gains often \emph{develop over years} as
biological and structural processes act on the drier profile. Moling in a Thai acid
sulphate clay increased the average basic infiltration rate from 3.9 $\pm$ 0.3 to
10.5 $\pm$ 1.4 mm h$^{-1}$, decreased bulk density and increased porosity (most
markedly at 25 and 40 cm), and raised air permeability and saturated hydraulic
conductivity, particularly below the plough layer \parencite{JHA1995}. A drainage-age
comparison in Minnesota showed that fields drained more than 15 years earlier had
saturated hydraulic conductivity 7.58 cm h$^{-1}$ faster than fields drained within 5
years ($P < 0.05$), with no difference in bulk density or aggregate stability but
higher biological indicators in the older fields; the authors proposed
preferential-flow development as the likely driver of the conductivity increase and
concluded that soil physical and biological properties evolve over time after
installation \parencite{Frankl2023}. Long-term Iranian paddy trials likewise found
that saturated hydraulic conductivity increased over four years in all layers (largest
in the 0--30 cm layer), with drain discharge and water-table depth increasing over
time, and that shallower drainage improved conductivity more than deeper drainage
\parencite{JafariTalukolaee2016,Talukolaee2018}. Drainable porosity varied seasonally,
being lower during rice than canola seasons \parencite{Talukolaee2018}. Tillage
interacts strongly with these hydraulic changes: a column study showed that after
tillage the tilled column drained earlier (by 11 h) and discharged more (55.2 versus
38.8 mm) than an untilled column, a difference reproduced by HYDRUS only when
tillage effects on hydraulic properties were included \parencite{Moroizumi2004}, and
shallow-tilled sandy soil yielded about twice the transient drainage of deep-tilled
soil, with correspondingly larger solute losses \parencite{MANSELL1980}. Amending the
trench backfill can also govern infiltration: lime-mixed backfill gave a mean
post-steady-flow infiltration rate of 3.2 m d$^{-1}$ versus 2.2 m d$^{-1}$ in control
backfill and 0.83 m d$^{-1}$ between drains, stabilising clay structure and reducing
the strong decline of the drainage coefficient with rising water content
\parencite{Saulys2006}.

\subsection{Mole drainage and subsoiling: engineered structure and its durability}

Where matrix permeability is inadequate, mechanical fissuring by mole drainage and
subsoiling creates the effective pore network. On UK heavy clays, flow through the
system was dominated by mole drains, with up to 90\% of flow occurring through them;
effective drainage deepened the winter water table (drained plots averaged 216 mm
deeper than undrained over 1980--88) and reduced flood risk by 16\% for a 10-year
event, while direct drilling improved subsoil structure enough to extend mole-drainage
life by at least 25\% but also increased surface compaction, runoff, and peak flow
\parencite{HARRIS1993}. On a very deep heavy clay in the former Yugoslavia, subsoiling
and mole drainage raised the soil drainage porosity by up to 20\% in all treated
variants, lowered the critical-period water table to 50 cm within 3 days at 20--30 m
spacing, and---together with permeable trench backfill---eliminated waterlogging
within 2 days; gravel-backfilled trenches retained favourable hydraulic conductivity
and bulk density after 5 years whereas unbackfilled trenches compacted quickly
\parencite{PLAMENAC1988}. The durability of moled structure is finite but
substantial: German long-term trials found mole channels formed above the water table
with an effective top depth $\geq 0.55$ m persisted as open cavities for 3--7 years
and thereafter as zones of increased horizontal permeability, with improved
permeability in disruption zones continuing for more than 54 months, although after 20
years backfilled soil tended to be less permeable than adjacent undisturbed soil
\parencite{MULLER1988}. Combined pipe-plus-mole systems modelled in Portugal drained
1333 m$^3$ ha$^{-1}$ (133 mm) with mole drains versus 1029 m$^3$ ha$^{-1}$ without---a
22\% loss of drainage capacity when moles were absent---and lowered the peak water
table by about 0.1 m \parencite{Castanheira2010}. Mole drainage in Australia increased
gas-filled porosity above the channels (though still below the optimum for roots),
while gypsum-enriched slots further raised slot porosity and improved mole-channel
stability under increased flow \parencite{Muirhead1996}. Deep ripping to 0.45 m with
gypsum, with or without drainage, lowered subsoil bulk density and strength, produced
smaller aggregates, and raised subsoil hydraulic conductivity and air-filled porosity,
with improvements persisting through the two-year study \parencite{Greenwood2006}.
Rice-husk drainage mole systems in Korean paddy soils similarly lowered soil moisture
and raised permeability and aeration, the 2-m interval giving the greatest improvement
and the most uniform moisture distribution \parencite{Jung2024}.

\subsection{Aggregate stability, soil strength, crusting, and trafficability}

Drainage modifies the mechanical behaviour of the surface and subsoil, generally
reducing crusting and improving trafficability while producing mixed effects on
aggregation. Undrained and ponded plots developed strong crusting and adverse
structure, and the undrained treatment had the highest crust density and resistance
and the largest, thickest crust units; crust thickness increased in the order combined
tile-and-surface \textless\ tile \textless\ surface \textless\ undrained
\parencite{HUNDAL1976,FAUSEY1969}. Surface-drained plots required greater penetration
force than plots with subsurface or combined drainage, and combined drainage produced
deeper, more uniformly distributed soybean roots and a significantly longer taproot
\parencite{FAUSEY1969}. Soil strength is tightly coupled to matric suction: in an
English clay, strength at 7.5--22.5 cm depth was linearly related to matric suction
over $-3$ to $-20$ cm water, and a 10-cm rise in suction was on average as effective
at reducing rutting as a 670-kg wheel-load reduction; in ploughed soil, subsurface
drainage reduced tractor-traffic damage, decreasing wheel sinkage and compaction, and
the authors recommended draining clay soils to below 50--60 cm to minimise structural
damage \parencite{STEINHARDT1974}. Trafficability effects were quantified directly in
sodic North Dakota soils: tile drainage lowered drawbar power relative to no drainage
when no amendments were applied, and drainage combined with the lower gypsum rate
produced penetration-resistance results similar to the higher gypsum rate, showing
that drainage can substitute for part of the amendment requirement
\parencite{Wamono2016,Wamono2016b}. Aggregation responses depend on the comparison:
the drainage treatment showed larger MWD and more macroaggregates than subirrigation
\parencite{Baker2004}, no-till plus drainage increased aggregate stability and MWD at
the surface \parencite{Kumar2014}, yet drainage reduced MWD and infiltration relative
to non-drainage in an Iranian trial \parencite{Shabanpour2022}, and drainage-water
recycling raised penetration resistance (1292 kPa) above drainage-only (1197 kPa) and
non-drained (1084 kPa) controls while lowering organic matter and altering texture at
depth \parencite{Kaur2023}. Elevated soil temperature is a further structural benefit:
subsurface drainage significantly warmed soils, with the largest May--July increases
at 30--60 cm and larger spring warming under narrower spacing
\parencite{Jin2008}, consistent with the reported 2--3$^{\circ}$C spring warming and
roughly 10-day-earlier sowing achieved by subsoiling/moling with drainage
\parencite{PLAMENAC1988}.

\subsection{Drainage-induced pedogenesis and long-term structural imprint}

Over years to decades, the redirected water fluxes of a drained profile accelerate
pedological processes and leave a lasting, spatially structured imprint. In a French
Albeluvisol studied by space-for-time substitution, subsurface drainage intensified
eluviation in the E\&Bt and upper Bt horizons, producing lower contents of the
\textless 2 $\mu$m fraction and Fe and higher quartz near the drain, with changes at
60 cm from the drain of the same nature as long-term differentiation but stronger than
those at 110 cm \parencite{Montagne2010}. Drainage removed swelling clay minerals and
more than one-third of the Fe oxides near the drain, increased precipitation of Mn
oxides and Mn-rich ferrihydrite with decreasing distance to the drain, and shifted the
dominant Mn form from Mn-rich ferrihydrite to Mn oxides after 16 years, with
eluviation most active shortly after installation \parencite{Montagne2008}. The
spatial pattern is process-specific and asymmetric: eluviation was largely restricted
to the first metre either side of the drain, whereas redox-driven Mn redistribution
and black-soil-volume formation extended over several metres; total lutum losses
ranged from about 18\% of the pre-degradation stock at 400 cm to more than 50\% at 60
cm, and the changes were interpreted as a divergent, initial-condition-sensitive
evolution consistent with deterministic chaos
\parencite{Montagne2013,Montagne2009}. The exported material is dominated by fine
colloids: the 0.45 $\mu$m--5000 Da fraction accounted for more than 80\% of the
suspended load, 60\% of it finer than 200 nm, and consisted mainly of 2:1
phyllosilicates with abundant small iron particles of superficial (A-horizon) origin,
so that drainage exports fine particles definitively from the profile with little
accumulation in the B horizon \parencite{Mercier2000}. These accelerated fluxes can
compress pedogenic timescales: the authors argue drainage-induced evolution over 16
years is comparable in magnitude to processes expected over much longer periods
\parencite{Montagne2010,Montagne2008}. In boreal Finland, about 70 years of improved
ditch and tile drainage was sufficient to form an argic/argillic horizon---with
illuvial clay coats maxima coinciding with the effective drainage depths---yielding
the first documented Luvisol/Cryaqualf in a boreal clay and a change judged probably
partly irreversible \parencite{YliHalla2009}.

\subsection{Macropore-controlled transport and its representation in models}

The structural findings above manifest hydrologically as rapid, connectivity-limited
preferential transport, and reproducing tile behaviour requires models that treat this
explicitly. Conservative and weakly sorbing tracers reach drains within minutes to
hours of surface application: chloride breakthrough increased abruptly by factors of
20--400 within hours, with the initial peak representing only 0.1--0.3\% of applied
mass \parencite{Villholth1998}; bromide appeared in tile effluent within 1 h and a
rainfall intensity near 2 mm h$^{-1}$ was the threshold above which concentrations
rose, yet cumulative capture was slow overall---8\% in 48 h, 27\% in 21 days, and
about 95\% only after roughly seven months \parencite{Frey2012}. Similarly, bromide
arrived at the drain as soon as drainage began even when offset from the trench, but
the bulk of transport was slow (10\% of load in 3--5 months, 50\% in 10--18 months,
90\% in 18--64 months), with travel time set by drain spacing and relative distance
from the drain \parencite{Kladivko2023}. Liquid-manure constituents (dissolved
reactive P, NH$_4^+$, Cl$^-$) reached the drain within 5 min of surface application,
and column experiments confirmed that soluble P moves through artificial macropores
with little sorption while matrix-flow columns strongly retain it---identifying
macropore flow as the primary mechanism for rapid P delivery and showing that ploughing
manure in disrupts macropores and promotes retentive matrix flow
\parencite{Stamm2002,Geohring2001}. Controlled column and laboratory experiments with
buried and surface-connected macropores quantified the geometry of these effects:
surface-connected macropores produced much faster drain response than buried ones (a
75-cm surface-connected macropore raised steady-state infiltration about fivefold),
macropore--matrix exchange was largely restricted to the profile near the drain, and
a linear total-flow-ratio relation implied an effective contributing distance of only
20--25 cm for directly connected macropores \parencite{Akay2007,Akay2008}.

These behaviours are captured only by models that represent two-domain flow, a
tile-induced two-dimensional flow field, or a direct macropore--drain connection. A
two-dimensional mobile--immobile model matched bromide breakthrough, level, and
short-term fluctuation far better than one-dimensional or single-porosity
formulations, with over 60\% of applied bromide initially transferred to immobile
regions and retained through the drainage season, indicating that both the 2-D
tile-flow field and strong mobile--immobile mass transfer control transport
\parencite{Köhne2006}. Including the macropore flow domain was essential to match
observed drainage chloride, and a one-domain simulation underpredicted tracer loss
(10\% versus the observed 21\%), while a time-variable macropore-spacing parameter
improved the chemograph fit \parencite{Villholth1998b}. In RZWQM, adding an
``express fraction'' of about 2\% reproduced the immediate breakthrough of conservative
tracer and pesticide that the unmodified model missed, improving early peaks at
multiple drained sites, though recession-limb concentrations remained poorly matched,
implying the modification only partially accounts for macropore--drain connectivity
\parencite{Fox2004,Fox2007b}. Activating a macropore component improved hourly drainage
peak amplitude but did not improve total event drainage, and predicted macropore flow
was relatively insensitive to macroporosity and pore radius \parencite{Li2023d}. A
macropore subroutine in APEX improved tile and total discharge calibration and raised
dissolved-reactive-P load performance from unsatisfactory to very good, with simulated
median annual macropore-flow and macropore-P-load contributions of 48\% and 43\%,
concentrated in winter and spring \parencite{Ford2017}. At watershed scale, tile
drainage and macropore flow were sensitive to a macropore-connectivity factor, with
fine-textured soils generating more macropore flow than coarse-textured soils, so that
soil texture explained spatial and connectivity-driven seasonal variation in flow
partitioning \parencite{Poon2021}. Biopore configuration further modulates leaching:
drain-connected biopores produced fast bromide leaching and high mass fluxes, whereas
matrix-terminating biopores reduced maximum leaching by routing some solute into the
soil \parencite{Holbak2021}. Field-scale modelling also confirms that layer-specific
macropore volumes and trench-backfill conductivity must be represented to reproduce
groundwater levels and weakly preferential tracer dynamics
\parencite{AURA1995,Bechtold2007}. Together these transport and modelling results
close the loop with the structural evidence: the small, depth-declining, drain-focused
macropore network documented morphologically is the same network that governs the
speed, magnitude, and management sensitivity of solute delivery to tile drains.

### Material: Phosphorus Loss Pathways and Soil Controls

\subsection{Overview}

Phosphorus (P) leaves agricultural fields along two hydrologically distinct
pathways---surface runoff and subsurface (tile) drainage---and the environmental
significance of each depends on how P is partitioned between them, on the balance
of dissolved and particulate fractions within each pathway, and on the soil
properties that govern P release and retention. The evidence assembled here
establishes several consolidated conclusions. First, subsurface drainage is not
the ``clean'' pathway it was once assumed to be: across humid, cold-temperate,
and tile-intensive landscapes it can dominate edge-of-field water export and
carry a large, sometimes majority, share of both dissolved reactive P (DRP) and
total P (TP) loads \parencite{Pease2018,VanEsbroeck2016,Qi2018,Sadhukhan2019b}.
Second, particulate P (PP) frequently dominates drainage P losses, a pattern that
overturned the older expectation of purely dissolved subsurface transport and is
tied mechanistically to preferential (macropore) flow of fine topsoil particles
to drains \parencite{Uusitalo2001,Lu2003,Laubel1999,Sims1998}. Third, dissolved P
concentrations and losses are strongly and predictably controlled by soil P
status---soil test P (STP), degree of P saturation (DPS), and P-sorption
capacity---so that soils enriched by long-term fertilisation or manuring, together
with sandy and organic soils of low sorption capacity, present the greatest
subsurface P-loss risk \parencite{Sharpley1995,Beauchemin2003,Siemens2004,Sims1998,McDowell2001b}.
Fourth, P export is highly episodic and seasonally concentrated: a small fraction
of the year, dominated by storm events, snowmelt, and the non-growing season,
carries the great majority of annual load
\parencite{King2015,VanEsbroeck2016,Brendel2019,Grant1996}. These lines of
evidence, spanning plot lysimeters, edge-of-field monitoring, watershed sediment
budgets, and process models, are developed in turn below, along with what is known
about soil P pools and speciation, organic and redox-active soils, management and
fertilisation controls, P-index and modelling frameworks, and edge-of-field
retention.

\subsection{Partitioning of Phosphorus Losses Between Surface Runoff and
Subsurface Drainage}

\subsubsection{Water partitioning and the hydrologic dominance of tile flow}

Tile drainage commonly captures the largest share of field water export. In
edge-of-field studies in the Eastern Corn Belt, subsurface discharge accounted for
$81 \pm 23\%$ of annual discharge \parencite{Pease2018}, and in humid
cold-temperate Ontario tile drains contributed 78--90\% of annual water export
with surface runoff supplying only 10--22\% \parencite{VanEsbroeck2016}. Installing
or improving subsurface drainage fundamentally re-routes water: improved drainage
in Finland shifted runoff from being surface-dominated (drainage 10--40\% of total
runoff before) to drainage-dominated (50--90\% after), with more than 80\% of
precipitation water evacuated by tiles and less than 10\% by surface runoff on
ploughed clay immediately after installation \parencite{Turtola1995}. Consistent
with this, subsurface drainage reduced surface runoff by an average of 35\% over a
ten-year study on Lower Mississippi Valley alluvial soil \parencite{BENGTSON1995},
and tile-drained plots in New York generated much greater cumulative runoff overall
but lower \emph{surface} runoff because most water left via tiles
\parencite{Klaiber2020}. Growing-season tile drainage generally tracked surface
water discharge but reached 80\% of surface water volume in a Massachusetts
cranberry system \parencite{Millar2025}, and in an intensively tiled Illinois
watershed subsurface drainage and surface runoff removed averages of 16.1\% and
2.6\% of rainfall, respectively \parencite{Algoazany2007}.

\subsubsection{Concentrations, loads, and pathway partitioning of P}

A recurring and important distinction is that surface runoff usually carries higher
P \emph{concentrations}, whereas tile drainage---because it carries more
water---can dominate annual \emph{loads}. Flow-weighted DRP and TP concentrations
were significantly greater in surface runoff than in subsurface discharge in Ohio,
yet annual DRP and TP loads were significantly greater from subsurface discharge,
which supplied 71\% of DRP and 69\% of TP load \parencite{Pease2018}. The same
concentration--load contrast appears in long-term Swedish monitoring, where TP
concentrations exceeded those of drainage in all but 3 of 32 years (32-year means
0.27 vs. 0.04~mg~L$^{-1}$) and annual P losses were greater via surface runoff,
while N losses were greater via drainage \parencite{Norberg2022}. In Ontario, tiles
exported 40--77\% of TP load and 19--67\% of DRP load
\parencite{VanEsbroeck2016}; in Wisconsin, tiles contributed 21--52\% of TP and
21--68\% of DRP field losses (concluded as 17--41\% of total P loss)
\parencite{Madison2014}; and a southern-Ontario field lost 65.4\% of annual TP
(2.85 of 4.36~kg~P~ha$^{-1}$) via tiles \parencite{Qi2018}. Regional syntheses put
subsurface drainage at 12--60\% of TP transfer versus 40--88\% for surface erosion
in north-west Europe \parencite{Ulén2007}, and a text-mining review reported tiles
transporting up to 77\% of TP, 67\% of soluble reactive P, and 63\% of watershed
suspended sediment \parencite{Elsayed2023}. Models corroborate the field
partitioning: process simulations attributed 66\% of DRP and 76\% of TP loss to
subsurface drainage against observed values of 70\% and 80\% \parencite{Grewal2025},
and RZWQM2-P reproduced tile drainage as the major DRP pathway with PP split roughly
equally between runoff and drainage \parencite{Sadhukhan2019b,Sadhukhan2019}.

\subsubsection{Settings where surface runoff dominates}

The dominance of tiles is not universal, and the counter-examples define the
conditions under which surface runoff prevails. In snowmelt-affected clay
(vertisolic) landscapes of the northern Great Plains, overland flow contributed
72--89\% of runoff and $>95\%$ of annual SRP and TP losses, because tiles were
decoupled from the surface by frozen ground and carried water only in wetter
periods; SRP and TP were 600\% and 500\% greater in overland flow than tile flow
\parencite{Kokulan2019b,Kokulan2021}. Surface runoff was also the major pathway for
sediment, TP, and soluble P (though not nitrate) under Minnesota tillage systems
\parencite{Zhao2001}, was the dominant P pathway from perennial pastures in Victoria
when runoff occurred \parencite{Melland2008}, and modelling of an Ohio field
attributed 78\% of predicted TP to surface runoff \parencite{Askar2021b}. In flooded
paddy systems, surface runoff exceeded subsurface pathways for P during rice growth,
and shallow subsurface drainage lowered TP loss mainly by reducing flooding
\parencite{DarziNaftchali2016}. Thus pathway partitioning is strongly modulated by
climate (snowmelt vs.\ rain-driven), soil texture, and drainage design rather than
being a fixed property.

\subsection{Dissolved and Particulate Phosphorus Fractions and Bioavailability}

\subsubsection{Particulate P as a major, often dominant, drainage fraction}

A central state-of-knowledge finding is that PP is frequently the dominant P form in
tile drainage, contrary to the earlier presumption that filtration by the soil
profile leaves subsurface water dissolved-dominated. In Finnish clay soils, PP was
the grand-mean 92\% of TP in both surface runoff and drainage, and most PP (93\%)
was nondesorbable \parencite{Uusitalo2001}; across sites, PP comprised 73--94\% of
runoff P \parencite{Uusitalo2003}. At Rothamsted's Broadbalk drains, PP averaged 54\%
of TP (vs.\ 36\% for molybdate-reactive P) and reached 81.1\% on individual plots,
with PP strongly linearly related to particulate matter (PM), which reached
$\sim$0.6~g~L$^{-1}$ \parencite{Lu2003,Lu2004}. PP dominated TP export in both tile
drainage and surface runoff in Ontario (though DRP:TP was higher in surface runoff)
\parencite{VanEsbroeck2016}, made up most of drainage TP in Massachusetts
\parencite{Millar2025}, and dominated Danish catchment losses (55--71\% of TP at
three of four catchments) \parencite{Grant1996}. Catchment monitoring likewise showed
drainage TP dominated by PP (only 39.8\% as DRP) with PP losses exceeding dissolved
losses \parencite{Brendel2019}, and level Dutch grassland lost nutrients dominated by
particulate forms via trenches and pipe drains \parencite{vanderSalm2012}. Over 80\%
of predicted surface and subsurface P losses were particulate in an Ohio simulation
\parencite{Askar2021b}, and pasture P export in New Zealand was $\sim$80\%
particulate-associated \parencite{Gillingham2000}. Even where TP was small, PP
dominated: a Finnish controlled-drainage peat field lost only
0.30~kg~P~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ of TP but 71\% as PP \parencite{Pham2023}.

\subsubsection{Dissolved-dominated systems and DRP importance}

Dissolved forms nonetheless dominate in specific settings, particularly
snowmelt-driven, grassland, high-STP, and organic-soil systems. In north-west
Europe the dissolved fraction spanned 9--93\% of TP \parencite{Ulén2007}. Tile SRP
averaged 60\% of TP (median 69\%) across Michigan sites \parencite{Clement2017}, and
DRP dominated fertilised continuous-grass tile losses (72\% of TP) even as PP
dominated corn and unfertilised systems \parencite{Zhang2015b}. In Scotland,
molybdate-reactive P accounted for only 42\% of subsurface TP loss, with non-MRP
dissolved forms 41\% and PP $\sim$17\%, underscoring the need to measure all P forms
\parencite{Hooda1999}. Organic soils in New Zealand and Ireland produced very high
filterable P loads and high overland-flow DRP concentrations, respectively
\parencite{McDowell2015b,Kurz2005}, and organic soils generally carry less
particulate but potentially higher TP concentrations than mineral soils
\parencite{Grenon2021}. Watershed orthophosphate:TP ratios rose in tile-drained and
baseflow-dominated Iowa systems \parencite{Schilling2017b}. Dissolved organic P
(DOP) is a consistently smaller but non-trivial fraction: DOP was 0.01--0.1~mg~L$^{-1}$
and generally below MRP at Rothamsted \parencite{Lu2003,Lu2004}, whereas the non-MRP
(DOP) fraction reached 44\% of total dissolved P in Scottish grassland
\parencite{Hooda1999}, and DOP leaching increased with peat thickness in Finland
\parencite{YliHalla2022}.

\subsubsection{Bioavailability of transported P}

Because algal availability, not merely total mass, determines eutrophication risk,
several studies quantified bioavailable P. Desorbable PP (PPi) and DRP were of equal
importance to potentially algal-available P in Finnish clays, with PPi accounting for
47\% of anion-exchange-resin P \parencite{Uusitalo2001}. Bioavailable estimates
depended strongly on the extraction: DRP + AER-PP yielded 62--669~g~ha$^{-1}$ across
sites, whereas the more aggressive DRP + BD-PP gave 145--1560~g~ha$^{-1}$, and PP
contributed significantly to bioavailable loss in both pathways, highlighting erosion
control as a water-protection measure \parencite{Uusitalo2003}. Only 8--18\% of TP
export from grassland lysimeters was molybdate-reactive \parencite{Bilotta2008}, and
in New Zealand streams bioavailable and total P in trapped sediment were highest at
downstream sites and were enriched when little sediment was trapped
\parencite{McDowell2004}. Sediment P enrichment declined with increasing sediment
yield (from $\sim$2000--4000 to $\sim$300--1000~mg~P~kg$^{-1}$), so total P yield was
not closely tied to sediment yield \parencite{Gillingham2000}.

\subsection{Preferential Flow, Sediment Mobilisation, and Particulate P Transport}

\subsubsection{Macropore flow as the dominant particulate-transport mechanism}

Preferential flow through cracks, fissures, macropores, and worm channels is the
mechanism reconciling substantial subsurface PP loss with the filtering expected of
matrix flow. It is identified as a major, rapid pathway delivering water and P to
drains \parencite{King2015,Sims1998}, and $^{137}$Cs tracing repeatedly shows that
drainage PM originates from topsoil: median grain sizes of 2--5~$\mu$m indicate fine
topsoil particles translocated to drains within hours of rainfall, creating a direct
link between topsoil and surface waters \parencite{Laubel1999}. Surface-runoff and
subsurface sediments were nearly identical in PPi content and $^{137}$Cs activity,
confirming macropore transport of surface soil particles through the profile, with
enrichment ratios of $\sim$2 for fine particles \parencite{Uusitalo2001}. Substantial
P losses occurred in Scottish grassland despite high subsoil P-sorption capacity,
implying preferential flow bypass \parencite{Hooda1999}, and macropore flow through
shrinkage cracks likely explained much DRP loss through Dutch pipe drains
\parencite{vanderSalm2012}. In vertisolic soils, preferential flow during dry summer
and fall raised tile P concentrations, though low flow limited loads
\parencite{Kokulan2021}. Ridge tillage increased subsurface sediment loss relative to
moldboard plowing, consistent with preferential flow \parencite{Zhao2001}. Field-scale
mapping links preferential transport to physical soil properties: in a Danish field
the clay-rich, high-bulk-density, low-organic-carbon northern third showed fast 5\%
tritium arrival, high tracer recovery (mean 67\%), and elevated particle and PP
leaching, identifying it as a leaching-vulnerable zone \parencite{Norgaard2013}.
First-flush particle concentrations of 62--390~mg~L$^{-1}$ carried PP of
0.07--1.73~mg~L$^{-1}$ before stabilising, with accumulated particle mass correlated
($r=0.66$) to first-flush PP \parencite{Norgaard2013}. The proportion of macropore
flow (MF) is recognised as the greatest uncertainty in representing tile P export in
indices, warranting further quantification across soils \parencite{Reid2012}.

\subsubsection{Event-driven particulate P and drainage as a sediment source}

PP loss is episodic and event-driven. PM, PP, and DP concentrations peaked in initial
drainage flow, and nearly one-third of annual PM and PP loss occurred during rapid
drainage in sampled storms \parencite{Laubel1999}. Storm drainage at a Danish
catchment was strongly enriched in DP and PP, PM peaked before discharge (165~mg~L$^{-1}$
$\approx$ 0.69~mg~P~L$^{-1}$ PP) suggesting exhaustion of fine-particle supply, and PP
was linearly correlated with PM at all catchments \parencite{Grant1996}. Because of
this episodicity, discrete sampling underestimated annual P loss by 26\% to $>$100\%
relative to intensive storm sampling, so reliable estimates require event-based
strategies \parencite{Grant1996}. Turbidity served as a strong suspended-solids proxy
in Swedish drainage \parencite{Ulén2018}, and grassland lysimeters exported up to
14.85~kg suspended solids and 50~g TP per event, with drained land exporting up to
52\% less than undrained land \parencite{Bilotta2008}.

\subsubsection{Catchment sediment budgets and tile drains as sediment sources}

At catchment scale, tile drains are quantitatively major sediment pathways.
Source-fingerprinting attributed 28--55\% of catchment sediment yield to field drains
across UK subcatchments, with eroding channel banks a smaller source (6--12\%) and low
overall sediment delivery ratios (14--27\%) indicating substantial in-catchment
redeposition \parencite{Walling2002}. In monitored UK drains, topsoil supplied more
than two-thirds of the sediment load (70.9--88.6\%), sediment load correlated with
drainage volume rather than rainfall, and drains contributed over 50\% of the annual
sediment budget in one catchment \parencite{Chapman2005}. Sediment yields ranged
70--978~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ with PP yields 0.04--1.1~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$
\parencite{Chapman2005}. Tile-drain sediment flocculates under shear, with maximum
flocculation at 0.169~N~m$^{-2}$ and a critical deposition shear stress of
0.056~N~m$^{-2}$, information relevant to its fate in receiving streams
\parencite{Stone1997}. Clayey soils exported measurable sediment-associated organic
carbon and N (up to 35\% and 20\% of loads) chiefly via subsurface drainage, whereas a
sandy soil eroded negligibly and exported only aqueous forms; discharge and soil
disturbance (recent plowing) controlled export \parencite{Manninen2023}. At larger
scales, non-field sources can dominate: streambanks and ravines supplied 76\% of
sediment at a Minnesota outlet \parencite{Dalzell2018}, and streambed storage exceeded
annual streambank erosion and suspended-sediment load in an Ohio lake-plain tributary,
where streambanks were the largest streambed-sediment source and much sediment had long
residence times \parencite{Williamson2025}.

\subsection{Soil Controls: Soil Test P, Sorption Capacity, and Degree of P Saturation}

\subsubsection{Soil test P and dissolved P release}

Dissolved P loss is tightly coupled to soil P status. Loss of P in surface runoff and
subsurface drainage is strongly related to soil P content
($r^2 = 0.85$--0.96 for surface water, 0.86 for subsurface), and at tile-drain depth
(40--50~cm) NaCl-extractable soil P related to storm drainage loss ($r^2=0.86$)
\parencite{Sharpley1995}. Higher STP was associated with higher DRP in both tiles and
runoff, while soil texture had limited overall influence in a large Ohio edge-of-field
dataset \parencite{Osterholz2020}. Higher pH and STP raised DRP losses, and greater
clay content lowered subsurface DRP losses, in a US--Canada synthesis; rainfall sharply
increased subsurface DRP loss where STP exceeded 41~mg~kg$^{-1}$
\parencite{Ni2020}. A change point in STP marks a threshold above which
CaCl$_2$-extractable P---and DRP in runoff and drainage---rises more steeply per unit
STP \parencite{McDowell2001b}. Drainage DRP was of similar magnitude to CaCl$_2$-P of
lysimeter topsoils, and water and CaCl$_2$ extractions were proposed to estimate runoff
and drainage DRP, respectively \parencite{McDowell2001b}. DRP concentration related to
soil P-AL in Swedish fields \parencite{Ulén2016}, high STP was an important spatial
driver of loss at the plot scale in Illinois \parencite{deOliveira2022}, and STP
strongly governed P desorption risk in flow-through experiments
\parencite{Mumbi2024}. Agronomic soil-P tests (Modified Morgan's P) related
significantly to drainage TDP and DUP across sites, with a steeper DRP relationship for
low-organic-matter cropland; batch extractions released far more soluble P than field
drain waters \parencite{Stutter2018}.

\subsubsection{Sorption capacity and degree of P saturation}

Where soils sorb P weakly, dissolved losses rise. Soils grouped as lower-P-sorbing had
distinctly higher tile-drainage TP and TPP (0.06 and 0.04 vs.\ 0.03 and 0.02~mg~L$^{-1}$)
and consistently exceeded the 0.03~mg~L$^{-1}$ standard; A-horizon degree of soil P
saturation (DSPS$_{M3}$) alone explained 83\% of TP variance, rising to 95\% with
water-extractable P, and grouping by sorption capacity increased predictive power to
96\% \parencite{Beauchemin2003}. In sandy German soils, rapid adsorption controlled
median drainage SRP: equilibrium concentrations and a DPS threshold of 0.25 were good
predictors, DPS exceeded 0.25 in all topsoils, and SRP $>$100~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ was
detected to great depth, showing local P transport and loss \parencite{Siemens2004}.
Across Norwegian profiles just 10~km apart, DPS-AL ranged 1--26\% and DPS-OX 3--37\%; a
whole-profile DPS-AL threshold near 10\% was suggested for TDP leaching, flow-weighted
TDP and DRP were best predicted by topsoil water-soluble P and whole-profile DPS-AL,
and the highest-leaching profiles combined large labile topsoil P pools with
low-sorption subsoils---evidence that subsoil characteristics must be included in
leaching risk assessment \parencite{Liu2024b}. Consistent with weak-sorption risk,
subsurface P losses were often small in fine-textured soils at moderate STP but became
environmentally significant in sandy soils, organic soils, and very high-P soils from
long-term fertilisation or manuring \parencite{Sims1998}. Adding alum to a
poor-P-sorbing soil reduced subsurface filtered reactive and total filtered P losses by
26--27\% at 50~kg~Al~ha$^{-1}$ (estimated cost 190--952~USD~kg$^{-1}$~P), with
mitigation rising with rate but declining over time \parencite{McDowell2015}. Anion
storage capacity was proposed to flag marginal soils with high P-loss potential
\parencite{McDowell2015b}.

\subsection{Soil Phosphorus Pools, Speciation, and Legacy Phosphorus}

Sequential and chemical fractionation link soil P pools to loss. P bioavailability and
loss are controlled by reactions with hydrous oxides and amorphous/crystalline Al, Fe,
and Ca complexes plus organic matter \parencite{Sharpley1995}. Sequentially extracted P
fractions correlated with P in overland flow and drainage, most strongly for anion
exchange membrane, bicarbonate, and HCl-extractable fractions, and isotopically
exchangeable P within 1~min correlated most closely; P lost to water was associated with
short-term (\textless24~hr) exchangeable pools linked to soil Al, Fe, and Ca, whereas P
exchangeable only after long periods was unrelated to solution P
\parencite{McDowell2001}. In organic soils, Ca-bound P was the largest pool and acted as a
P sink; under controlled drainage TP correlated most with HCl-P ($r=0.76$) and DRP with
water-extractable P ($r=0.81$), whereas under pipe drainage both correlated most with
NaOH-P \parencite{Grenon2022,Grenon2021}. Depressional (closed-depression) soils in
tile-drained watersheds were P-cycling hotspots: water-extractable, Mehlich-3, and total P
and all Hedley fractions were greater in depressions than hillslopes, decreasing with
depth, with NaOH-P and HCl-P the main pools; management history (P application and tillage)
governed concentration, DPS, and stratification \parencite{Mumbi2024}. Cultivated peatland
had the highest P and P-binding element contents, dominated by inorganic P (NaOH-Pi in plow
layers, H$_2$SO$_4$-Pi in subsoil), whereas abandoned, forest, and pristine peat retained
more organic and residual P \parencite{Höyhtyä2025}. Drainage sediments themselves store
substantial P---predominantly non-occluded Al/Fe-bound P---with high retention capacity
correlated to oxalate-extractable Fe, total C, and pH, and can release P under some
conditions even when water concentrations are low \parencite{Nguyen2002}. Legacy P is
manifest at watershed scale: cumulative SRP load gradients in Lake Erie tributaries rose
94--253\% after 2002 while cropland P balances were drawn down by 17--39~kg~P~ha$^{-1}$ over
15 years, with 63--66\% of the post-2002 SRP increase attributable to greater delivery
rather than higher runoff, consistent with increased tile drainage and connectivity
\parencite{Jarvie2017}.

\subsection{Organic Soils, Peatlands, and Redox-Mediated P Dynamics}

Organic and drained peat soils are distinctive high-risk P sources. They have been linked
to excessive P leaching and large TP loads, with water movement---modulated by
wetting/drying, organic-matter degradation, hydrophobicity, and shifting flow paths---the
key transport driver \parencite{Grenon2021}. Flooding organic soils increased P release to
drainage by 4--8 times relative to drained soils, and organic-P mineralisation can supply
up to 25~kg~P~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ in temperate mineral soils and up to
160~kg~P~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ in organic soils \parencite{Sharpley1995}. Very large filterable
P loads were measured from an Organic soil (86.7~kg~P~ha$^{-1}$) versus intergrade (9.0) and
Podzol (1.7), with added P leaching throughout the Organic profile but stratified in the top
15~cm of the Podzol \parencite{McDowell2015b}. In Finnish cultivated peat, thicker peat
increased dissolved-P share and load, dissolved P was higher in thick than thin peat plots
(38 vs.\ 22~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$), and mineral subsoil retained P better than peat subsoil, so
drains set in mineral subsoil may capture P more effectively than those under thick peat
\parencite{YliHalla2022,Pham2023,Höyhtyä2025}. Degraded, artificially drained peatlands were
identified as the highest-risk eutrophication source in a German catchment, where grassland
peat P dynamics were governed by seasonal, redox-mediated remobilisation rather than
discharge alone, and fast flow exported most TP at tile-drained sites
\parencite{Tiemeyer2009}. Redox and iron chemistry directly mediate P: storm-triggered
mycelial ochre flocs from tile drains carried 27--141~mg~L$^{-1}$ TP with Fe and P
co-distributed along fungal mycelia and active Fe oxides forming 59--94\% of total Fe,
implying large episodic P discharge \parencite{Zimmer2016}. In boreal acid sulfate soils,
the redox interface lay at a median 1.6~m, with soil texture exerting stronger control than
drainage method (faster oxidation in sandy/silty than clayey soils)
\parencite{Virtanen2017}; reactive-transport modelling reproduced the geochemistry only when
both pyrite and faster-oxidising mackinawite and cation exchange were included, and increased
drainage efficiency and steady flow promoted sulfide oxidation above drain depth
\parencite{Salo2023}.

\subsection{Temporal Dynamics: Seasons, Events, and Concentration--Discharge Behaviour}

Phosphorus loss is highly concentrated in time. P concentrations and losses in tile drainage
are highly variable in space and time; losses are typically greater during storms than
baseflow, and most annual loss occurs in the non-growing season
\parencite{King2015}. In Ontario, the non-growing season accounted for 83--97\% of runoff,
84--100\% of DRP, and 67--98\% of TP loss \parencite{VanEsbroeck2016}, and models and field
data likewise placed most drainage volume and TP export in the non-growing season
\parencite{Qi2018,Sadhukhan2019b}. Event dominance is stark: more than 75\% of DRP and 66\% of
TP export occurred in the top 25\% of flows, and a single 6-day event supplied 46.6\% of TP,
84.0\% of DRP, and 81.0\% of suspended solids in one subwatershed \parencite{Brendel2019}; a
cited study found 88\% of TP loss in only 10\% of observation time \parencite{Elsayed2023};
and flooding/high-flow events dominated tile P loss in Illinois \parencite{Li2020b}.
Concentration--discharge behaviour differs by pathway: tile TP and DRP increased with
discharge (chemodynamic) while surface runoff was more chemostatic, and the tile DRP:TP ratio
fell at high flow, indicating a growing PP contribution \parencite{Osterholz2020}. Dissolved
silica in tiles was chemostatic but transport-limited at watershed outlets
\parencite{Sethna2022}. Seasonally, soluble P peaked in growing months in tile-intensive
Illinois \parencite{Algoazany2007}, whereas snowmelt and winter thaws dominated exports in
cold regions, where nutrients are exported predominantly in dissolved form---limiting the
value of particulate-focused practices \parencite{Baulch2019,VanEsbroeck2016}. Loads were
highest during and after snowmelt in Michigan \parencite{Clement2017}, and extreme events
after fertiliser application drove episodic export in Massachusetts and Indiana, where a
drought-then-deluge sequence in 2021 produced unprecedented P export (43\% of a 12-year DRP
load in nine months) \parencite{Millar2025,Mumbi2025b}.

\subsection{Management and Fertilisation History Controls}

\subsubsection{Fertilisation and manure history}

Long-term P inputs raise subsurface P losses. Long-term fertilisation increased DRP, dissolved
unreactive P, PP, and TP in tile drainage, generally in the order continuous grass $>$ rotation
$>$ continuous corn, and fertilised continuous grass shifted from a P sink to a significant P
source---implying that P removal by harvest is needed to limit losses
\parencite{Zhang2015b}. Swine-manure compost produced much greater DRP, PP, and TP than control
or yard-waste compost regardless of water-table management, with 4-year cumulative DRP up to
25.2~kg~ha$^{-1}$ and annual TP up to 8.36~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ \parencite{Zhang2015}. Manure
source and form matter: solid cattle manure reduced flow, PP, and TP losses relative to
inorganic P and liquid manure despite similar DRP loads, and liquid manure gave the highest
immediate post-application DRP \parencite{Wang2018}. Organic amendments raised DRP loss more
under no-till than conventional tillage (a 113\% larger increase), driven solely by
concentration under no-till but by concentration and flow under conventional tillage
\parencite{Zhang2017}. Losses generally rose where soils were P-enriched, application rates
exceeded recommendations, or applications preceded large storms; adhering to soil-test-based
rates lowered loss, though organic vs.\ inorganic source and broadcast vs.\ subsurface placement
did not differ significantly in some Ohio comparisons \parencite{King2018}. Incidental losses
after surface application are important---about 60\% of DRP losses from a no-till cover-crop site
followed fall surface fertilisation \parencite{Macrae2023}, rainfall shortly after application
caused sharp Dutch pipe-drain P spikes \parencite{vanderSalm2012}, and a single manure event
supplied 8\% of a 12-year DRP load in Indiana \parencite{Mumbi2025b}. Across the MANAGE database,
generally less than 2\% of applied P was lost in drainage, loss rose only weakly with rate, and
organic vs.\ inorganic applications did not differ significantly \parencite{Christianson2016}.

\subsubsection{Tillage, cover crops, and rotation}

Tillage and residue management involve trade-offs between particulate and dissolved P.
Conservation tillage, no-till, and cover crops were associated with higher subsurface DRP
losses, while tillage increased PP and TSS \parencite{Ni2020,Macrae2023}. No-till DRP losses were
consistently greater and $\sim$60\% incidental, and lower PP at the no-till cover-crop site only
partly offset higher DRP so that TP was only slightly lower there \parencite{Macrae2023}. Reduced
tillage cut erosion but markedly increased DRP, so it should be paired with efficient subsurface
drainage to minimise runoff \parencite{Koskiaho2002}; no-tillage had significantly greater
dissolved P drainage loads than conventional tillage in the MANAGE synthesis
\parencite{Christianson2016}. Cover-crop effects on P are inconsistent: a cereal rye cover crop
did not significantly reduce drainage discharge or DRP in Illinois \parencite{deOliveira2022},
cover crops tended to raise DRP and TP leaching in lysimeters but not significantly at field drain
depth \parencite{Liu2019}, yet modelled winter rye reduced DRP by 16\% (mostly in runoff)
\parencite{Grewal2025} and an oat cover crop reduced predicted DRP and TP by 30\% and 54\%
\parencite{Askar2021b}. Corn--soybean and corn--soybean--wheat rotations reduced subsurface DRP
relative to continuous corn \parencite{Ni2020}, and DRP losses were greater in corn years than
soybean years \parencite{Zhang2015}. Simulations indicated moldboard/conventional tillage could reduce
drainage DRP and PP (by up to 48\% and 30\% as tillage intensity rose from no-till to
moldboard plow) while simultaneously lowering runoff P losses: under one compost scenario,
conventional tillage decreased overall runoff P loss by $\sim$50\% and tile-drainage P loss by
$\sim$19\% relative to no-till \parencite{Pan2023}, and shifting tillage to spring or injecting fertiliser could cut TP loss by
$\sim$10\% and 25\% \parencite{Qi2018}. In snowmelt regions, however, zero tillage and perennial
forages can \emph{increase} dissolved P despite reducing erosion---alfalfa forage lost 160\% more
TP than cropland, largely via a 221\% rise in dissolved P---so late-fall/winter applications are
consistently high-risk \parencite{Baulch2019}.

\subsubsection{Drainage design and grazing}

Drainage geometry alters both amount and pathway of loss. Increasing drain spacing reduced
subsurface volumes and TP loss (more strongly on finer soils) while increasing surface runoff and
its TP load \parencite{Morrison2013}. Shallower, closer drains increased P-loss risk in paddy
systems \parencite{DarziNaftchali2016}, and shallow tiles and surface inlets are among the field
attributes prone to elevated tile P \parencite{King2015}. Grazing intensity sharply raised losses:
a single intensive dairy grazing event increased sediment 50\%, particulate-associated P 40-fold,
and dissolved P 15-fold in tile water \parencite{Gillingham2000}, and grass--clover pasture leached
more subsurface MRP and TP than monoculture grass \parencite{Hooda1999}. Poorly drained,
high-STP sites had large potential to lose DRP in overland flow, with an 11-fold higher
flow-weighted DRP concentration from a high- versus low-fertility grassland
\parencite{Kurz2005}.

\subsection{Phosphorus Indices and Process Models for Drainage P}

Phosphorus indices are semiquantitative tools that delineate critical source areas by combining
source and transport factors, and their scores generally correlate positively with measured or
simulated losses across scales, though many region-specific versions exist and the more
physically based ones (e.g.\ the Swedish index) demand more data
\parencite{Buczko2007}. The Norwegian P index explained 66\% of variation in relative TP
concentration and identified the highest-risk fields and subcatchments, with soil P status
explaining 66\% of the source factor and erosion risk and contributing distance driving transport
\parencite{Bechmann2007}. A recurring critique is that most indices ignore tile drains or treat
them crudely; component-index frameworks have been proposed to represent tile P export explicitly
through source\,$\times$\,transport terms, accounting for the macropore-flow fraction of
precipitation and the dissolved and particulate concentrations it carries, with MF identified as
the dominant uncertainty and seasonal export still to be incorporated
\parencite{Reid2012}. Catchment nutrient-loss indices commonly use contributing distance,
connectivity, soil properties, and erosion; P is chiefly controlled by overland-flow conduits and
barriers whereas subsurface flow controls N more, and incorporating riparian removal capacity is
recommended \parencite{Pärn2012}. A simplified P index ranked subsurface treatments as lower risk
and surface-drained paddy as highest \parencite{DarziNaftchali2016}.

Process models remain limited for drainage P but are advancing. Few reviewed models address P in
drainage: SWAT includes tile flow but not tile P, DRAINMOD simulates drains but not P, and
ICECREAMDB---designed specifically for drainage P---is promising but needs testing; nested,
paired field experiments and uncertainty assessment are called for
\parencite{Radcliffe2015,Wang2020b}. DRAINMOD-P, developed to represent P dynamics in drained
cropland, satisfactorily predicted subsurface discharge (monthly NSE 0.59) and TP but poorly
predicted monthly DRP, attributing $\sim$21\% of tile DRP to macropore flow ($\sim$15\% of
discharge) \parencite{Askar2021,Askar2021b}. ICECREAM satisfactorily simulated hydrology, TP, and
DRP but fared worse for tile PP and soil moisture in Ontario, where observed tile TP was 65\% of
total \parencite{Qi2018}. RZWQM2-P---using Richards, Hooghoudt, and Poiseuille formulations for
matrix, tile, and macropore flow---simulated surface and tile DRP and PP and total P well, was the
first to accurately simulate daily DRP, and reproduced pathway partitioning
\parencite{Sadhukhan2019,Sadhukhan2019b,Grewal2025}. EPIC reasonably modelled flows and tile DRP,
and coupling with SurPhos improved runoff DRP and manure representation
\parencite{Wang2019}. RZWQM2-P also captured long-term tillage effects on tile P
\parencite{Pan2023}, and DRAINMOD linked to empirical regressions predicted tile P loads with
minimal parameters and confirmed that wider drain spacing reduces subsurface P loss
\parencite{Morrison2013}. Models such as SurPhos and manure-runoff and direct-fertilizer models
have shown accuracy for runoff P, while ICECREAM performed poorly for soil moisture and tile PP
\parencite{Wang2020b}.

\subsection{Edge-of-Field Retention and Mitigation}

The soil and pathway controls above define where mitigation must act, and a consolidated body of
evidence quantifies edge-of-field practices---while emphasising trade-offs and site specificity.
Drainage water (controlled) management can reduce loss by curtailing discharge: it cut dissolved P
by 63\% and TP by 50\% in Minnesota \parencite{Kröger2013}, and managed drainage reduced tile
ortho-P loss by 80\%, with more than half the reduction occurring in spring at similar drainage,
suggesting enhanced crop P uptake \parencite{Nash2015c}. Controlled drainage with subirrigation
reduced DRP, PP, and TP for low-P treatments but not for swine-manure compost
\parencite{Zhang2015}, and reviews report TP reductions of 57\% and soluble-P reductions of
57--80\% \parencite{Elsayed2023}; however, controlled drainage can also raise DRP/TP or shift loss
to surface runoff, and organic-soil concentrations may stay above limits even as loads fall
\parencite{Grewal2025,Sadhukhan2019b,Grenon2021}. Blind inlets replacing tile risers reduced TP by
up to 78\% and soluble P by up to 72\% \parencite{Kröger2013}, with 50--78\% TP and 65--72\% DRP
reductions reported elsewhere \parencite{Singh2020c}. Subsurface placement and 4R management are
highlighted as promising, with deep placement cutting DRP by 69--72\%
\parencite{Wilson2019,Sharpley2019,Singh2020c}. Denitrifying bioreactors gave modest, variable DRP
removal (median 12\% efficiency; outflow DRP less than half inflow) and occasionally acted as DRP
sources under low summer flow \parencite{Bailon2025}. Filter media (Fe- and Al-based superior to
Ca/Mg) and passive Fe-oxide resins target dissolved P, and P-sorbents can extend system lifetime
under oxidising conditions \parencite{Mendes2020,Li2020b}. Constructed and vegetated systems retain
P chiefly by particulate sedimentation, with retention of 27--100\% but strong dependence on
hydraulic residence time, sorbent availability, and loading---systems can flip from sink to source
during events \parencite{Mendes2020,Kröger2013,Singh2020c}. Vegetated ditches retained
$\sim$44\% of inorganic P annually \parencite{Kröger2013}, drainage sediments and ditch beds shifted
seasonally between P sink, source, and equilibrium \parencite{Ahiablame2010,Nguyen2002}, and routing
$\sim$25\% of tile flow through a riparian buffer achieved near-complete removal of dissolved
nutrients and sediment \parencite{Jacquemin2020}. Because buffers can bypass the root zone via tile
loads and may saturate over time, modified, bypass-preventing designs and trap-and-treat ditches
are emphasised \parencite{Mendes2020,Millar2025,Baulch2019}. Finally, tile drainage does not
inevitably raise P export: under some conditions installing tiles reduced surface runoff and SRP
losses without increasing TP export \parencite{Klaiber2020}, and in frozen vertisolic landscapes
tile expansion may not exacerbate P (though it can raise N) \parencite{Kokulan2021}. Overall,
effective P control depends on matching the right practice to site hydrology, connectivity, weather,
and legacy P, and on recognising N--P trade-offs among conservation practices
\parencite{King2015,Sharpley2019,Ni2020,Baulch2019,Wilson2019}.

### Material: Tile Drain Nitrate Loss Model Calibration

\subsection{Overview}

Simulation models are the principal tools for quantifying subsurface (tile) drainage flow and nitrate-N loss and for extrapolating short field experiments across weather, soils, cropping systems, and management. The body of calibration and validation studies synthesised here spans several model families: the process-based DRAINMOD suite (DRAINMOD, DRAINMOD-N, DRAINMOD-N\,II, DRAINMOD 5.1/6.1, and the coupled DRAINMOD-DSSAT); the Root Zone Water Quality Model and its variants (RZWQM, RZWQM2, RZWQM2-P, the RZWQM--DSSAT hybrid, and RZ-SHAW); the watershed-scale Soil and Water Assessment Tool (SWAT) with successive tile-drainage and nitrogen routines, together with the related APEX model; cropping-system and biogeochemical models (APSIM, DNDC, \textit{ecosys}, HERMES, WHCNS, HYDRUS, FLUSH, CERES-Maize, and PNM); field-scale nutrient models such as ADAPT; and a growing set of statistical, regression, and machine-learning surrogates \parencite{Ma2001,David2009,Luo2010,Fang2012,Negm2014,Smith2020}.

Across this literature a consistent picture emerges. After site-specific calibration, models reproduce annual and cumulative subsurface drainage and nitrate-N loss well---frequently within roughly $\pm5$--$20\%$ of observations---and they generally capture the direction and relative magnitude of management effects even when absolute values are imperfect \parencite{Feyereisen2007,Youssef2006,Singh2020b,Dayyani2010}. Performance is strongest at the annual scale, weaker at the monthly scale, and often unsatisfactory at the daily scale \parencite{Liang2018,Ikenberry2017,Marjerison2016,Ojeda2018}. Nitrate-N loss is typically predicted less accurately than drainage flow, and crop nitrogen uptake, residual soil N, and grain yield are recurrent weak points \parencite{Ma2007,Ahuja2007,Malone2007c}. Cold-season snowmelt and freeze--thaw dynamics are a widespread source of error, motivating dedicated model developments for northern climates \parencite{Wang2006c,Dayyani2010b,Yang2007,Salazar2009}. Finally, calibrated models converge on robust management conclusions: nitrogen application rate is the dominant control on nitrate loss, drainage design (depth and spacing) and controlled drainage measurably reduce losses, and winter cover crops reduce nitrate export to tile drains \parencite{Davis2000,Moriasi2013,Fang2012,Malone2017}.

\subsection{The DRAINMOD family of drainage and nitrogen models}

\subsubsection{Hydrology and water-table dynamics}

DRAINMOD and its nitrogen extensions are the most widely tested drainage models, and they simulate water-table depth, drain outflow, and drainage volume with generally good accuracy after calibration. For a Normania clay loam in southern Minnesota, DRAINMOD-N reproduced annual total tile flow with a mean deviation of 1.38\,cm, absolute deviation of 2.07\,cm, and $R^2 = 0.97$ \parencite{Zhao2000}. In North Carolina, simulated and observed daily water-table depths agreed with mean absolute errors of 11.8--13.5\,cm and modelling efficiencies (EF) of 0.70--0.76, and monthly subsurface drainage volumes gave EF of 0.64--0.67 across plots \parencite{Youssef2006}. In Minnesota, DRAINMOD-N\,II achieved Nash--Sutcliffe efficiencies of 0.74--0.99 during calibration and 0.59--0.97 during validation for drainage and nitrate loss, and predicted monthly drainage as ``very good'' during calibration and ``good'' during validation \parencite{Luo2010,Ale2013}. Comparable performance was reported for cold and humid Sweden, where DRAINMOD-N\,II matched monthly drain outflows with no clear over- or under-prediction bias and predicted 12 of 14 significant snow events with snow cover on 82\% of measured days \parencite{Salazar2009}.

The model has also been transferred to newly reclaimed, waterlogged land in India. In Punjab, DRAINMOD-N\,II calibration and validation for drain-flow depth gave RMSE of 8.88 and 9.66, PBIAS of 15.41\% and 19.52\%, and NSE of 0.53 and 0.47 \parencite{Dar2022}, while a DRAINMOD v6.1 application to rice systems reported water-table-depth RMSE of 7.5\,cm (calibration) and 8.16\,cm (validation), with observed and simulated mean water-table depths of 46.13 versus 48.92\,cm and 53.53 versus 55.42\,cm in the two seasons, and non-significant $t$-test differences \parencite{Dar2025}. For no-till fields in Canada, DRAINMOD-N\,II reproduced monthly drain outflows closely, though nitrate losses were somewhat overestimated in the wheat season and underestimated in the soybean season and were poorly predicted in winter and early spring under the available inputs \parencite{Garmdareh2018}.

Cold-climate performance was substantially improved by DRAINMOD 5.1, which added freeze--thaw and snowmelt components. It produced much smaller monthly drainage errors than the original model, which had overestimated outflow in colder months, and it matched the timing and magnitude of drainage events more closely \parencite{Wang2006c}. A subsequent application simulated daily water-table depth ($R^2 = 0.81$--$0.91$), daily drain flow ($R^2 = 0.84$--$0.88$), and monthly drain flow ($R^2 = 0.95$--$0.97$) under both free drainage and sub-irrigation \parencite{Dayyani2010b}.

\subsubsection{Nitrate-N loss and crop yield}

Nitrate simulation with the DRAINMOD suite is generally satisfactory at annual and cumulative scales but weaker at monthly and daily resolution. In Minnesota, annual nitrate loss was reproduced with a mean deviation of 2.16\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ and $R^2 = 0.98$, while corn yield was captured less precisely ($R^2 = 0.69$) \parencite{Zhao2000}. In North Carolina, monthly nitrate-N loss was poorly matched (EF of 0.48 for the calibration plot and near zero or negative for others), but annual losses were well predicted for most plots (yearly EF up to 0.92) and cumulative six-year losses were predicted very accurately, with errors of only $-1.3$ to $4.0\%$ \parencite{Youssef2006}. Indian applications gave nitrogen-load NSE of 0.82--0.84 with cumulative predicted losses of 32.15 and 28.77\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ against observed 33.07 and 26.88\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Dar2022}, and daily nitrate-N loads with NSE and $R^2$ of 0.66--0.70 (calibration) and 0.88 (validation), with mean daily concentrations of 13.64 versus 12.9 and 11.09 versus 11.19\,mg\,L$^{-1}$ \parencite{Dar2025}. DRAINMOD-N reproduced nitrate-N losses in surface runoff with high accuracy but was less accurate for drain-outflow losses, tending to overestimate them during colder months and freeze--thaw periods; it nonetheless gave reasonable predictions across tillage and intercropping treatments not explicitly represented as inputs \parencite{Yang2007}. Two-year total nitrate-N loss errors of 1.32--1.40\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ (calibration) and $-1.64$ to 0.97\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ (validation) were reported for DRAINMOD 5.1 in Canada \parencite{Wang2006c}, and monthly nitrate-N losses were matched with $R^2 = 0.90$--$0.94$ under free drainage and sub-irrigation \parencite{Dayyani2010b}. Simulated annual nitrogen process rates (mineralisation, nitrification, denitrification, volatilisation, deposition) fell within reported literature ranges \parencite{Salazar2009}, and studies in Minnesota consistently indicated a nitrogen budget surplus, implying scope to reduce fertiliser rates and thereby drainage nitrate losses \parencite{Ale2013}.

Recent work has extended the DRAINMOD framework to simultaneous nitrogen and phosphorus dynamics. In Ohio, DRAINMOD simulated mean annual subsurface discharge (28.4 versus measured 27.6\,cm) and monthly nitrate-N load (overall NSE 0.63) with ``good'' accuracy, and monthly dissolved reactive phosphorus (DRP) load reasonably (NSE 0.63), but total phosphorus was simulated poorly (validation NSE $-0.82$; cumulative TP underpredicted by 19.2\%); simulated macropore flow contributed about 4\% of annual discharge \parencite{Askar2025}. By contrast, the deterministic DRAINMOD-GLEAMS model underpredicted atrazine concentrations and loads in tile outflow, especially in spring and fall \parencite{Rudra2005}.

\subsubsection{DRAINMOD-DSSAT and process integration}

Coupling DRAINMOD hydrology with the DSSAT crop models produced a whole-system tool that jointly simulates hydrology, water quality, and crop growth on drained land. In Iowa, DRAINMOD-DSSAT predicted annual subsurface drainage very well (annual NSE 0.95, monthly 0.83; cumulative ten-year drainage underpredicted by only 2.2\%), annual nitrate-N losses across high, medium, and low N treatments (NSE 0.87, 0.93, 0.94), and crop yield with absolute error below 8\% in 27 of 30 plot-years \parencite{Negm2014}. A companion study under free and controlled drainage reported monthly drainage-flow NSE of 0.81 (free) and 0.63 (controlled) and monthly nitrate-N NSE of 0.76 and 0.66, with measured and simulated controlled-drainage reductions of 24.6\% and 27.1\% for drainage and 34.8\% and 33.5\% for nitrate loss \parencite{Negm2017}. In east-central Illinois, DRAINMOD-DSSAT achieved very good calibration and validation of monthly drainage, monthly nitrate loss, and yield, with cumulative nitrate-N loss over 1992--1998 of 262.8\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ against a measured 263.8\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Singh2020b}; a further evaluation at the same site found the model simulated monthly drainage more closely than RZWQM2, matched RZWQM2 for monthly nitrate loss, and achieved excellent crop-yield performance, with both models agreeing that split spring pre-plant plus side-dress N reduces nitrate loss without lowering corn yield \parencite{Singh2022c}.

\subsection{RZWQM, RZWQM2, and root-zone hybrids}

The Root Zone Water Quality Model has been evaluated across many U.S. states and internationally, reproducing water movement, crop growth, nitrogen dynamics, and pesticide behaviour reasonably well, with water balance identified as the key mediator whose errors propagate into plant water stress and nitrogen availability \parencite{Ma2001}. In central Iowa the model reproduced year-to-year variation in tile flow and nitrogen loading ($r^2 = 0.74$ and $0.71$), matched average annual tile flow exactly (13.5\,cm simulated and measured), and predicted soil-water storage and water-table depth within experimental replicate RMSEs; however, N loading was somewhat over-predicted (21.3 versus 17.6\,kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$) and plant growth, N uptake, and soybean N fixation remained weak \parencite{Ma2007}. RZWQM adequately captured tillage effects, simulating rising flow-weighted nitrate concentration from no-till through moldboard plow, and crop-rotation effects, with lower drain flow and nitrate concentration under corn--soybean than continuous corn \parencite{Ma2007b}. It reproduced yearly drain flow closely (225.3 versus 222.5\,mm; EF 0.99) and nitrate-N loss reasonably (EF 0.8), and indicated that the fraction of applied N lost in drainage rose from 7 to 16\% as N rate increased from 30 to 180\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Bakhsh2004}. A dynamic soil-microbial formulation reproduced ten-year annual nitrogen losses across treatments (e.g., 348 versus 369\,kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$) and annual tile flow (RMSE 3.4\,cm, ME 0.89); notably, disabling microbial growth gave very similar N-loss results ($r^2 = 0.94$) and improved residual soil nitrate simulation \parencite{Ma2008}.

Two consistent lessons run through the RZWQM literature. First, measured soil hydraulic properties yield much better simulations of soil water, water table, and nitrate loss than default texture-class parameters, with lateral saturated conductivity in the tile layer (101--130\,cm) identified as the single most sensitive parameter for drain flow and nitrate loss \parencite{Ma2007c,Ahuja2007}. Second, integrating field research with even imperfect models improves quantitative understanding and exposes knowledge gaps \parencite{Ahuja2007}. RZWQM2 has since been calibrated with PEST to predict monthly tile flow, nitrate loss, and yield under free and controlled drainage \parencite{Fang2012}, applied to estimate N$_2$O emissions and cover-crop effects over nine years (observed and simulated flow-weighted nitrate-concentration reductions of 60\% and 54\%; Tier III emission factors of 0.032 and 0.041) \parencite{Gillette2018}, and extended to phosphorus as RZWQM2-P, which performed well for subsurface discharge (calibration NSE 0.66, validation 0.76) and satisfactorily for total phosphorus but unsatisfactorily for DRP because of difficulties simulating post-fertilisation and ponded conditions \parencite{BinShokrana2023b}. A hybrid RZWQM--DSSAT model reasonably simulated tile flow and nitrogen load in a subbasin different from the calibration basin \parencite{Singer2011}, and the model performed adequately for soil moisture and tomato yield but less well for evapotranspiration and water-use efficiency in China \parencite{Zhang2022b}. A distinctive cross-site test in Canada calibrated RZWQM at one soil series and validated it at another: calibration total tile flow was within 5\% and yields within 1\% of observations, but at the independent validation site observed daily flows were 41\% higher than simulated and yields were underpredicted \parencite{Ahmed2007}. The RZWQM--SHAW coupling (RZ-SHAW) has been used specifically for cold-season processes, simulating snow depth, winter soil temperature, and winter subsurface drainage satisfactorily and partitioning the 30-year snowmelt and rainfall contributions to winter and spring drainage \parencite{Li2024b,Li2025}.

\subsection{SWAT and watershed-scale tile-drainage routines}

At the watershed scale, SWAT reproduces streamflow well but nitrate flux less accurately, and it has undergone successive tile-drainage and nitrogen-routine developments. In an Illinois watershed, SWAT simulated monthly streamflow with NSE of 0.85 (calibration) and 0.69 (validation) but achieved weaker riverine nitrate performance, underestimating annual nitrate flux by 27\% and overestimating soybean N fixation \parencite{Hu2007}. Incorporating the Hooghoudt steady-state and Kirkham tile equations produced a Modified SWAT with daily and monthly streamflow NSE of 0.76 and 0.85 (calibration), and demonstrated that tile drainage substantially lowers soil water while raising streamflow \parencite{Moriasi2012}. The Revised SWAT partitioned water and nutrient budgets well, matched observed corn yield (8.6\,t\,ha$^{-1}$), and improved monthly tile flow and nitrate-N prediction over standard SWAT by 48\% and 28\%, respectively, avoiding the unrealistic curve-number values otherwise needed to force adequate tile flow \parencite{Moriasi2013,Moriasi2013b}. Updated routines have repeatedly improved fidelity: the SWAT\_HKdc drawdown routine reduced simulated tile-flow flashiness and matched literature-based annual tile flow of 85--172\,mm (8.5--16.2\% of precipitation), with tile nitrate contributing 66\% of total nitrogen export in one year \parencite{Boles2015}; the SWAT-tile routine outperformed SWAT-684 for nitrate load at both field and watershed scales, driven by transport- and lag-related parameters \parencite{Kim2023}; and new tile and nitrogen modules improved daily and monthly streamflow and nitrate-load simulation in Iowa \parencite{Qi2025}. Comparative work in Germany found both curve-number and Green--Ampt infiltration schemes reproduced discharge and tile flow reasonably (daily streamflow NSE 0.50/0.42 and 0.45/0.39; daily tile-flow NSE $\approx$0.33--0.36), with tile flow plus groundwater accounting for 91--98\% of total flow \parencite{Bauwe2016}. Studies also documented representative tile-flow parameter sets \parencite{Guo2018b,Brighenti2023} and the value of soft data and multi-criteria constraints, showing that removing tile drainage drastically reduced simulated streamflow, total nitrogen, and total phosphorus while barely affecting evapotranspiration or yield \parencite{Nelson2018}.

Several watershed applications expose structural and partitioning limitations. Across the Western Lake Erie Basin, three SWAT models performed well at the outlet but varied upstream, tended to over-predict discharge yet under-predict phosphorus in tile drainage and nitrogen in surface runoff, and disagreed on surface-versus-tile partitioning despite an observed subsurface dominance \parencite{Apostel2021}. Modified nitrate algorithms improved daily fluctuations and made monthly nitrate-load performance good to very good, but daily nitrate-concentration NSE remained unsatisfactory, indicating a need for more physically based soil-nitrogen routines \parencite{Ikenberry2017}. A landscape-based SWAT\_LS produced far more behavioural parameter sets than SWAT2005 (1{,}829 versus 377) and added riparian denitrification, estimating present nitrate removal of 67--257\,t\,yr$^{-1}$ (4.3--16.5\%) rising to 367--1{,}322\,t\,yr$^{-1}$ if all riparian zones were undrained \parencite{Hoang2017}. The related APEX/NTT framework simulated annual tile discharge, DRP, and crop yields satisfactorily across 24 edge-of-field sites, though corn yield and particulate phosphorus were less well captured \parencite{Guo2020b}. A multi-model intercomparison in an Illinois watershed found that most models simulated corn and soybean yields within 12\% and annual drainage flux within observations (28.2--31.2 versus 30.2\,cm, except one model at 20.3\,cm), and predicted nitrate-N loss of 21--28 against a measured 28\,kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$, but denitrification estimates diverged widely (3.8--21\,kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$), with models that underestimated riverine nitrate tending to predict high denitrification \parencite{David2009}. SWAT calibrated for soil moisture and yield across Iowa, Ohio, and Illinois further showed that long-term drought stress caused much larger yield reductions than aeration stress \parencite{Wang2016}.

\subsection{Cropping-system and biogeochemical models}

A diverse set of cropping-system and biogeochemical models has been calibrated for tile-drained conditions. APSIM has been evaluated repeatedly in the U.S. Midwest: a modified version simulated yearly drain flow within error criteria for 38 of 44 observations and yearly nitrate-N loss for 30 of 45, correctly predicting higher drainage and nitrate loss under continuous corn than corn--soybean and a 38\% cover-crop reduction in nitrate loss over a 41-year simulation \parencite{Malone2007c}. In Indiana, APSIM gave very good corn stover yield (concordance 0.96), satisfactory grain yield (0.85), and acceptable annual drainage (0.63--0.75), performing better in corn--soybean than continuous corn, with daily drainage poor and under-predicted events concentrated in winter and early spring \parencite{Ojeda2018}. A heavily customised APSIM reproduced biomass, yield, soil temperature, soil water, and subsurface drainage in Iowa (biomass RRMSE 0.13--0.14), aided by an improved soil-temperature routine \parencite{Dietzel2016}, and an APSIM-based analysis of water-table dynamics across the United States achieved $R^2 = 0.68$, $d = 0.90$, and RRMSE of 33\%, attributing 91\% of water-table variance to interannual weather versus 7\% to management \parencite{Elli2023}.

Biogeochemical models have been adapted to represent tile hydrology explicitly. A revised DNDC reproduced surface runoff ($R^2 = 0.99$), subsurface drainage ($R^2 = 0.97$, ME 0.96), and nitrate leaching ($R^2 = 0.95$, ME 0.79, though about 20\% underestimated) in China \parencite{Deng2011}, and hydrology-focused developments improved DNDC's soil water storage, daily flow, and nitrogen loading to tile drains, performing at least as well as RZWQM2 while running four times faster and capturing controlled-drainage and sub-irrigation effects \parencite{Smith2020}. The oxygen-explicit \textit{ecosys} model validated well for yield and monthly tile flow in Iowa and identified soil oxygen dynamics as the central mediator linking hydrology, biogeochemistry, and crop growth, showing that tile drainage increased subsurface discharge, inorganic nitrogen loss, and yield---especially in wet years \parencite{Ma2025}. Simpler models also performed strongly: HERMES reproduced annual nitrate loss under cover-crop and no-cover-crop treatments (43.8 versus 44.4 and 17.6 versus 18.9\,kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$) with cumulative EF above 0.95 \parencite{Malone2017}; the WHCNS model captured 16-year annual drainage ($R^2 = 0.92$, NSE 0.90) but was weaker monthly, and recommended a 100--120\,kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$ rate to keep drainage nitrate near the drinking-water criterion \parencite{Liang2018}; HYDRUS matched soil water, drainage, and nitrate fluxes across drainage systems in rainfed Iran \parencite{Haghnazari2020}; the dual-permeability FLUSH model replicated nitrate concentration dynamics in Finnish clay soils (NSE 0.68 and 0.45) \parencite{Salo2015}; new tile-drainage algorithms in CERES-Maize described water and nitrate movement reasonably, over-predicting tile flow and nitrogen while under-predicting soil nitrate \parencite{Garrison1999}; and the PNM model, requiring minimal calibration and basic inputs, simulated annual drainage and nitrate leaching better than daily or mid-year totals \parencite{Marjerison2016}. Dedicated cover-crop routines (Grosub and RyeGro) correctly ranked rye biomass production and estimated seasonal cumulative drainage and nitrate loss within about $\pm20\%$ for corn--soybean rotations \parencite{Feyereisen2006,Feyereisen2007}.

\subsection{ADAPT and related field-to-watershed nitrate models}

The ADAPT model has been calibrated extensively in Minnesota for monthly subsurface drainage and nitrate loss. On a Webster clay loam it matched drainage better than nitrate loss, under-predicting drainage in wet months and nitrate in some wet years following drought \parencite{Davis2000}. On commercial fields it achieved calibration NSE of 0.75 (flow) and 0.56 (nitrate) and validation NSE up to 0.81 and 0.73, predicting annual tile flow of 23.2\% of precipitation (measured 25.3\%) and annual nitrate loss of 59.6\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$, equivalent to about 46\% of applied N \parencite{Nangia2008,Nangia2010c}. Watershed-scale ADAPT applications reproduced monthly flow and nitrate trends and were used to isolate climate from management, concluding that nitrate export responded more to an increasingly wet climate than to management, with recent wet conditions raising discharge by 51--70\% relative to historical climates \parencite{Nangia2010,Nangia2010b}. A related spatial-process model explained 67--75\% of the variability in flow and nitrate for two Minnesota watersheds and showed nitrate losses rising with the proportion of tile-drained cropland \parencite{Gowda2007}. The linked DRAIN-WARMF model improved daily and monthly flow over WARMF alone, especially during spring snowmelt (validation daily $R^2 = 0.76$, monthly 0.97), and simulated monthly nitrate loss satisfactorily (validation $R^2 = 0.86$), slightly under-predicting cumulative losses \parencite{Dayyani2010}.

\subsection{Statistical, regression, and machine-learning approaches}

Alongside process models, simpler data-driven tools have proven capable of predicting drainage and nitrate export. Artificial neural networks outperformed deterministic alternatives: radial-basis-function (RBF) networks predicted daily tile outflow and nitrate-N concentration better than feedforward back-propagation (FBP) networks, which underestimated tile flow and were reliable only at monthly resolution \parencite{Sharma2003,Rudra2005}. Regression tools have been developed as efficient alternatives to mechanistic models. In North Carolina a regression tool estimated annual drainage flow (adjusted $R^2 \geq 0.91$) and annual nitrate loss (adjusted $R^2 \geq 0.88$) from readily available variables, reproducing drainage-water-management reductions of 6.3--6.6\,kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$ close to the DRAINMOD-simulated 7.2--8.6\,kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$, with discrepancies concentrated in extreme-rainfall years \parencite{Negm2016}. In Denmark a parsimonious model linked annual tile flow to annual precipitation, validated across 38 drainage stations, and was more accurate at catchment than field scale while explaining a large share of nitrate-N export \parencite{Frederiksen2023}. Regression equations derived from DRAINMOD-N\,II output in Minnesota related nitrate loss to fertiliser rate and growing-season precipitation ($r^2$ of 0.53--0.56), and predicted that a full shift to split applications would reduce nitrate loads to surface waters by 28\%, rising to 41\% with a 30\% rate reduction \parencite{Wilson2020}. A multivariable regression in Iowa explained much of the observed variation in yields, tile flow, and nitrate loss and confirmed reductions from spring versus fall N application \parencite{Ahuja2007}. Machine-learning surrogates such as Cubist and SVM-RBF matched or exceeded the physically based RZ-SHAW model for winter subsurface drainage while training far faster, although RZ-SHAW remained preferable for extrapolation to future climates because it explicitly represents snow, freezing, and evaporation \parencite{Li2025}.

\subsection{Recurring performance patterns across models}

\subsubsection{Temporal aggregation}

A near-universal finding is that predictive skill improves with temporal aggregation. Annual and cumulative drainage and nitrate loss are simulated well even where monthly and, especially, daily values are not \parencite{Liang2018,Marjerison2016,Malone2017,Ojeda2018}. Monthly nitrate-load simulations routinely meet accepted performance criteria while daily nitrate concentrations do not \parencite{Ikenberry2017,Qi2025}, drainage predictions tend to be slightly more accurate than nitrate predictions at the same scale \parencite{Luo2010}, and statistical models are more accurate at catchment than field scale \parencite{Frederiksen2023}.

\subsubsection{Cold-season and snowmelt limitations}

Errors concentrate in late winter and early spring across model families. Prediction errors were mainly associated with snowmelt and freeze--thaw flow events \parencite{Luo2010}; models under-predicted early-spring drainage during snowmelt or over-predicted drainage in colder months \parencite{Nangia2008,Yang2007}; SWAT-based watershed models had difficulty partitioning flow between subsurface drainage and surface runoff under ice blockage and freeze--thaw \parencite{Nangia2010}; and RyeGro predicted late-winter/early-spring drainage and nitrate loss too early when snow cover was present \parencite{Feyereisen2007}. These limitations directly motivated cold-climate developments such as the freeze--thaw and snowmelt components of DRAINMOD 5.1 and DRAIN-WARMF and the RZ-SHAW coupling \parencite{Wang2006c,Dayyani2010b,Dayyani2010,Li2024b}.

\subsubsection{Parameterization and sensitivity}

Calibration studies repeatedly identify soil-hydraulic and water-flow parameters as controlling tile-drain and nitrate predictions. Measured soil hydraulic properties substantially outperformed default texture-class values \parencite{Ma2007c,Ahuja2007}, with lateral saturated conductivity in and just above the tile layer the most sensitive parameter \parencite{Ma2007c} and Brooks--Corey parameters, saturated conductivity, and drainable porosity most sensitive for tile flow \parencite{Ahmed2007}. Soil field capacity was the single most sensitive parameter for nitrate loss and flow-weighted concentration in HERMES and RZWQM, through its control on denitrification and mineralisation \parencite{Malone2017,Gillette2018}. Precipitation was the most sensitive driver of nitrogen loading, while slope governed sediment and particulate-N loss and fertiliser rate governed nitrate leaching \parencite{Deng2011}; moisture and decay-rate limiters strongly affected nitrate loads in FLUSH \parencite{Salo2015}; and drain-depth and drain-spacing parameters (DDRAIN, SDRAIN) were moderately sensitive for streamflow in SWAT \parencite{Moriasi2012}. Constraining models with soft data and multiple objectives lowered nominal efficiencies but produced more realistic water budgets and yields \parencite{Nelson2018}, and companion soil-moisture calibration was shown to be essential for accurate yield simulation \parencite{Wang2016}. Auxiliary tools such as the modified Checkbook water-balance model further improved in-field soil-moisture-deficit estimates under shallow water tables by adding upward flux and subsurface drainage \parencite{Kolars2019}.

\subsection{Management insights from calibrated models}

\subsubsection{Nitrogen rate and timing}

The most robust cross-model conclusion is that nitrogen application rate is the dominant control on nitrate-N loss and offers far greater reduction potential than altering drainage design. In Minnesota, reducing N application from 200 to 100\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ cut nitrate loss by 67\% (from 33.8 to 11.1\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$) with no predicted yield penalty \parencite{Moriasi2013}, and long-term ADAPT and DRAINMOD simulations reached the same ranking of rate over spacing and depth \parencite{Davis2000,Ale2013}. Simulated nitrate loss rose steeply with N rate in Iowa and Minnesota \parencite{Bakhsh2004,Zhao2000}, and SWAT predicted riverine nitrate reductions of 10--43\% for fertiliser cuts of 10--50\% \parencite{Hu2007}. Beyond total rate, shifting applications from fall to spring or to split timing consistently reduced losses: fall-to-spring switches cut watershed nitrate by 33--35\% \parencite{Gowda2007,Nangia2010}, and spring and split applications reduced N loads by 22--29\% and 37--46\% relative to fall depending on rate and precipitation \parencite{Wilson2020}. Split spring pre-plant plus side-dress N reduced nitrate loss without affecting corn yield in Illinois \parencite{Singh2020b,Singh2022c}, and reducing the N rate from 245 to 140\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ lowered N loss by roughly 40\% with little yield effect \parencite{Fang2012}.

\subsubsection{Drain depth and spacing}

Calibrated models consistently show that shallower drains and wider spacing reduce tile flow and nitrate loss, generally at modest yield cost. Reducing drain depth from 1.5 to 0.9\,m lowered simulated tile flow from 207 to 191\,mm and nitrate loss from 34.0 to 29.4\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$, and widening spacing from 27 to 60\,m reduced flow from 205 to 182\,mm and nitrate loss from 33.8 to 28.4\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Moriasi2013}. Widening spacing from 27 to 40\,m cut nitrate loss from about 45 to 22\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ with only a 7\% yield reduction \parencite{Nangia2010c}, and shallower depths reduced nitrate loss more than wider spacing for fine-textured soils \parencite{Davis2000}. Field and simulation studies in Illinois confirmed that plots with 18.3 and 24.4\,m spacing had lower drain flow and better nitrate retention than 12.2\,m spacing while maintaining or increasing yield \parencite{Hwang2024}. Scenario analyses further indicate optimal designs balancing yield and losses---for example, 1.3\,m depth with 42\,m spacing in India \parencite{Dar2025}, 28\,m spacing for a Minnesota clay loam \parencite{Zhao2000}, 1.2\,m depth with 24\,m spacing for the northern Corn Belt \parencite{Luo2010}, and wider spacing being environmentally preferable for nitrate under warming \parencite{Ziaeifar2025}. Increasing tile depth raised tile flow and, under physically consistent routines, tile nitrate load, whereas an inconsistent routine failed to reproduce this relationship \parencite{Kim2023,Bauwe2016}.

\subsubsection{Controlled drainage, cover crops, and rotation}

Controlled drainage (drainage water management) emerges as an effective nitrate-loss mitigation practice. Simulations show it produces the largest reductions in drainage volume and nitrate loss among alternative practices \parencite{Luo2010,Ma2007b}, cutting tile flow and nitrogen by about 30\% when the outlet is raised in fall and winter \parencite{Ahuja2007} and reducing nitrate loss by 22--39\%, with the effect increasing with rainfall and mostly diverted to lateral seepage and crop uptake \parencite{Fang2012,Negm2017}. DRAINMOD-N\,II projected long-term nitrate-load reductions of 26--35\% across rotations in North Carolina and Illinois, and an efficient approximate method reproduced these effects within 3\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ in most years \parencite{Skaggs2012,Negm2016}. A large multi-site synthesis found controlled drainage had no statistically significant average effect on corn yield (10.62 versus 10.53\,Mg\,ha$^{-1}$), but strongly modulated yield by moisture regime---raising yield 4--14\% in dry seasons and lowering it 4--10\% in wet seasons ($R^2 = 0.67$ against dry/wet stress) \parencite{Youssef2023}. A rainfall-probability-index approach for controlled-drainage and sub-irrigation systems in Louisiana reduced excess and deficit soil water and raised relative maize yields \parencite{COOPER1988}. Winter cover crops were another consistently beneficial practice: simulated winter rye or wheat reduced annual nitrogen loads to tile drains by roughly 19--28\% \parencite{Singer2011}, and HERMES, RZWQM, and APSIM each captured the cover-crop reduction in nitrate loss and flow-weighted concentration \parencite{Malone2017,Gillette2018,Malone2007c}. Crop rotation and tillage effects were also reproduced, with lower drain flow and nitrate loss under corn--soybean than continuous corn \parencite{Ma2007b,Malone2007c}.

\subsection{Climate-change projections from calibrated drainage models}

Once validated, drainage models are widely applied to project climate-change impacts, and their results form a coherent body of evidence on future drainage and nitrate dynamics. In cold and humid regions, warming is projected to increase winter subsurface drainage and shift its timing: under RCP8.5, RZ-SHAW and machine-learning models projected large end-of-century increases in winter drainage and a reversal making February the peak and April the lowest month, accompanied by reduced snow cover, warmer soils, and higher winter evaporation \parencite{Li2025}. In Denmark, ensemble projections indicated higher subsurface drainage on sandy loam and increased autumn/winter rainfall \parencite{Rasmussen2018}, while in France the annual maximum discharge and peak flows changed significantly under all scenarios, with the drainage season shortening and the network saturating earlier under the most severe scenario \parencite{Jeantet2022}. Elsewhere, projections diverge with local precipitation trends: drainage and nitrate fluxes increased under warming in rainfed Iran (growing-season drainage up 25--112\% and nitrate fluxes up 4--125\%) \parencite{Haghnazari2020}, whereas in Michigan drainage discharge and water tables were projected to decline and optimal drain spacing to widen \parencite{Dialameh2025}. Nitrate losses were projected to decrease slightly under both scenarios in one Iranian study, with wider spacing preferred environmentally \parencite{Ziaeifar2025}, while a Minnesota analysis concluded that an increasingly wet climate, combined with higher fertiliser rates and greater corn acreage, was the primary driver of rising nitrate export since the 1970s \parencite{Nangia2010b}.

Climate change is also projected to alter crop production and phosphorus losses in drained systems. Corn yields generally declined under future scenarios, driven mainly by heat and drought stress, with corn more affected than soybean \parencite{Adhikari2020,Dialameh2025,Kanber2019}; delayed planting increased canola yield by 20--64\% and water-use efficiency as a key adaptation \parencite{Asgari2022}. In Ontario, combined warming and CO$_2$ enrichment were projected to raise corn and soybean yields by 14--16\% and, through reduced subsurface-drainage phosphorus transport, to lower total dissolved reactive phosphorus loss by 11\% despite higher surface losses \parencite{Wang2021}, and biochar and groundwater-depth management interacted with climate to affect drainage nitrogen losses in China \parencite{Zhang2022b}. APSIM projected slightly deeper future water tables in the U.S. Midwest, with interannual weather variability accounting for the great majority of water-table variance \parencite{Elli2023}. Purpose-built tools such as the loosely coupled drainage--climate interface integrate climatological data with DRAINMOD-based simulation to support county-level evaluation of drainage depth and spacing under current and future climate \parencite{Adhikari2020}.

### Material: Nitrate-N Losses Under Drainage Management

\subsection{Overview}

Subsurface (tile) drainage is a dominant conduit for nitrate-nitrogen (NO$_3$-N) transport from agricultural land, and the way a drainage system is designed and operated exerts strong, quantifiable control over how much nitrate reaches shallow groundwater, drain water, and downstream surface waters. The evidence assembled here spans continuously monitored field plots, drainage-district and watershed outlets, isotopic and gaseous tracer studies, and process-based simulation across North America, Europe, Asia, Australasia, and irrigated arid systems. Several conclusions are consistently supported. First, nitrate is the overwhelmingly dominant form of nitrogen exported in tile discharge, and tile flow itself typically accounts for a majority of watershed water and NO$_3$-N export \parencite{Williams2015,Grose2022,Hayashi1999}. Second, nitrate loads are governed first and foremost by hydrology: annual and event loads scale tightly with drainage flow, so that the great majority of export occurs during high-flow periods of the non-growing season \parencite{Kladivko2021,ROSSI1991,Bauwe2020,Ikenberry2014,Speir2021}. Third, drainage design (drain spacing and depth) and drainage \emph{management} (controlled drainage, subirrigation, and shallow drainage) modulate both flow volume and nitrate delivery, with managed strategies consistently reducing tile NO$_3$-N losses relative to conventional free drainage, primarily by reducing drainage volume \parencite{Cooke2012,Nash2015d,Elmi2004,Smith2019,Ross2016}. Fourth, edge-of-field practices (riparian buffers, integrated buffer zones, denitrification beds) and in-field management (cover crops, fertilizer rate and timing) provide additional, well-quantified reductions \parencite{Jaynes2014,Carstensen2021,Kaspar2007,Chaplot2004}. This section synthesizes the magnitudes, mechanisms, and management responses that the literature has established, together with the greenhouse-gas trade-offs and the modeling tools used to predict them.

\subsection{Nitrate Concentrations and Loads Under Conventional Free Drainage}

\subsubsection{Concentration magnitudes and drinking-water exceedances}

Tile-drainage water in intensively cropped landscapes carries persistently elevated nitrate. In three Iowa drainage districts, tile water averaged about 13 mg\,L$^{-1}$ NO$_3$-N (range 2.3--21.0), exceeding the 10 mg\,L$^{-1}$ drinking-water standard roughly 85--90\% of the time, with annual loads above 33 kg\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ \parencite{Schilling2012b}; a companion drainage-district study reported outlet concentrations of 0--29.3 mg\,L$^{-1}$ averaging 11.1--14.7 mg\,L$^{-1}$ \parencite{Schilling2013}. Across three tile-drained Iowa catchments, the flow-weighted mean was 17.1 mg\,L$^{-1}$ with 74.2\% of samples exceeding the 10 mg\,L$^{-1}$ maximum contaminant level (MCL) \parencite{Ikenberry2014}. Early Iowa work similarly found flow-weighted NO$_3$-N of 21.0 ppm and individual samples of 2.3--44.2 ppm \parencite{BAKER1975}. Elsewhere, drain-water NO$_3$-N averaged about 10 mg\,N\,L$^{-1}$ (nitrate being $>$90\% of total N) in Japan \parencite{Hayashi1999}, 6.6 $\pm$ 4.4 mg\,L$^{-1}$ (2.3--17.3) in eastern Canada \parencite{Smith2011}, and 14.3 mg\,N\,L$^{-1}$ (volume-weighted) in a dryland Washington catchment \parencite{Kelley2017}. Very high concentrations characterize some European and irrigated systems: Italian drainage water averaged 128, 216, and 94 mg\,NO$_3$\,L$^{-1}$ across three years, mostly exceeding the 90 mg\,NO$_3$\,L$^{-1}$ surface-water limit \parencite{ROSSI1991}; Polish drainage water usually exceeded 70 mg\,L$^{-1}$ (maximum 98.5) \parencite{Dragon2016}; and Iranian drain effluent ranged from 0.5 to 28.6 mg\,L$^{-1}$ \parencite{DarziNaftchali2017}. In a German tile-drained catchment, 15\% of high-frequency data exceeded the drinking-water limit despite low soil-surface N surplus \parencite{Bauwe2020}. By contrast, a long-term Indiana experiment kept annual NO$_3$-N generally in the 4--9 mg\,L$^{-1}$ range, below 10 mg\,L$^{-1}$, through reduced fertilizer rates and cover cropping \parencite{Kladivko2021,Kladivko2004}. In organic (peat) soils, drainage NO$_3$-N was even higher, averaging 14.8--42.7 mg\,L$^{-1}$ \parencite{MILLER1979}.

\subsubsection{Load magnitudes and variability}

Annual nitrate loads are highly variable and strongly tied to water yield. Iowa tile systems exported roughly 15--40 kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ \parencite{Kelley2017,Schilling2012b,Ikenberry2014}, with early measurements spanning 0--93 kg\,ha$^{-1}$ and losses "highly variable and strongly dependent on the amount of water lost" \parencite{BAKER1975}. An Ontario field exported 39.1 kg\,ha$^{-1}$ over the monitoring period (flow-weighted 7.0 mg\,L$^{-1}$) \parencite{Smith2011}; an Indiana site accumulated 30.7 kg\,ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Williams2022}. Italian losses averaged about 214 kg\,NO$_3$\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ ($\approx$50 kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$) \parencite{ROSSI1991}. In Japanese onion systems an estimated 179 kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$ (58\% of applied N) was leached by subsurface drainage \parencite{Hayashi1999}, and Latvian sites lost on average 14.3\% of applied mineral fertilizer N (net 18.90 kg\,ha$^{-1}$), representing an economic loss of about 61 EUR\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ \parencite{Siksnane2017}. Under irrigated arid conditions, nitrate movement below the root zone reached 44--153 kg\,NO$_3$-N\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ depending on crop and fertilization \parencite{ROBBINS1980}. These magnitudes reflect that NO$_3$-N losses to subsurface drains arise chiefly from asynchrony between mineralization and crop uptake and from large pools of potentially mineralizable soil organic N \parencite{Cambardella1999}.

\subsection{Hydrologic Control of Nitrate Export}

\subsubsection{Flow--load coupling and high-flow dominance}

The single most robust finding across scales is that nitrate load is controlled by drainage discharge. Annual nitrate-N losses were strongly related to annual drainage flow, with differences among drain spacings well explained by flow ($p<.001$) \parencite{Kladivko2021}. Annual discharge had a strong positive relationship with annual NO$_3$-N losses at both plot and ditch scales in Germany, leading the authors to conclude that hydrology is the key control on nitrate export \parencite{Bauwe2020}. In Italy, NO$_3$ losses correlated strongly with drainage water ($r=0.93$) but only weakly with precipitation ($r=0.45$) \parencite{ROSSI1991}, and Canadian drain NO$_3$-N concentrations rose with flow rate ($R^2=0.94$) \parencite{Smith2011}. High-flow periods dominate export: more than half of total nitrate export in Iowa catchments occurred during the highest 10\% of daily discharges, and nearly all during the upper half of daily flows \parencite{Ikenberry2014}; in Indiana watersheds, 79--95\% of NO$_3$-N export occurred at the 60th--100th percentile of flows \parencite{Speir2021}; and in Iowa BMP catchments the highest 50\% of flows carried most of the load \parencite{Law2022}. High-frequency monitoring of a drained North Carolina field likewise showed the highest flows accounting for a disproportionate share of nitrate loss \parencite{Liu2020}.

\subsubsection{Seasonality}

Because loads track flow, they are strongly seasonal. About 70\% of annual drainage and nitrate-N load occurred in the November--April fallow season in Indiana \parencite{Kladivko2021,Kladivko2004}. Nitrate losses in Italy peaked in winter and early spring \parencite{ROSSI1991}, and in Iowa most NO$_3$-N losses occurred when crops were absent (November--May) \parencite{Cambardella1999}. Watershed and tile NO$_3$-N loading occurred mainly during larger winter and spring flow events \parencite{Williams2015}, and seasonal patterns extended to loads highest in late spring and fall \parencite{Chaplot2004}. Short high-drainage episodes can dominate: 21 kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$ was lost in only 11 days during snowmelt in Japan \parencite{Hayashi1999}. Concentration seasonality is also common---lowest in summer and highest in fall in Canada \parencite{Smith2011}, and declining to $\leq$5 mg\,L$^{-1}$ in late summer and $<$2 mg\,L$^{-1}$ during extended dry periods in Iowa \parencite{Schilling2013}. In dryland Washington systems, sudden early-winter increases in tile discharge and nitrate followed about 150--160 mm of fall precipitation that saturated the profile and mobilized high-nitrate soil water \parencite{Kelley2017,Keller2008}. Summer concentration spikes during corn years had minimal effect on annual loads because drainage was low then \parencite{Kladivko2021}.

\subsubsection{Concentration--discharge and hysteresis behavior}

High-frequency records reveal characteristic concentration--discharge (C--Q) signatures. Annual NO$_3$-N export is largely chemostatic---yields increase proportionately with runoff \parencite{Speir2021}---and persistent chemostasis (log-log slope magnitude $<$0.04) has been documented in Illinois tile drainage \parencite{Hu2024}. Watershed NO$_3$-N concentration increased with discharge up to the 75th flow percentile and then reached an asymptote in Ohio \parencite{Williams2015}. Seasonally, chemostatic fall and spring behavior can shift to source or transport limitation in winter and summer \parencite{Speir2021}. Storm-event hysteresis is predominantly counterclockwise (anti-clockwise): 77\% of storms in Indiana \parencite{Speir2021}, most of 22 delineated events in North Carolina \parencite{Liu2020}, and $>$75\% of events (annual mean hysteresis index $-0.34$) in a long-term Ohio record \parencite{Liu2022b}, indicating dominance of distal nitrate sources during delivery. Flushing versus dilution was more evenly split, with the flushing index near zero overall and strong seasonality (dilution in winter/summer, flushing in spring/fall) \parencite{Liu2022b,Speir2021}. The Ohio analysis concluded that intensive subsurface drainage, rather than deep groundwater, was the dominant factor shaping nitrate C--Q relationships, and that the Richards--Baker flashiness index tracked the history of drainage installation \parencite{Liu2022b}. At the event scale, total storm nitrate load is controlled by the same factors that dictate tile runoff, which in turn emerges from a threshold in above- and below-tile antecedent storage and soil-water mobilization \parencite{Cain2022}. Storm nitrate yields were strongly driven by event runoff ($R^2$ up to 0.97), while antecedent precipitation was a poor predictor \parencite{Speir2021}. In some settings nitrate load, but not concentration, was related to hydrograph metrics \parencite{Williams2022}, and tile discharge showed threshold behavior once soil-moisture deficit exceeded $-17\pm3$ mm and water table rose above $0.84\pm0.03$ m \parencite{Williams2022}.

\subsubsection{Rapid tile response and preferential flow}

Tile nitrate responds within hours of water inputs. After irrigation, tile nitrate rose sharply within hours, stayed high for days, then dropped abruptly \parencite{Mohanty1998}, and modeling indicated preferential flow intercepted by the drain originated close to and above the tile \parencite{Mohanty1998}. Bypass (macropore) flow was identified as a major mechanism carrying most leached nitrogen in Japan \parencite{Hayashi1999}. In cracking clay soils, summer drought produced nitrate-rich salt crusts that were rapidly mobilized to tile drains by crack flow during the first post-drought rains \parencite{Colombani2016}. Pulses or waves of water with differing NO$_3$-N concentrations moving through the profile were invoked to explain abrupt concentration changes in tile water \parencite{BAKER1975}. Higher-drainage catchments routed more event water (peak event-water fractions of 50\% versus 12\%) and produced larger, earlier solute pulses than low-drainage catchments \parencite{Kennedy2012}.

\subsection{Drainage Design: Drain Spacing and Depth}

Drain spacing and depth are established design controls on both drainage volume and nitrate loss. More closely spaced drains produce greater drainage per unit area and greater nitrate-N loss than wider spacings \parencite{Kladivko2021,Kladivko2004}; in Indiana the 5-m spacing generally yielded the most drainage and the 20-m the least, and relative flow differences among spacings narrowed as the system aged \parencite{Kladivko2004,Kladivko2021}. Importantly, nitrate \emph{concentrations} generally did not vary consistently with spacing---loads differences were driven by flow \parencite{Kladivko2021,Kladivko2004}. Modeling in China showed nitrogen loss increasing with drain spacing (as a second-degree parabola) and linearly with drain depth, and increasing with wider/higher filters \parencite{Tao2021}. Shallower drains were more effective at removing excess water but caused more nitrate loss, and in an Iranian heavy soil spacings $>$40 m could not combat waterlogging; the D0.40L50 configuration was judged best from combined nitrate and water-loss viewpoints \parencite{Hashemi2020}. In salt-affected coastal clay soils, wider spacing enhanced the risk of nitrate leaching and groundwater pollution relative to narrower spacing, which removed nitrate faster and added less to groundwater \parencite{Singh2002,Singh2007,Singh2001c}. A meta-analysis found greater relative reductions in tile discharge under drainage water management with narrower spacing and deeper drains \parencite{Ross2016}.

\subsection{Managed Drainage: Controlled Drainage, Subirrigation, and Shallow Drainage}

Managed drainage strategies consistently reduce nitrate loss compared with conventional free drainage, and the literature establishes both the magnitude and the mechanism.

\subsubsection{Controlled drainage and drainage water management}

In Illinois, annual NO$_3$ loads from managed systems were always less than from free drainage, with flow reductions of 35--96\% and NO$_3$ load reductions of 37--79\% (averaging 61\% across sites) \parencite{Cooke2012}. A synthesis reported drainage water management reducing tile flow by about 46\% and tile NO$_3$-N loads by about 45--48\%, with the caveat that surface runoff can increase and that reductions arise mainly from reduced tile discharge rather than enhanced denitrification \parencite{Ross2016}. Reviews likewise cite controlled drainage reducing NO$_3$-N exports by about 48\% \parencite{Ward2018}. In Missouri claypan soils, managed drainage cut annual water drained by at least 73\% and reduced annual NO$_3$-N loss by 78--85\% in two of four years (e.g., 3.8 versus 25.5 kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$, an 85\% reduction) \parencite{Nash2015d}. In China, controlled drainage significantly reduced groundwater NO$_3$-N, NH$_4$-N, and total phosphorus, with the highest NO$_3$-N reduction reaching 73.2\% \parencite{Cui2016}. Submerged pipe (raised-outlet) operation reduced NO$_3$-N losses by 24\% and 51\% at 20-cm and 40-cm submergence, respectively, relative to free outflow \parencite{Tao2021}.

\subsubsection{Subirrigation and water-table management}

Subirrigation (SI) lowered soil-profile NO$_3$-N by up to 50\% relative to free drainage and reduced drainage-water NO$_3$-N concentrations by 55--73\% \parencite{Elmi2004}. Seasonal mean drainage-effluent NO$_3$-N fell by 74\%, 55\%, and 64\% across three years, and losses dropped from 7.2 to 1.5 kg\,ha$^{-1}$ in one year \parencite{Elmi2004}. SI reduced residual soil nitrate by 19--45\% and produced more rapid nitrate decline with depth, with water-quality benefits accruing even when SI outflow occasionally exceeded free-drainage outflow---implicating enhanced denitrification and reduced loading rather than merely reduced volume \parencite{Elmi2002}. Water-table control that elevated the water table enhanced denitrification and N$_2$O evolution while reducing potential N transport to surface water, denitrification increasing as the water table became shallower \parencite{KLIEWER1995}. Controlled drainage--subirrigation combined with a cover crop reduced five-year tile NO$_3$ concentration and loss by about 47\% (from 9.45 to 4.99 mg\,N\,L$^{-1}$ and 102 to 53.6 kg\,N\,ha$^{-1}$) \parencite{Drury2014}.

\subsubsection{Shallow drainage}

Comparative trials show shallow drainage reduces flow and load strongly. In Canada, shallow drainage reduced flow by 38--64\% relative to deep/conventional drainage (controlled drainage reduced flow by 24--37\%), and controlled drainage reduced NO$_3$-N loading by 42.3--58.2\% in two of three years \parencite{Smith2019}. Shallow drainage had the lowest mean nitrate concentration and lowest loading among three systems and exceeded the drinking-water guideline least often \parencite{Smith2011b,Smith2019}, though shallow drainage sometimes reduced yields whereas controlled drainage trended toward higher yields \parencite{Smith2019,Smith2011b}. Locally repairing or blocking a drainage section measurably altered whole-system runoff and, when drains were blocked to simulate controlled drainage, reduced ammonia and bacteria concentrations \parencite{Wesström2015,RITTER1995}.

\subsection{Fertilizer, Cropping, and Cover-Crop Controls on Nitrate Loss}

Nitrogen input rate is a strong lever on nitrate load but has a weaker, less consistent influence on concentration than hydrology does. Modeling showed that changing N application by $-60$\% to $+40$\% did not affect flow or sediment but strongly affected NO$_3$-N loads, with 20/40/60\% input cuts reducing loads by 23/49/94\% \parencite{Chaplot2004}. Fertilizer rate above recommendations pushed weekly drainage NO$_3$-N above 10 mg\,L$^{-1}$ and increased annual losses to 4--64 kg\,ha$^{-1}$ in mineral soils \parencite{MILLER1979}. Yet several long-term records found that reducing fertilizer rate did not immediately reduce nitrate export---precipitation, not fertilizer rate, predicted annual flux at a Washington catchment \parencite{Kelley2017}, and NO$_3$-N temporal patterns were unrelated to fertilizer timing or amount in Iowa \parencite{Cambardella1999}, consistent with soil-N legacy effects that may delay management responses by years to decades \parencite{Bauwe2020}. Latvian data showed no correlation between fertilizer incorporated and N lost \parencite{Siksnane2017}. Incidental loss within 30 days of fertilizer application nonetheless accounted for 52\% of cumulative tile NO$_3$-N load at one Indiana site \parencite{Williams2022}, and fall soil NO$_3$-N was the strongest control on leachate NO$_3$-N ($r^2=0.76$) in North Dakota, where careful N management returned leachate and groundwater NO$_3$-N toward pre-irrigation levels \parencite{Derby2009}.

Nitrogen source and timing generally had modest tile-drainage effects: manure versus urea did not differ in NO$_3$-N losses over multiple years \parencite{Randall2000}, and a large ($\sim$1,000 site-year) analysis found no significant difference in tile N export by application timing or method \parencite{Ward2018}. Isotopic evidence, however, showed pulsed, fertilizer-driven shifts in the NO$_3$ source signature during the corn growing season \parencite{Hu2024}. Cropping system matters strongly: replacing corn--soybean with pasture reduced NO$_3$-N losses to $<$3.5 t\,yr$^{-1}$ \parencite{Chaplot2004}; potato rotations produced the highest losses, and a winter-wheat cover after potato cut flow-weighted nitrate by 30\% to below 10 mg\,L$^{-1}$ \parencite{RITTER1995}; and alfalfa in irrigated rotations contributed substantial residual nitrate to subsequent crops \parencite{ROBBINS1980}. Cover crops are among the best-quantified in-field practices: a rye winter cover crop reduced flow-weighted NO$_3$ concentrations by 59\% and loads by 61\% over four years, chiefly by lowering concentration rather than drainage volume, whereas a permanent gamagrass strip did not significantly reduce losses \parencite{Kaspar2007}. Cover crops reduced tile NO$_3$-N by 21--38\% (concentration) and 14--16\% (load) in Canada while modestly increasing drainage volume \parencite{Drury2014}, and reviews report cover-crop NO$_3$-N reductions of 13--94\% \parencite{Ward2018}. Long-term declines in Indiana NO$_3$-N were attributed to combined reductions in fertilizer rate, rotation and tillage changes, and a cover crop acting as a trap crop \parencite{Kladivko2004,Kladivko2021}.

\subsection{Sources, Transport Pathways, and Transformations}

\subsubsection{Isotopic source apportionment and denitrification}

Dual-isotope studies indicate that drainage nitrate is often derived largely from organic sources (manure and soil organic matter) via nitrification, with denitrification not always dominant. In Ontario, $\delta^{15}$N and $\delta^{18}$O relationships lacked the 2:1 denitrification slope, pointing to organic sources and continued mineralization/nitrification through all seasons \parencite{Smith2011,Smith2011b}. Controlled incubation and field isotope work in Illinois quantified oxygen-isotope exchange during nitrification (exchange fractions of 59\% fertilized versus 0\% non-fertilized) and showed tile-drainage NO$_3$ isotopes were highly responsive to discharge and fertilizer input, with strong $\delta^{15}$N--$\delta^{18}$O coupling in unfertilized periods \parencite{Hu2024}. Where denitrification is active, it is detectable isotopically and geochemically: along Polish groundwater flow paths nitrate declined while sulfate, hardness, excess N$_2$, $\delta^{15}$N (9.9--23.5 per mil), and $\delta^{18}$O (7.9--17.2 per mil) increased \parencite{Dragon2016}. Nested French watersheds showed $^{15}$N enrichment as nitrate declined, and nitrate budgets implied 34--82\% retention dominated by underground (not benthic) denitrification \parencite{Billy2011,Billy2013}. In Iowa drainageways, denitrification in slowly permeable soils could remove essentially all upland groundwater nitrate, providing a natural treatment system \parencite{Schilling2007}, and deep unoxidized loess had the highest denitrification potential, with a large lifetime subsoil NO$_3$ removal capacity \parencite{Cambardella1999}. In arid Idaho, comparison of root-zone leaching with drainage concentrations (mean 3.2 ppm) suggested considerable denitrification and/or dilution at the water-table interface \parencite{ROBBINS1980}.

\subsubsection{Preferential flow, hydrograph sources, and legacy}

Tile discharge is dominated by pre-event water. Three-component separation showed tile flow was 65\% groundwater and 29\% shallow soil water, with precipitation only 6\% \parencite{Williams2022}. At the watershed scale, shallow groundwater supplied 86\% of tile flow, and tile drains carried the highest NO$_3$-N of any source, contributing over 99\% of watershed-scale nitrate export during a monitoring budget \parencite{Grose2022}. Tile drainage acts as a rapid conduit that limits infiltration of solutes into the subsoil: for a 100 kg\,ha$^{-1}$ urea scenario, 39\% of nitrate-N was leached through tile drains rather than reaching the subsoil, and without drainage nitrate could reach the profile bottom and groundwater \parencite{Filipovic2015}. Repair of a Swedish drainage system increased the proportion of infiltrated NO$_3$-N delivered directly to the stream via the tile outlet rather than diffusely via groundwater \parencite{Wesström2015}. Landscape position strongly differentiates groundwater chemistry, with oxic, high-nitrate uplands (8.5--12 mg\,L$^{-1}$) contrasting with reduced, low-nitrate drainageways ($<$0.5 mg\,L$^{-1}$) \parencite{Schilling2007}. Nitrate concentrations were consistently higher in tile drainage water than in water below the root zone and decreased with depth below the drains \parencite{Randall2000}, while subsurface-drainage NO$_3$-N averaged only 20\% of shallow-groundwater concentrations and lagged by 1--2 years \parencite{Derby2009}. Because losses stem from asynchronous mineralization and uptake and large mineralizable-N pools, land-management effects may only become visible after years to decades owing to soil-N legacies \parencite{Cambardella1999,Bauwe2020}. Rapid nitrification of applied ammonium ($>$90\% converted to NO$_3$ within 72 h) provides a ready nitrate supply for leaching \parencite{Hu2024}.

\subsection{Watershed-Scale Integration and Downstream Fate}

Tile drainage is the primary pathway controlling watershed water and nitrogen export in drained landscapes. In Ohio, tile drainage accounted for 56\% of annual watershed discharge (37--74\%) and 44--82\% (mean 62\%) of watershed NO$_3$-N export, with strong month-to-month coupling between tile and watershed loads \parencite{Williams2015}. Tile drains contributed an average of 57\% of streamflow and dominated watershed NO$_3$-N export in Indiana, where three large county drains disproportionately delivered 69\% of watershed nitrate, obscuring field-scale cover-crop effects \parencite{Grose2022}. Nitrate delivery from intensively drained Iowa landscapes can be spatially and within-season homogeneous, with the largest drains contributing 70--87\% of exported water and nitrate \parencite{Coupe2020}. Scaling drainage-district losses to the receiving Boone River suggested drainage districts could account for essentially all river nitrate export in most years, underscoring drainage-district-scale management \parencite{Ikenberry2014}. Downstream, concentrations decline with drainage area---e.g., from tile outlets averaging $\sim$13 mg\,L$^{-1}$ to 8.4, 7.6, 6.4, and 4.2 mg\,L$^{-1}$ progressively downstream in Iowa \parencite{Schilling2012b}---and nitrate is generally lowest in forested sub-catchments and highest in drained croplands \parencite{Billy2013}. Substantial in-network retention (34--82\%) occurs via underground denitrification \parencite{Billy2011}, but in-stream processing immediately below tile outlets offered little potential for meaningful downstream reduction because its timing was poorly matched to seasonal loading \parencite{Schilling2012b}. Achieving downstream targets was estimated to require about a 55\% load reduction at the tile outlet \parencite{Schilling2012b}. Regional analyses show catchment-outlet nitrate flux driven by both discharge and concentration, whereas tile and open-drain fluxes are overwhelmingly discharge-controlled, and groundwater plus farm-scale drainage inputs explained about 60\% of downstream N-load variability \parencite{Goeller2019}. Long-term monitoring can detect improvement: Czech outlet N-NO$_3$ declined from 1,175 to 587 $\mu$g\,L$^{-1}$ between 2004 and 2018 \parencite{Fraindová2022}, and stacked catchment-scale BMPs reduced NO$_3$-N load by 60.3\%, most strongly at high flow \parencite{Law2022}. Paired-watershed designs at drainage-district tile outfalls are feasible, with minimum detectable change around 8\% for the best control--treatment pair and improved detection when restricted to the March--July drainage period \parencite{Schilling2013}.

\subsection{Edge-of-Field and Downstream Mitigation}

Beyond in-field practices, edge-of-field structures provide well-documented nitrate removal. Redirecting tile flow into a riparian buffer diverted 55\% of tile flow (over 18,000 m$^3$) as infiltration, raised the water table $\sim$35 cm, and removed nitrate at 1.01 $\pm$ 0.07 mg\,N\,L$^{-1}$\,m$^{-1}$; chloride showed no gradient, confirming denitrification rather than dilution drove the decline \parencite{Jaynes2014}. Integrated buffer zones reduced drainage total-N loss by 29\% and 71\% at two sites (removal of 77 and 237 g\,N\,m$^{-2}$\,yr$^{-1}$, mostly nitrate), with the filterbed accounting for 70--93\% of NO$_3$ removal; hydraulic short-circuiting via overflow pipes and preferential surface paths limited performance \parencite{Carstensen2021}. Denitrification (woodchip) beds followed Michaelis--Menten kinetics (half-saturation constant 7.2 mg\,N\,L$^{-1}$, maximum rate 7.1 mg\,N\,L$^{-1}$\,h$^{-1}$ at 23.5\,$^{\circ}$C, $Q_{10}=2.8$), with seasonal removal of 0.01--4.64 mg\,N\,L$^{-1}$\,h$^{-1}$ and very low greenhouse-gas surface emissions \parencite{Ghane2015}. Saturated riparian buffers removed nitrate without increasing N$_2$O emissions, keeping total N$_2$O similar to traditional buffers and below corn--soybean rotations, and reducing indirect downstream N$_2$O \parencite{Davis2019}. Reviews also credit vegetated ditches, gypsum amendment, blind/gravel inlets, and BMP combinations (a SWAT analysis suggested $\sim$60\% pollutant-load reductions) with nutrient mitigation, though effects on dissolved phosphorus can differ from nitrate \parencite{Ward2018}. Ephemeral drainageways can themselves function as natural nitrate treatment systems \parencite{Schilling2007}.

\subsection{Greenhouse-Gas Dimensions of Drainage and Nitrate}

Drainage nitrate is coupled to greenhouse-gas fluxes, both directly and indirectly. Drainage water is commonly supersaturated with dissolved N$_2$O, CH$_4$, and CO$_2$; in Japan, indirect emissions via subsurface drainage were 0.747 kg\,N$_2$O-N, 0.115 kg\,CH$_4$-C, and 132 kg\,CO$_2$-C\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$---equivalent to 4.6\%, 58.3\%, and 2.5\% of direct emissions---with N$_2$O-N/NO$_3$-N ratios (0.00076--0.0105) below the IPCC factor of 0.015 \parencite{Sawamoto2003}. Dissolved N$_2$O emissions were largest in upland-rice plots where leached N was greatest (50--67\% of aboveground emissions), and EF5g values exceeded the IPCC default by more than threefold in some systems \parencite{Minamikawa2010}. The presence of a groundwater table increased total N$_2$O emissions via dissolved pathways even under zero fertilizer-N \parencite{Minamikawa2013}. Two-phase co-evolution of [NO$_3^-$], [N$_2$O], and [CO$_2$] during non-rainy periods traced heterotrophic denitrification, yielding drainage-weighted EF5g of 0.0084 \parencite{Minamikawa2015}. In Canada, dissolved N$_2$O losses in tile drainage were substantive---approaching surface N$_2$O emissions---strongly seasonal (up to 62\% in the non-growing season), and declined along tile length, implying an in-drain sink and likely underestimation of the IPCC drainage coefficient \parencite{Burton2024}. A global synthesis of 52 studies found subsurface drains had the highest mean emission factor among artificial waters, that EFs scale downward with increasing NO$_3$ loading, and that the stationary IPCC EF tends to overestimate N$_2$O production \parencite{Webb2021}.

Conversely, installing subsurface drainage sharply reduces methane in paddy systems by improving aeration: drained fields emitted 71\% less CH$_4$ than non-drained fields (3.5 versus 14.8 g\,CH$_4$\,m$^{-2}$), with faster post-drainage Eh recovery and lower pre-submergence soil moisture \parencite{Shiratori2007}. Drainage lowered methane and CO$_2$ production potential by $\sim$40\% through reduced substrate availability and more oxidative conditions, accelerating rice-straw decomposition \parencite{Furukawa2008}. Management and fertilizer choices modulate N$_2$O: polymer-coated urea reduced soil N$_2$O by 47\% and NH$_3$ volatilization by 73\% versus non-coated urea, whereas tile drainage itself did not affect gaseous N loss in a claypan system \parencite{Nash2015e}. Controlled drainage kept cumulative N$_2$O below 1\% of applied N across drainage types \parencite{Smith2019}, and integrated buffer zones acted as net sinks of waterborne N$_2$O while remaining net CH$_4$ sources \parencite{Carstensen2021}. In saline systems, cumulative N$_2$O rose with wider drain spacing and lower organic-fertilizer replacement (up to 17.6 kg\,ha$^{-1}$ under 0\% organic, 20-m spacing) \parencite{Zhai2023}. Adding tile drainage to diversion terraces and grassed waterways reduced CO$_2$ and N$_2$O emissions relative to non-tiled structures by regulating soil water \parencite{Driscoll2025}. Carbon export from drained fields is dominated by inorganic carbon through tiles ($\sim$96\% of total carbon export; $\sim$1107 kg\,ha$^{-1}$ dissolved carbon leached in one year), so reducing water throughput supports carbon retention \parencite{Schilling2023}.

\subsection{Saline/Sodic Reclamation and Specialized Cropping Systems}

In salt-affected soils, drainage design governs both reclamation and nitrogen dynamics. Sustained subsurface drainage lowered soil salinity from 35 to 4 dS\,m$^{-1}$ at 15-m spacing and improved rice yield (6.5 versus 1.8--1.9 Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ at wide spacings), with narrower spacing favoring nitrate removal and reduced groundwater loading, while wider spacing allowed ammonium losses and impaired yields \parencite{Singh2002,Singh2001c}. Total N loss ranged 3.7--23.5 kg\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ across spacings, and subsurface drainage caused roughly 3--20\% loss of applied N through effluent \parencite{Singh2002}. Discontinuing drainage caused nitrite to accumulate 18--34-fold over three years as salinity inhibited nitrification, whereas functioning drainage promoted microbial activity, nitrification, and ammonium storage on the clay complex \parencite{Singh2007}. In subsurface-drained paddies, adding a canola crop improved soil-N utilization and considerably decreased N losses relative to fallow, keeping health-risk quotients below thresholds \parencite{DarziNaftchali2017}. Combining subsurface drainage with organic--inorganic fertilization improved saline soils, crop yield, and quality: smaller spacing and higher organic fertilizer raised alfalfa and sunflower yields and reduced surface-soil salinity (e.g., 36--46\% reduction at close spacing), though higher organic fertilizer could increase surface salt content \parencite{Zhang2024d,Zhai2023}. Optimization identified 10-m spacing with 75\% organic plus 25\% inorganic N as best for sunflower under TOPSIS evaluation \parencite{Zhu2025}. In irrigated sugarcane, a field N balance attributed 43\% of N input to nitrate drainage loss, 13\% to ammonium drainage loss, 9\% to denitrification, and 31\% to uptake \parencite{Matinzadeh2017}, while sewage-irrigation modeling identified denitrification as the main loss pathway with no soil-profile N accumulation up to $\sim$66 mg\,L$^{-1}$ \parencite{Yang2008}.

\subsection{Modeling Nitrate Dynamics Under Drainage}

Process-based models reproduce drainage nitrate concentrations and loads well after site-specific calibration and are established tools for evaluating management scenarios. DRAINMOD-N II and RZWQM2 both satisfactorily predicted tile NO$_3$-N in Iowa, with DRAINMOD-N II performing slightly better (concentration NSE 0.87 versus 0.76; loss NSE 0.84 versus 0.64) and both yielding threshold N rates close to field observations \parencite{Du2017}. RZWQM2 independently tracked NO$_3$-N concentration across N rates and identified an MCL-meeting application rate consistent with long-term data \parencite{Qi2012}. DRAINMOD-N II reproduced daily nitrate variation in an Iranian heavy soil, though it overestimated total seasonal losses, and it ranked drainage designs by combined nitrate/water-loss criteria \parencite{Hashemi2020}. HYDRUS captured how rainfall intensity/timing, initial groundwater depth, soil-N content, and submerged-pipe height controlled N loss \parencite{Tao2021}. A field-scale system-dynamics model matched cumulative nitrate loss (168.71 versus 176.68 kg\,ha$^{-1}$) and identified mineralization and denitrification rate constants as the most sensitive parameters \parencite{Matinzadeh2017}. The parsimonious NIT-DRAIN model simulated hourly nitrate concentrations with high performance (e.g., $R^2=0.83$, NSE 0.83, KGE 0.86), but its accuracy depended heavily on discharge inputs, degrading sharply when simulated rather than observed discharge was used \parencite{Chelil2022}. A FILTER-type model reproduced water tables and drainage nitrate for sewage irrigation \parencite{Yang2008}, and a CHAIN\_2D formulation with bimodal hydraulic functions improved simulation of preferential tile-flow nitrate transport \parencite{Mohanty1998}. At larger scales, SWAT estimated flow, NO$_3$-N, and sediment with monthly mean errors under 10\% and quantified farming-practice effects \parencite{Chaplot2004}, and a mechanistic denitrification-bed model coupling Forchheimer, Michaelis--Menten, and Arrhenius terms predicted outflow nitrate satisfactorily ($R^2=0.86$, NSE 0.85) \parencite{Ghane2015}. Groundwater flow-and-transport modeling showed that drainageway denitrification at 0.015 day$^{-1}$ could reduce upland nitrate to below detection at the catchment outlet, and that adding tile drainage would increase catchment drainage by 22.5 mm\,yr$^{-1}$ and deliver an additional 12.3 kg\,yr$^{-1}$ of nitrate \parencite{Schilling2007}.

### Material: Manure Application Effects on Soil and Drainage Microbiology

\subsection{Overview}

Manure and other organic amendments sit at the intersection of two functions of subsurface (tile) drainage systems: they enrich soils with nutrients and organic matter, and they introduce fecal microorganisms, resistance genes, hormones, and trace elements that drainage networks can rapidly route to receiving waters. The evidence assembled here establishes several consolidated conclusions. First, the \emph{method} of manure application and the \emph{moisture state} of the soil, rather than manure presence alone, largely govern how much fecal contamination reaches tile drains, with preferential (macropore) flow acting as the dominant transport mechanism \parencite{Truhlar2015,Thiagarajan2007,Collins2007,Ding2021}. Second, manure and organic amendments deliver measurable agronomic and soil-structural benefits---reclamation of saline--sodic soils, higher yields, and, when combined with subsurface drainage and biochar, improved soil physical properties and nitrogen-use efficiency---though some physical benefits are transient \parencite{Qadir2001,Tian2023b,Liu2025f,Xiong2025,Brikmans2025}. Third, drainage systems and their engineered analogues (denitrifying bioreactors, constructed wetlands, reservoirs, and reconstructed oxbows) host distinct and dynamic microbial communities and can substantially attenuate nitrate and pathogen loads \parencite{Andrus2014,Porter2015,Hathaway2015,Knorr2023,Schilling2017,Haverstock2017}. Fourth, drainage water can concentrate and mobilise trace elements---most infamously selenium at Kesterson, but also arsenic, boron, uranium, and micronutrients---with documented consequences for wildlife \parencite{SAIKI1987,Ohlendorf2002,Ryu2010,Gao2007,Tayfur2010,Gul2013}. Finally, subsurface drainage reshapes the ecology of agricultural landscapes, from soil fauna to birds, amphibians, and semi-aquatic species \parencite{Nuutinen2001,Marja2013,Dreyfuss2018,Campbell2023}. This section synthesises the mechanisms and quantified outcomes underpinning each of these lines of evidence.

\subsection{Manure Application Method, Soil Moisture, and Microbial Transport to Tile Drains}

\subsubsection{Application method controls the transport of fecal bacteria}
Controlled comparisons demonstrate that how manure is placed on the field determines the microbial signal reaching drains. \textcite{Truhlar2015} found that roughly 45\% of \textit{E.\ coli} isolated from manure produced curli (an adhesive amyloid fibre) before field application, and that curli-producing \textit{E.\ coli} were far more abundant in tile drains from farms that surface-applied manure than in the drain of a farm that incorporated it. The same pattern held in soil-column leachate: the proportion of curli-producing \textit{E.\ coli} was greater under surface application than under incorporation or no manure, while total \textit{E.\ coli} recovered from leachate was higher in both manure treatments than in controls but did not differ between surface-applied and incorporated manure. Because the column manure came from a single source, and because the functional gene \textit{csgA} was present in all seven tested strains regardless of curli phenotype, the authors ruled out strain composition as the sole explanation and concluded that application method itself shapes the phenotype of \textit{E.\ coli} transported to drains---a result consistent across two surface-applied periods with different rainfall \parencite{Truhlar2015}. Complementary indicator work found that faecal coliforms, coliphages, and \textit{Salmonella} behaved differently: \textit{Salmonella anatum} was not detected in any of 36 leachate samples, male-specific coliphages were detected in all 36 samples, somatic coliphages were generally lower, and phage counts exceeded bacterial indicator counts in all plots; indicator numbers were similar in manure-treated and control plots, peaking in weeks 1--2 and declining thereafter \parencite{Malik2004}.

\subsubsection{Tillage, preferential flow, and pathogen loads}
Tillage governs pathogen delivery mainly through its effect on macroporosity and flow partitioning. Across two Canadian sites, zero tillage (ZT) generally produced higher subsurface flow volumes and greater \textit{E.\ coli} loads than conventional tillage, which the authors attribute to macropore development and preferential flow \parencite{Thiagarajan2007}. At Streets Ridge, combined-drainage \textit{E.\ coli} loads showed no overall significant tillage effect and the direction of differences switched between years, but when surface and subsurface drainage were separated, subsurface drainage under ZT contributed the greatest \textit{E.\ coli} losses in 2003--2004. At BEEC, tillage did not significantly affect \textit{E.\ coli} concentrations, yet subsurface-drainage loads were significantly higher under ZT in 2003--2004. Most samples exceeded drinking-water guidelines, and recreational-guideline exceedances were concentrated soon after manure application and were greater at Streets Ridge than at BEEC \parencite{Thiagarajan2007}. Hydrologic partitioning by tillage was also evident for manure-borne hormones: \textcite{Jenkins2009} reported that no-till plots had greater drainage and lower runoff than conventional-till plots in June 2002, and that November 2001 runoff loads of estradiol and testosterone were lower from no-till than conventional-till plots.

\subsubsection{Soil moisture, water table, and drain-flow contamination}
Antecedent soil-moisture state and water-table position exert first-order control on contamination episodes. In New Zealand pastoral systems, effluent applied to wet soil produced drain-flow \textit{E.\ coli} of $10^{5}$--$10^{7}$ per 100\,ml, whereas a greater soil-moisture deficit reduced concentrations to about $10^{4}$ per 100\,ml \parencite{Collins2007}. Peak drain-flow \textit{Campylobacter} exceeded $10^{3}$ per 100\,ml following effluent application at negligible soil-moisture deficit, and where preferential flow occurred, drainage concentrations approached those of the applied effluent. Over reported drainage events, \textit{E.\ coli} yield from a 1080\,m$^{2}$ plot ranged between $10^{8}$ and $5\times10^{10}$; installing subsurface drains on Pallic Soils reduced surface runoff by 60--80\%, redirecting water (and its microbial load) to the subsurface pathway \parencite{Collins2007}. Winter reuse of undisinfected wastewater in Ohio showed the same water-table dependence: during a non-irrigated control winter, \textit{E.\ coli} was detected in 2 of 6 subsurface samples when the water table was within the drain depth and briefly after it dropped below the drains; during the winter irrigated season, \textit{E.\ coli} was detected three times, always when the water table rose above the drain under cold, wet soil conditions \parencite{Ding2021}. Soil moisture and temperature were significantly positively related at a given depth, sunlight exposure of wastewater in metal pans for 2--3\,h produced up to 3-log \textit{E.\ coli} reductions, and \textit{E.\ coli} was not detected in groundwater 15\,m from the field---leading the authors to conclude that winter wastewater reuse poses a public-health risk to surface water via subsurface drainage, with irrigation rate and disinfection as key management levers \parencite{Ding2021}. At the pathway scale, \textcite{Collins2007} further documented that direct herd crossings of the Sherry River temporarily raised \textit{E.\ coli} to more than 100 times background (about $10^{11}$ \textit{E.\ coli} deposited during one crossing over 25 defecation events), and estimated a 60--75\% probability of \textit{Campylobacter} infection over an irrigation season from observed groundwater concentrations.

\subsubsection{Antibiotic resistance genes and hormones carried by manure}
Manure is a reservoir of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) that persist through drained soils into drainage water. Shotgun metagenomics identified 320 unique ARGs, with 173 in manure, 176 in soil, and 85 shared; manure treatment significantly shifted the soil and drainage-water resistomes away from the control-soil resistome \parencite{Smith2019b}. Of 88 ARGs unique to the manure resistome, 75 appeared in manure-treated soil and 69 in manure-treated effluent, and 25 showed strong persistence across at least half of samples and all time points. Manure-derived ARGs dominated the abundance in manure-treated effluent (manure-enriched 0.612, water-enriched 0.308, manure persisters 0.071), with \textit{tet}(33) the most abundant persisting sequence, rising over time to become most abundant by day 108. Whereas untreated-soil ARGs were dominated by multidrug-efflux genes, many manure-derived ARGs were associated with mobile genetic elements in food- and water-borne pathogens---evidence that manure-derived genes have strong potential to persist in soil and water in tile-drained landscapes \parencite{Smith2019b}. For steroid hormones, poultry litter did not increase the flow-weighted concentration or load of estradiol or testosterone in subsurface drainage or surface runoff relative to conventional fertiliser/control treatments; both hormones were detected in drainage and runoff but total loads were generally not different among treatments, soil concentrations declined with depth (indicating stratification), and mean bulk-soil testosterone was greater in poultry-litter-amended plots while soil estradiol did not differ \parencite{Jenkins2009}.

\subsection{Organic Amendments and Soil Amelioration in Drained Soils}

\subsubsection{Manure and organic fertiliser for saline--sodic reclamation}
Farm manure is an effective amendment for reclaiming saline--sodic soils. In Pakistan, active desalinisation and desodication occurred in all treatments with non-significant differences among them; after the final wheat crop the 1.2\,m profile reached ECe $\approx 7\pm0.5$\,dS\,m$^{-1}$ and SAR $\approx 15\pm2$, indicating comparable amelioration by phytoremediation, gypsum, sulphuric acid, and farm manure \parencite{Qadir2001}. Farm manure nonetheless produced the best yields, giving 4188\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ for the first wheat crop versus 3462\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ (100\% gypsum), 2854\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ (phytoremediation), 2786\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ (sulphuric acid), and 2754\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$ (50\% gypsum); infiltration rate declined by about 18\% (from $2.03\times10^{-6}$ to $1.67\times10^{-6}$\,m\,s$^{-1}$) with non-significant treatment differences, and phytoremediation required minimal capital input \parencite{Qadir2001}. Under monsoonal Russian conditions, drainage and organic fertiliser had stronger effects on soil pore volume than biochar, with organic fertiliser increasing porosity in 2019 \parencite{Brikmans2025}. A North Dakota case study showed that a legacy of combined subsurface drainage, no-till, gypsum, and cover crops protected crops from salinisation through soil processes not revealed by soil indices alone: in the soil-health-legacy zone, 6 of 9 chemical, 5 of 14 microbial, and none of 3 physical properties differed between areas with dead/damaged plants and living plants, with higher ECe, Olsen-P, Ca, Na, and SAR (and lower pH) where plants were dying, and lower protein, POXC, arbuscular-mycorrhizal fungi, and G+:G- ratio \parencite{Daigh2025}.

\subsubsection{Biochar combined with subsurface drainage on saline--alkali soils}
A consistent body of Chinese coastal-soil experiments shows that pairing biochar with subsurface drainage improves saline--alkali soils beyond either practice alone. Subsurface drainage plus biochar (B-S) significantly reduced soil electrical conductivity and water content relative to no drainage, and increased soil total nitrogen, available phosphorus, nitrate, and organic matter relative to both control and drainage-alone; B-S raised alkaline phosphatase and urease activity, increased bacterial alpha diversity (Chao1, Shannon), and shifted community composition (Proteobacteria dominant), with available phosphorus and organic matter identified as dominant drivers of enzyme activity and bacterial abundance \parencite{Tian2023b}. Narrower drain spacing and higher biochar rates lowered bulk density and raised porosity, field capacity, and soil moisture in the plow layer, affected the entire 0--80\,cm profile, and reduced salinity---with drainage a stronger salinity control than biochar; the S1C3 combination (6\,m spacing, 15\,t\,ha$^{-1}$ biochar) gave the most satisfactory salt reduction and, with the S1 spacing, the largest yield gains \parencite{Liu2025f}. Using $^{15}$N-labelled urea, \textcite{Xiong2025} showed that narrower spacing and more biochar promoted $^{15}$N absorption across all three alfalfa harvests (drainage having the more persistent effect); S1C3 produced the highest uptake (6.8, 15.0, 12.8\,kg\,ha$^{-1}$) and raised $^{15}$N use efficiency to 46.0\% (versus 21.6--28.4\% in controls), with 21.6--46.0\% of applied $^{15}$N taken up by crops, 38.6--67.5\% retained in soil, and 8.5--18.1\% lost. In contrast, biochar's physical benefits in clay-loam soils were transient: dose-dependent decreases in bulk and aggregate density in 2018 dissipated by 2019, total porosity showed no sustained biochar effect, and intra-aggregate pore structure (persistent mesopore dominance) resisted amendment, consistent with biochar occupying inter-aggregate spaces temporarily rather than reshaping intra-aggregate pores \parencite{Brikmans2025}.

\subsection{Soil Physical and Hydraulic Properties under Drainage and Management}

Management practices exert clearer effects on soil structure than tile drainage does in several settings. In South Dakota silty clay loam, no-till significantly increased bulk density, soil organic matter (SOM), and wet aggregate stability (WAS) while decreasing field-saturated hydraulic conductivity (Kfs) relative to conventional tillage; more diverse rotations decreased bulk density and increased SOM and WAS; and cover crops and tile drainage had minimal or no significant impact on SOM, bulk density, WAS, or Kfs \parencite{Saha2024}. The tillage$\times$rotation interaction was significant (e.g., higher Kfs under conventional tillage than no-till in the 2-year rotation), bulk density was negatively correlated with SOM and WAS, SOM was strongly negatively correlated with pH, WAS was positively correlated with SOM, and stacking no-till with cover crops had no significant added effect \parencite{Saha2024}. Grass ley consistently improved surface aggregate stability of both drained and undrained soils relative to spring barley underseeded with clover, with improvement significantly correlated with rising soil organic carbon; the grass/drained treatment gave the greatest structural improvement, while the grass/undrained treatment matched it only when the soil was dry, and aggregate stability varied seasonally (lower in early spring, higher in fall) with soil water content \parencite{Hermawan1996}. On an Australian sodic soil, a drainage system functioned adequately (rapid post-rainfall drainage, clear drainage water), sunflower seed yield averaged 1.2\,t\,ha$^{-1}$ and was unaffected by drainage or gypsum, but wheat establishment was much lower on the undrained fallow, no-gypsum treatment (45 versus 214 plants\,m$^{-2}$); gypsum raised EC by $\sim$0.2\,dS\,m$^{-1}$, lowered pH from 5.8 to 5.5, minimised dispersed clay, and air-filled porosity was lowest on fallow, no-gypsum plots (8.3\% versus 15.1\% after sunflowers) \parencite{GARDNER1991}. Drainage itself altered texture and pore structure in a Russian silt loam, increasing the silt fraction, reducing the sand fraction, and driving opposite seasonal shifts in pore volume \parencite{Brikmans2025}.

\subsection{Perennial Crops, Rotations, and Nutrient/Water Loads in Tile Drainage}

Introducing perennials into annual rotations reduces subsurface nutrient export, with the strongest benefits during high-loading periods. A before--after control--impact (BACI) analysis in Ohio found that alfalfa significantly affected mean monthly subsurface discharge at Site A ($F=4.3$, $p=.04$) but not Site B; alfalfa significantly reduced subsurface NO$_3$-N, TN, and DRP loads at Site A but not Site B, significantly reduced TP at both sites, and reduced the variability of discharge and nutrient loads (smaller post-planting IQR increases in impact fields) \parencite{Arrueta2022}. Seasonally, alfalfa lowered median weekly NO$_3$-N loads in spring and winter at Site A while showing no significant seasonal effect at Site B \parencite{Arrueta2022}. A storm-event analysis at the same sites---grouping events into dry, wet, and large types---found alfalfa had little effect on discharge but significantly lowered subsurface NO$_3$-N and TN across all event types at Site A; during large events, DRP and TP loads were lower in the alfalfa field at \emph{both} sites, identifying large storms as the most important period for phosphorus-export reduction \parencite{Arrueta2025}. Collectively these results support partial, targeted conversion of marginal cropland to perennial cover to cut nutrient loads (especially dissolved and particulate P) and increase system resilience, ideally alongside reduced fertiliser rates and gypsum \parencite{Arrueta2022,Arrueta2025}. Drainage adequacy is itself indexed by perennial performance: on a sloping Cabot silt loam, subsurface drains (especially at 30.5\,m spacing) improved alfalfa growth and survival, percent alfalfa in summer cuttings correlated with plant density ($r=0.93$), percent winterkill correlated with spring ($r=0.79$) and fall ($r=0.84$) soil water, and draining to the 0.1-bar water content rendered winterkill negligible ($\approx$10\%), such that 30.5\,m spacing would ensure $\geq$50\% survival under severe conditions \parencite{BENOIT1967}. At the catchment scale, crop composition modulated phosphorus loss: summer crops occupied 63\% of the agricultural area and winter crops 34\%, summer-rape area correlated positively with summer P loss, winter-wheat area correlated strongly negatively with summer P load, grassland area correlated with winter P load, and no significant correlation linked N load to crop area \parencite{DambenieceMigliniece2018}. Long-term fertilisation and liming further shaped productivity: unlimed soil acidity changed with fertiliser rate (from $+0.3$ pH units unfertilised to $-1.1$ units at the highest rate over 34 years), liming raised pH to about 6.0--6.4, and fertiliser rate was the main control on perennial-grass productivity and sward botanical composition \parencite{Vigovskis2016}.

\subsection{Microbial Communities in Drainage Systems and Denitrifying Bioreactors}

Microbial communities in drained soils, bioreactors, and wetlands are spatially structured, temporally dynamic, and strongly shaped by the contemporary environment. In agricultural soil, the general bacterial community showed significant spatial structure by depth and transect, whereas the denitrifying community did not; directional geostatistics gave bacterial correlation ranges of 5.6\,m overall, 10.4\,m along the flow path, and 10.7\,m at 1.52\,m depth, supporting efficient sampling design (formalised in the \texttt{COMMUNITYCORRELOGRAM} R package) \parencite{Andrus2014}. In denitrifying bioreactors, depth and season shaped bacterial, fungal, and denitrifying communities; communities at 1.52\,m were more temporally variable than at 0.76\,m; bacterial communities cycled with a $\sim$125-day correlation time and roughly annual periodicity; denitrifiers recurred at $\sim$300 days; and fungi were more stable ($\sim$150--200-day autocorrelation, no significant annual cycling), with seasonal flow/moisture (Jan--Jun) and temperature/precipitation (Jul--Dec) gradients evident \parencite{Porter2015}. Comparing habitats, bacterial and clade-I \textit{nosZ} denitrifier communities were distinct among soils, bioreactors, and wetlands (PERMANOVA: significant location and habitat effects), with bioreactor communities more similar to wetlands than to fields---evidence that favourable contemporary environmental conditions can be engineered to develop effective denitrifier assemblages \parencite{Hathaway2015}. In a Canadian agricultural stream network, source locations (tile drains and the drainage ditch) had the highest N and P cycling functional potential and the most abundant nitrification/denitrification genes, storm sampling was associated with elevated nutrient concentrations and functional capacity, archaeal N-cycling capacity and NH$_4$-N rose after harvest, microbial P-mobilisation potential tracked P availability, and water passing through the wetland generally showed reduced nutrient concentrations and functional capacity, though the wetland response was variable \parencite{Knorr2023}.

\subsection{Drainage Water Treatment: Wetlands, Reservoirs, and Reconstructed Oxbows}

Engineered receiving features can substantially attenuate drainage-borne nitrate, though their effectiveness for pathogens is more variable. A reconstructed Iowa floodplain oxbow fed primarily by tile drainage retained nitrate with May--September efficiencies of 44\% (2014) and 47\% (2015) by N:Cl ratio, with retention greatest in late summer/early fall (monthly maxima of 92\% in August and 78\% in September 2015); nitrate concentrations were highest in tile water, similar in upgradient groundwater and the oxbow, and lowest downgradient, and groundwater contributed only a small fraction of discharge---placing oxbow performance on par with bioreactors, wetlands, and saturated buffers as a viable tile-drainage BMP \parencite{Schilling2017}. A Canadian water-and-reservoir-for-irrigation (WRI) constructed wetland reduced mass loads by 67.6\% for nitrate-nitrogen and 63.3\% for \textit{E.\ coli}; outflow nitrate averaged 1.2\,mg\,L$^{-1}$ (below the drinking-water guideline but exceeding the aquatic-life guideline on 25\,d), outflow \textit{E.\ coli} averaged 178\,CFU\,100\,mL$^{-1}$ (exceeding the irrigation guideline on day 119, higher in the warm season), measured residence time was 15.0\,d (design 14.5\,d), and areal uptake rates were 8.0\,m\,y$^{-1}$ (nitrate-N) and 6.4\,m\,y$^{-1}$ (\textit{E.\ coli}); the wetland captured 15{,}600\,m$^{3}$ (14{,}064\,m$^{3}$ after evapotranspiration, $\approx$780\,mm over 1.8\,ha), and a relatively small wetland (5.7\% of drainage area) sufficed \parencite{Haverstock2017}. Not all wetlands attenuate pathogens, however: a New Zealand wetland was consistently a net exporter of \textit{E.\ coli}, with loads increasing by a median 10.2-fold (range 1.5--26$\times$) across ten flow events (median inflow $3.8\times10^{8}$ versus outflow $2.5\times10^{9}$\,MPN/event), and monthly monitoring likewise showed higher outflow than inflow concentrations \parencite{Stott2023}. Selenium-removal wetlands treating drainage water in California retained about 59\% of inflow Se (outflow 35\%, seepage 4\%, volatilisation 2\%), achieving 21--55\% concentration reduction and 48--76\% mass removal in 2000, with the nonvegetated cell least effective---indicating that vegetation-promoted reducing conditions and retention in the organic detrital layer and surface sediment drive removal \parencite{Gao2003}.

\subsection{Trace Elements and Salinity in Agricultural Drainage Water}

\subsubsection{Selenium and the Kesterson legacy}
The Kesterson Reservoir remains the definitive case of trace-element hazard from agricultural drainage. Waterborne selenium at Kesterson averaged 0.122\,mg\,L$^{-1}$---about 230 times the $<$0.001\,mg\,L$^{-1}$ reference at Volta---and mean tissue selenium ran 10--200 times higher, biomagnifying from water to sediment to plants to animals; detritus reached 440\,$\mu$g\,g$^{-1}$ dry weight and some mosquitofish 370\,$\mu$g\,g$^{-1}$, levels judged probably toxic to sensitive consumers and consistent with the observed dead and deformed bird embryos \parencite{SAIKI1987}. \textcite{Ohlendorf2002} documented that 40\% of 347 nests in 1983 held one or more dead embryos and 20\% held severely deformed embryos or chicks; across 1983--1985 at least 39\% of 578 nests contained a dead or deformed embryo; bird-liver selenium was $\sim$10$\times$ and egg selenium 20--30$\times$ the Volta reference; food-chain organisms averaged 20--332\,$\mu$g\,g$^{-1}$ dry weight ($\sim$100$\times$ Volta); inflow selenium averaged 300\,$\mu$g\,L$^{-1}$; and coots showed mortality, reproductive failure, and selenium-toxicosis lesions. These integrated field and laboratory studies fulfilled the equivalent of Koch's postulates, identified selenium as the sole causative agent, and are regarded as a gold standard of retrospective ecological risk assessment \parencite{Ohlendorf2002}. Remediation drew on natural attenuation: groundwater beneath Kesterson was protected by biogeochemical immobilisation (selenium exceeded 10\,$\mu$g\,L$^{-1}$ in only 16 wells in 1985, clustered in two areas, within a $\sim$650\,ha drainage plume fed by $\sim$430\,ha-m\,yr$^{-1}$ of seepage during 1981--1986), and a risk-based strategy left the selenium inventory in place and filled low-lying areas to prevent wildlife-attracting ephemeral pools \parencite{BENSON1993}.

\subsubsection{Arsenic and uranium in evaporation ponds}
In agricultural evaporation ponds, evapoconcentration and redox chemistry jointly control trace-element mobility. Total dissolved arsenic rose from 108--132\,$\mu$g\,L$^{-1}$ at the central inlet to as high as 2{,}099\,$\mu$g\,L$^{-1}$ toward terminal cells; As increased with electrical conductivity yet arsenate As(V) remained dominant ($>$67\% of dissolved As in all cells, $>$95\% at the inlet), while reducing conditions toward terminal cells and in deeper layers lowered dissolved oxygen and nitrate and raised As(III), Fe(II), and sulfide \parencite{Ryu2010}. Sequential extraction showed carbonate-associated As as the largest sediment sink under both oxidising and reducing conditions, with soluble and adsorbed fractions much larger in the reduced terminal cell, so arsenic mobility was controlled by evaporation and by high-DOC reducing conditions \parencite{Ryu2010}. \textcite{Gao2007} corroborated this: pond-water As increased linearly with chloride (behaving more conservatively than selenium), speciation shifted toward As(III) and organic/methylated forms along the flow path and in reducing cells, sediments accumulated As but not strongly enough to prevent rising dissolved concentrations, and DOC increased with chloride and reducing conditions. Uranium showed analogous behaviour: sediment U decreased abruptly with depth (concentrated at the surface), was greatest in the most saline ponds, and correlated with sediment total organic carbon, implying association with algal organic matter; the measured Pond-14 K$_d$ of $\sim$50 far exceeded the isotherm-predicted 2, salt crusts held up to 5.8\,mg\,U\,kg$^{-1}$, and 75\% of surface-sediment U was oxidised versus 25\% reduced \parencite{Duff1997}.

\subsubsection{Boron and micronutrient balances}
Boron transport in agroforestry soils was successfully simulated by a two-dimensional finite-element model (mean absolute error 1.5\,mg\,L$^{-1}$, relative error 6.5\%), which reproduced Mendota field data for 1990 and 1992 and captured the effects of Langmuir constants: higher adsorption equilibrium constant, maximum adsorptive capacity, or root-absorption coefficient lowered soil-solution boron, while higher irrigation-water boron raised it, and adsorption was greater in silt loam than in sandy or clay loam \parencite{Tayfur2010}. Boron isotopes offer a manure/fertiliser tracer: hog manure held 0.4--8.2\,mg\,L$^{-1}$ B (mean 2.9) with $\delta^{11}$B of 7.2--11.2\textperthousand, cattle-feedlot runoff averaged 23.2\textperthousand, and Cobb River subsurface drainage averaged 0.06\,mg\,L$^{-1}$ B with $\delta^{11}$B of 5.3--15.1\textperthousand; the comparatively narrow hog-manure range makes $\delta^{11}$B useful for tracing feedlot contamination and mixing between water sources \parencite{Komor1997}. Micronutrient budgets can run negative under intensive drainage: in Pakistan, drainage-water removal of Cu, Fe, Mn, and Zn exceeded inputs from irrigation and fertiliser at both sites, giving negative balances and net soil depletion; Cu and Mn losses exceeded Fe and Zn (Zn least), losses were generally greater in maize (Kharif) than wheat (Rabi), and extractable concentrations rose with depth \parencite{Gul2013}.

\subsubsection{Major ions and salinity}
The ionic composition of central-Iowa drainage water was dominated (in decreasing order) by bicarbonate, calcium, magnesium, nitrate, chloride, sodium, and sulfate \parencite{Zimmerman2017b}. Bicarbonate, calcium, and magnesium were higher post-growing season while nitrate and chloride were higher in-season; corn-based systems had greater magnesium and chloride and lower calcium than soybean; bicarbonate correlated positively with calcium and negatively with nitrate and chloride; and flow, pH, and temperature did not differ among cropping systems, so they did not explain the ionic differences \parencite{Zimmerman2017b}. Salinity itself drove nonlinear soil responses in a North Dakota case study, where NO$_3$-N increased nonlinearly and Olsen-P linearly with salinity, while POXC, protein, and arbuscular-mycorrhizal fungi decreased \parencite{Daigh2025}; and subsurface drainage proved a stronger salinity control than biochar across the 0--80\,cm profile in coastal Chinese soils \parencite{Liu2025f}.

\subsection{Ecological Responses to Subsurface Drainage}

\subsubsection{Soil fauna}
Subsurface drainage patterns measurably shape earthworm populations. \textit{Lumbricus terrestris} abundance was typically higher above drains than between them---above the pipes the median individual count was twice as high and total fresh mass five times as high, with mean differences of 2.5 individuals\,m$^{-2}$ (95\% CI 1.0--4.0) and 6.6\,g\,m$^{-2}$ (95\% CI 3.6--9.6)---and stained burrows reached the 1\,m drain depth and below, so drainage pattern is a factor affecting population density and should inform sampling design (with worm activity potentially affecting subdrain function) \parencite{Nuutinen2001}. A long-term inoculation study confirmed both the above-drain preference (significantly higher densities above tile lines under no-till and ploughing) and strong management and dispersal gradients: by 2009 density reached 41 individuals\,m$^{-2}$ near margins versus 13 inside constantly unploughed plots, plot margins exceeded no-till which exceeded ploughing, density and fresh weight declined with distance from the inoculation area (14 to 1 individuals\,m$^{-2}$ from 5--9\,m to 56--60\,m), and an indicative colonisation rate of 4.6\,m\,yr$^{-1}$ was estimated---underscoring that undisturbed margin habitat and long-term ($>$10-year) monitoring are decisive for evaluating inoculation success \parencite{Nuutinen2011}.

\subsubsection{Birds, amphibians, and semi-aquatic species}
Drainage type is associated with farmland-bird outcomes. In this first long-term, large-scale Finnish comparison, skylark populations increased significantly on open-drained fields (ODF) but were stable on subsurface-drained fields (SDF); meadow pipit increased significantly on ODF (its SDF decline non-significant) and bred in significantly greater numbers on ODF; both species favoured ODF over SDF, with abundance not differing statistically between drainage types overall but territories fewer on arable than grassland fields \parencite{Marja2013}. Tile-drainage-associated pesticides posed hazards to amphibians: tile wetlands had cumulative pesticide concentrations 4.7 times higher than reference wetlands (neonicotinoids dominant), with clothianidin $\sim$8$\times$ and thiamethoxam nearly 9$\times$ higher (thiamethoxam detected at all tile wetlands and no reference wetlands); juvenile frogs from tile wetlands carried $\sim$2.5$\times$ more imidacloprid in brain tissue and nearly 2$\times$ more desnitro-imidacloprid in liver, and reference-wetland frogs had slightly longer standardised cerebellum lengths---the first field quantification of imidacloprid in juvenile amphibian brains and evidence that these chemicals can alter brain structure \parencite{Campbell2023}. Subsurface drainage of meadows was also identified, alongside ditch cleaning and gyro-crushing, as a leading cause of decline in the semi-aquatic snails \textit{Galba truncatula} and \textit{Omphiscola glabra}, whose occupied habitats fell by 30\% and 38\% respectively by 2016--2017, with the largest losses in vernal pools and significant reductions in drainage-furrow habitat area \parencite{Dreyfuss2018}.

\subsubsection{Vegetation and restored aquatic habitats}
Over 24 years, drained UK grassland shifted from a \textit{Lolium perenne}-dominated community with \textit{Juncus} patches toward one of \textit{Lolium perenne} and \textit{Trifolium repens}; plant diversity depended on topography and spatial effects rather than drainage, total N and C generally decreased with drainage and depth (but only interactively), the C/N ratio was drainage-independent, and $\delta^{13}$C depended significantly on drainage (depleted in drained plots) whereas $\delta^{15}$N did not \parencite{Harris2018}. In restored Iowa oxbows, tile drainage had negligible effects: physical variables did not differ significantly between multipurpose (tiled) and non-tiled oxbows, no biotic variable differed, and macrophyte, macroinvertebrate, and fish assemblages were similar (44 invertebrate taxa, 35 fish species, and 2{,}682 Topeka shiners recorded), providing preliminary but promising evidence that multipurpose oxbows can enhance biodiversity without ecological penalty \parencite{Leberg2024}. Wetland restoration outcomes improved with sediment excavation, which initially reduced invasive cover (notably hybrid cattail and reed canary grass) and increased diversity and evenness, though the invasive-suppression benefit was lost by $\sim$6 years; community composition differed significantly by wetland age, with younger and excavated wetlands favouring native perennials and submerged macrophytes \parencite{Winikoff2020}.

\subsection{Amendments, Cover Management, and Nutrient Retention: Additional Practices}

Two further practice classes complete the picture. Polyacrylamide (PAM) reduced downward phosphorus transport in soil columns, with the 0.20\% treatment optimal (cumulative soluble-P loss 348 versus 541\,mg\,tube$^{-1}$; mean soluble-P concentration 87 versus 138\,$\mu$g\,ml$^{-1}$ in the control); in field plots all PAM treatments significantly cut runoff volume and total-P loss and significantly reduced leachate volume to tile drains, though total-P losses in leachate were not significantly different (attributed to low surface-application rate and high variability); PAM alone maximised wheat growth and plant-P uptake, whereas PAM plus calcium amendments best reduced runoff-P losses \parencite{Jiang2010}. In Spanish citrus, combining anti-weed mesh with a drainage system improved vegetative growth, flower production, and yield relative to the traditional control: netted treatments generally produced larger canopies (the largest canopy volume 2256.17\,L and trunk diameter 40.38\,mm under one treatment) and higher juice yield, while the uncovered control produced the heaviest individual fruits; metabolomic profiling showed a shift from 27 to 25 detected metabolites between seasons (proline the most abundant amino acid, quinic acid the most abundant organic acid), with certain netted-and-drained treatments best maintaining metabolomic homeostasis, and the authors recommend anti-weed mesh for commercial plantations in water-scarce regions for its reduced water, herbicide, and labour costs \parencite{GiménezValero2024,GiménezValero2025}.

### Material: Herbicide Loss Timing and Concentrations in Drainage

\subsection{Overview}
The timing of runoff and drainage events relative to herbicide application is the single most powerful control on peak concentrations and mass losses observed in both surface runoff and subsurface (tile) drainage. Synthesising more than thirty North American field studies, \textcite{Kladivko2001} established the consolidated picture that frames this theme: pesticides are commonly detected in subsurface drainflow during the first drainage event(s) after application, concentrations usually decline rapidly during that event or in successive events, and most mass losses are very small---generally less than 0.5\% and often less than 0.1\% of applied. Because subsurface drainage lowers runoff and sediment losses, pesticide concentrations and mass losses in tile drainage are usually much lower than in surface runoff, frequently by an order of magnitude \parencite{Kladivko2001}. The evidence base spans plot- and field-scale monitoring, catchment-scale mass balances, controlled tracer experiments, and numerous simulation models, with strong convergence on several mechanisms: preferential (macropore) flow delivers a fraction of applied compound to drains almost immediately after application; early events dominate the seasonal loss; and year-to-year weather variability governs whether substantial losses occur at all \parencite{Kladivko2001}. Against this well-supported backdrop, one methodological gap is explicit in the literature: there are no reports quantifying how flux-averaged pesticide concentrations in drainage water differ from concentrations between the drains, so evidence-based conclusions on that specific comparison cannot yet be drawn \parencite{Jacobsen2007}.

\subsection{Timing of loss relative to application and drainage onset}

\subsubsection{First drainage events and rapid concentration decline}
The recurring observation across sites is that runoff events shortly after application produce the greatest herbicide concentrations and losses in both surface runoff and subsurface drainage \parencite{GAYNOR1995}. In Louisiana, atrazine and metolachlor concentrations in runoff declined rapidly during the first 34 days after application and then remained consistently low (below 10~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$) from day 54 to season end; early runoff events contributed disproportionately to seasonal loss, and most atrazine and metolachlor loss occurred within the first 61 days after application \parencite{SOUTHWICK1990}. In Illinois tile systems, most atrazine loss occurred within three months of application, and higher application rates increased subsurface losses \parencite{Kalita2006}. Catchment-scale work reinforces this: more than 80\% of total herbicide losses from a whole catchment occurred during just two rain events, 23 and 28 days after a controlled application \parencite{Leu2004}. In France, applying pesticides after the start of drainage increased the exported ratio for all compounds studied, and annual exported flux was highly correlated with maximum drainwater concentration for all four herbicides \parencite{Julien2023}.

Late-season losses are comparatively minor but occur primarily through tile discharge: in Ontario, up to 7\% of applied atrazine and 5\% of applied metolachlor was lost when runoff occurred within a week of application, whereas late-season losses were only 0.1 to 2.0\% and moved mainly via drains \parencite{GAYNOR1995}. Tracer experiments confirm near-instantaneous arrival: bromide and both herbicides produced breakthrough curves typical of preferential flow, with all chemicals arriving at tile drains at the same time, and even the first drainage after application contained all three compounds \parencite{Fortin2002}. Similarly, herbicides first arrived at drains after less than 1~cm of net drainage, interpreted as rapid transport through about 3\% of the pore volume \parencite{Dousset2004}.

\subsubsection{Seasonal partitioning, snowmelt, and carry-over timing}
Timing is not confined to the days after application. In a boreal Finnish study, none of the herbicides were detected in subsurface drainage during the first 200 days; ethofumesate and glufosinate-ammonium were detected only in April, and most herbicide loss occurred during snowmelt and soil thaw in spring rather than immediately after application \parencite{Siimes2006}. In structured French soils, metolachlor losses followed two seasonal phases: high concentrations in relatively small spring/summer drainage volumes, and large autumn/winter drainage volumes with low concentrations \parencite{Novak2001}. In the Vertic Cambisol, 91\% of metolachlor losses occurred within three months of treatment in 1996/97, compared with 32\% on the Stagnic Luvisol \parencite{Novak2001}. Comparable two-phase behaviour appeared elsewhere: preferential flow during spring--summer with fast herbicide migration, and matrix flow during fall--winter with slower migration \parencite{Dousset2004}. In the Paris basin, triazine herbicides stayed in the first 40~cm of soil during dry periods and moved downward mainly during the first water infiltrations when drains resumed working in autumn and winter \parencite{Garmouma1998}. Herbicide detections can also carry over between seasons: residual dinoseb appeared in tile outflow in spring and again in fall at a site where none was applied that year, with carry-over confirmed for up to 23 months at one site while another showed no measurable residue after 42 months \parencite{ONEILL1989}. Field studies leaving pesticide selection and application to the farmer, with rainfall dictating drainage, provide realistic timing information of this kind \parencite{ONEILL1989}.

\subsection{Magnitude of mass losses in drainage versus surface runoff}
Quantitatively, mass losses to tile drainage are consistently small. \textcite{Kladivko2001} report that losses are usually below 0.5\% and often below 0.1\% of applied, the main exception being a Woodslee, Ontario site with herbicide losses as high as 3\%. Site-specific values corroborate this range. In Illinois, long-term average atrazine losses were 1.13, 1.68, 1.48, and 0.93~g~ha$^{-1}$ at four sites, corresponding to only 0.14\%, 0.15\%, 0.22\%, and 0.11\% of application \parencite{Kalita2006}. In New York plot studies, mass loss to drainage was much smaller for herbicides than for chloride: chloride loss was 1.3\% of applied, whereas 2,4-D loss was 0.08\% and atrazine loss was 0.02\% \parencite{Shalit1996}. In Minnesota tile water, mean atrazine concentrations ranged from about 0.29 to 1.24~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ across 1986--1991 with estimated annual losses of only 0.4 to 4.1~g~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{BUHLER1993}. Tracer comparisons showed less than 1\% of applied herbicide recovered in tile drains over two years, even though 3.2 to 28.2\% of applied bromide was recovered \parencite{Fortin2002}. In Finland, ethofumesate, glufosinate-ammonium, and glyphosate losses to runoff over 302 days were 1.0\%, 0.2\%, and 0.1\% of applied \parencite{Siimes2006}. Annual herbicide losses in Ontario ranged from below 0.3 to 2.7\% of application \parencite{Gaynor2001}.

The magnitude contrast between drainage and surface runoff is a robust finding. Because subsurface drainage lowers runoff and sediment losses, pesticide concentrations and mass losses are usually much lower in tile drainage than in surface runoff, often by an order of magnitude \parencite{Kladivko2001}. In Louisiana, seasonal runoff losses of both herbicides were less than half from plots with subsurface drains than from undrained plots, mainly because of lower runoff volume with a smaller contribution from lower first-event concentrations \parencite{SOUTHWICK1990}; for the first runoff event, concentrations were 65\% greater for atrazine and 60\% greater for metolachlor from undrained plots \parencite{SOUTHWICK1990}. Early irrigated-field work reached the same conclusion: tailwater contained much higher residue concentrations than tile drainage effluent (ratios up to about 68:1 during one flooding period), and the total residue in tile effluent did not exceed the quantity applied in the irrigation water alone \parencite{JOHNSTON1967}. An open drain collecting both surface and subsurface waters carried residues about ten times those in tile effluent \parencite{JOHNSTON1967}.

\subsection{Preferential and macropore flow as the dominant transport mechanism}
The mechanism reconciling near-immediate arrival with small overall losses is preferential (macropore) flow. \textcite{Kladivko2001} conclude that preferential flow is the most likely mechanism for pesticide movement to tile drains soon after application. Tracer experiments make this explicit: breakthrough curves for bromide and herbicides were typical of preferential flow, atrazine and metolachlor concentration peaks occurred earlier than bromide peaks, and 50\% of herbicide relative cumulative mass was reached after less than 50~mm cumulative drainage, whereas bromide required a much larger share of cumulative drainage \parencite{Fortin2002}. Rapid arrival after less than 1~cm of net drainage indicated transport through only about 3\% of the pore volume \parencite{Dousset2004}, and more residues were detected from the silty clay soil than the silt loam, attributed to preferential flow and more intensive drainage \parencite{Dousset2004}.

Soil structure, rather than sorption or degradation, dominated the difference between soils in several studies. Total metolachlor losses were greater in the clayey Vertic Cambisol than in the loamy Stagnic Luvisol, with peak drainwater concentrations consistently higher in the Vertic Cambisol; the authors conclude that differences were much more influenced by soil structure and macropore flow than by adsorption or degradation \parencite{Novak2001}. Modelling supported this: macroporosity had a much greater effect on bromide and metolachlor losses in the Vertic Cambisol, where losses were 2.1 and 1.3 times greater when macroporosity was set at 10\% than when not simulated, and macroporosity and sorption kinetics were identified as the main factors controlling metolachlor transport \parencite{Novak2003}. In Iowa, atrazine concentrations were higher in one plot than others, suggesting preferential movement, with deethylatrazine-to-atrazine ratios indicating shorter residence time and minimal microbial contact where the greatest concentrations occurred \parencite{JAYACHANDRAN1994}. At catchment scale, the largest spatial differences in herbicide losses were linked mainly to fast transport mechanisms---surface runoff and preferential flow to tile drains---rather than to compound properties alone, with topography, soil permeability, subsoil texture, and the location of drainage systems identified as key controls \parencite{Leu2004}. Where nonequilibrium transport prevailed, the equilibrium advection-dispersion equation failed to reproduce drainwater concentrations, whereas a mobile--immobile (MIM) nonequilibrium model gave much better agreement across three sites, and a silty clay soil required modified hydraulic functions to represent preferential flow through drying cracks \parencite{Boivin2006}.

\subsection{Concentrations, peaks, and exceedance of water-quality thresholds}
Although mass losses are small, transient concentrations can be high and can exceed regulatory thresholds. In Ontario, maximum concentrations reached 763 and 721~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ for atrazine and metolachlor in surface runoff and 287 and 210~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ in tile discharge; the USEPA atrazine maximum contaminant level (MCL) was exceeded in 74\% of surface runoff samples and 60\% of tile drainage samples from conventional tillage, and metolachlor exceeded its health advisory level in 4 to 14\% of samples \parencite{GAYNOR1995}. In Illinois, long-term flow-weighted atrazine concentrations were below 3~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ for most events but occasionally reached about 25~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ at three sites and about 90~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ at a fourth \parencite{Kalita2006}. In Iowa, atrazine concentration exceeded the drinking-water MCL in more than 40\% of samples, and total atrazine residue (atrazine plus degradates) could exceed the MCL even when atrazine alone was below 3~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ \parencite{JAYACHANDRAN1994}. Glyphosate work in a multi-basin study recorded the highest measured glyphosate concentration (430~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$) at an overland-flow site, while an Iowa subsurface drain had higher median concentrations than surface-water sites and maxima above 250~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ \parencite{Coupe2012}. Persistent low-level occurrence is also documented: atrazine was detected in 97\% of Minnesota tile samples across 1985--1990 and 78\% in 1991, versus alachlor in only a handful of samples \parencite{BUHLER1993}. Toxic thresholds were exceeded episodically for other compounds, with dinoseb in tile outflow exceeding the maximum acceptable toxic concentration of 0.5~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ on nineteen occasions \parencite{ONEILL1989}.

\subsection{Degradates, metabolites, and residual persistence}
Herbicide degradates contribute meaningfully to the loss signal and can be more mobile than parent compounds. Deethylatrazine was found in both surface runoff and tile drainage and accounted for 10 to 27\% of combined atrazine and deethylatrazine loss \parencite{GAYNOR1995}, and desethyl atrazine comprised 7 to 39\% of total triazine loss in a related study \parencite{Gaynor2001}. N-de-ethylated atrazine and cyanazine II moved through the soil profile into subsurface drainage in greater proportions than their parents, and N-de-ethylated atrazine persisted at 0.015--0.02~ppm twelve months after application \parencite{MUIR1978}. In Iowa, atrazine, deisopropylatrazine (DIA), and deethylatrazine (DEA) were consistently detected in tile drainage and shallow groundwater, with DEA generally greater than DIA \parencite{JAYACHANDRAN1994}. Parent persistence varies widely: atrazine dissipation in surface soil was biphasic, with rapid loss over the first 60 days followed by very slow dissipation, and detection frequency in the surface 7.5~cm exceeded 95\% over 1991--1995, whereas metribuzin dissipated much faster (first-order half-lives of 15 and 21 days) and was rarely detected after one season \parencite{Moorman1999}. After atrazine use stopped in Minnesota, tile concentrations did not decline during the first year, then fell by more than 50\% in the second and continued to decline while remaining detectable, with residual atrazine persisting in soil 12 and 26 months after last application \parencite{BUHLER1993}. Metabolite fate can also diverge from parents under management change: predicted losses of the metolachlor degradate OXA decreased with residue removal, in contrast to increased parent-herbicide transport \parencite{Shipitalo2016}. Solubility differences drive contrasting mobility, as when lindane---about 100 times more soluble than DDT---leached readily and appeared at higher concentrations in drainage effluent \parencite{JOHNSTON1967}.

\subsection{Representativeness of flux-averaged drainage concentrations}
A distinct and less-developed line of evidence concerns whether drainage-water concentrations represent conditions in the field. \textcite{Jacobsen2007} emphasise that no literature reports quantify how the flux-averaged concentration of pesticides in drainage water differs from that found between the drains, so evidence-based conclusions on that comparison cannot be drawn. The existing literature suggests that concentration in drainage water does not always correspond to the concentration at drain depth between drains, and depending on conditions may be higher or lower \parencite{Jacobsen2007}. Representativeness is most questionable on very well-drained soils and on poorly drained soils with little capacity for lateral transport beneath the plough layer, whereas relatively porous soils and moderate climatic conditions favour representative concentrations; drainage water is also more representative for weakly sorbed than for strongly sorbed pesticides \parencite{Jacobsen2007}. Despite these caveats, drainage monitoring offers the practical advantages that samples integrate spatial variability across the whole field and that existing drains can be used without disturbing the soil or installing new equipment \parencite{Jacobsen2007}.

\subsection{Controls on variability: soils, tillage, drainage design, and weather}
The literature identifies several interacting controls on the magnitude and partitioning of loss. Weather and hydrology are paramount: year and source-by-treatment effects were more important than tillage alone, with no tillage effect detected for total atrazine loss and only one instance of metolachlor loss reduced 20\% by conservation tillage \parencite{GAYNOR1995}; year-to-year weather variation strongly controls whether and how much loss occurs \parencite{Kladivko2001}. Losses can be higher in drier years because wetter years lengthen the interval between treatment and the first drainage event \parencite{Dousset2004}.

Tillage effects on partitioning are documented but modest for total loss. Conservation tillage increased surface runoff 42\% and decreased tile discharge 15\% relative to conventional tillage, yet total field runoff was unchanged; averaged over years, herbicide concentration was 1.3 and 1.8 times greater from conservation than conventional tillage in surface runoff and tile discharge, respectively \parencite{GAYNOR1995}. In Minnesota, tillage system had minimal impact on atrazine concentration or loss in tile water, with differences between moldboard plow and no-till generally small and often not significant \parencite{BUHLER1993}. In a tracer study, mean maximum bromide concentration was significantly higher under reduced than conventional tillage (19.9 versus 10.7~mg~L$^{-1}$) in one year \parencite{Fortin2002}. Drainage design and water-table management also matter: herbicide concentrations in runoff from drained plots showed no trend with drain spacing \parencite{SOUTHWICK1990}, and drain lines intercepted a reasonable amount of dissolved pesticide and reduced concentrations below the drainage depth, with predicted concentrations lower under 30 and 60~cm water tables than under a 90~cm water table \parencite{Kalita1998}. Controlled drainage-subirrigation increased herbicide loss in surface runoff but decreased loss through tile drainage, so total loss did not differ between drainage treatments \parencite{Gaynor2001}.

\subsection{Management and remediation of herbicide loss}
Several management interventions with quantified effects emerge from the evidence. Reducing surface transport is effective: soil-saver tillage combined with a ryegrass intercrop reduced herbicide loss in tile drains and surface runoff by 46 to 49\% relative to moldboard plow in one year and had the potential to decrease total loss by at least 49\% by reducing surface transport, while band application reduced loss irrespective of environmental factors \parencite{Gaynor2001}. Practices that decrease atrazine use, such as banding or rotations, and that decrease runoff and increase atrazine retention in soil, have the potential to improve water quality and reduce subsurface losses \parencite{Moorman1999}. Application timing offers a direct lever: a soil wetness index (SWI) threshold of 85\% separated low- and higher-risk application periods, with exported ratios below 0.2\% below the threshold but reaching up to 3.23\% above it; the SWI-based restriction reduced risk metrics, was reported as more efficient than other restriction factors, and can be calculated easily from local weather and soil data \parencite{Julien2023}.

By contrast, corn stover harvest can worsen drainage quality: a 50\% residue-removal scenario, combined with reduced surface crust conductivity and macroporosity, increased atrazine and metolachlor transport to tile drains \parencite{Shipitalo2016}. Edge-of-field and end-of-pipe treatments show promise. Denitrifying woodchip bioreactors removed both nitrate and atrazine, with removal increasing with hydraulic residence time; biochar-amended bioreactors removed 93\% of atrazine at 72~h versus 55\% for woodchip-only, and atrazine removal was primarily abiotic since aeration and influent nitrate did not affect effluent atrazine \parencite{Hassanpour2019}. Wood-chip bioreactors also rapidly dissipated atrazine, enrofloxacin, and sulfamethazine, mainly by sorption, without inhibiting denitrification by atrazine \parencite{Ilhan2011}. Constructed remediation zones exploit sorption and degradation: retention capacity ranked straw $\gg$ sediments $>$ soils, with straw showing the highest adsorption coefficients and the largest non-desorbable residues \parencite{Vallée2014}, while total pesticide dissipation over 120 days ranged from 1.4\% to 100\% and constructed wetlands were shown to alternate retention during drainage and degradation during non-drainage periods \parencite{Vallée2016}. Purpose-made adsorbents are also effective: canola-stalk activated carbon achieved a maximum 2,4-D uptake of 135.8~mg~g$^{-1}$, removed 100\% of the herbicide from real agricultural drainage water, and retained 83.79\% efficiency after five regeneration cycles \parencite{Amiri2020}.

\subsection{Modelling herbicide transport in drained fields}
A substantial modelling literature supports and extends the field observations. Simplified event-based models can reproduce drainage and pesticide dynamics: PESTDRAIN simulated both surface runoff and tile-drainage flow with associated concentrations, achieving relative errors of 14\% for drainage discharge and 4\% for surface runoff and reproducing high-resolution IPU concentrations, with an exponential transfer function separating fast and slow transport \parencite{Branger2009}. Process models emphasise sorption kinetics and macroporosity: adsorption-desorption kinetics (ADK) reproduced observed metolachlor export patterns better than instantaneous reversible adsorption, and export was more sensitive to ADK rates than to degradation \parencite{Novak2003}. HYDRUS-2D simulated drain discharge well for homogeneous soils but required modified hydraulic functions and a mobile--immobile transport formulation for a cracking silty clay, with the solute mass-transfer coefficient critical for matching bentazone transport \parencite{Boivin2006}. RZWQM accurately simulated metolachlor transport and reasonably matched drainage volume, giving confidence for scenario prediction of residue-removal effects \parencite{Shipitalo2016}. At catchment scale, SWAT+ simulated discharge very well (calibration NSE 0.86, validation NSE 0.74), with performance improving with pesticide mobility; subsurface drainage was an important pathway (on average 15.7\% tile flow, rising to 21.2\% in a wet winter), and tile flow became increasingly important after application for moderately and very mobile compounds and their transformation products, whereas surface runoff dominated peak losses for non-mobile pesticides \parencite{Wendell2024}.

Rainfall resolution and process representation shape predictions. Rainfall intensity and duration affected transport in runoff more than in subsurface drainage, and hourly rainfall mattered most when rain fell shortly after application; however, for long-term mean and upper-percentile subsurface losses, temporally discrete rainfall data gave limited benefit, with less than 2\% difference in percent loss and hourly or average-duration events needed to stay within about 7\% of predicted loss \parencite{Fox2007}. Mixing-layer models successfully described drainage outflow, especially for incorporated treatments where mixing was more complete, and predicted plausible partition coefficients (1.44~cm$^3$~g$^{-1}$ for 2,4-D and 12.40~cm$^3$~g$^{-1}$ for atrazine) and a mixing-layer depth matching the plow zone \parencite{Shalit1996}. The same framework performed especially well for the preferential-flow portion of outflow and offered a way to distinguish preferential and matrix components; the ratio of outflow to mixing-layer depth can estimate the fraction of matrix participating in fast macropore flow \parencite{SHALIT1995,Shalit1996}. The ADAPT model reproduced observed atrazine and alachlor groundwater concentrations reasonably, with predicted concentrations decreasing with soil depth and with shallower water tables, demonstrating the water-quality benefit of water-table management \parencite{Kalita1998}.

\subsection{Related contaminants: biosolids-borne compounds and emerging chemicals}
Beyond conventional herbicides, tile drainage transports a broader suite of contaminants whose behaviour mirrors the timing and preferential-flow patterns above. After dewatered municipal biosolids application, selected PPCPs were detected only transiently in tile drainage and shallow groundwater, incorporated aggregates retained compounds up to one year with generally exponential decay, and the high-rate application did not significantly impact tile drainage or groundwater quality \parencite{Gottschall2012}. Hormones were detected only briefly in tile drainage at low ng~L$^{-1}$ levels while sterols such as coprostanol persisted and served as useful fecal-contamination indicators strongly related to fecal bacteria in tile water \parencite{Gottschall2013}. PBDEs and perfluoroalkyl acids were detected in tile drainage and groundwater both before and after application, with statistically significant post-application increases for several BDE congeners and substantial in-aggregate dissipation (many congeners reduced by more than 90\% within a year; half-lives about 30 to 223 days) \parencite{Gottschall2017}. A cross-study review found that surface runoff and tile-drainage concentrations of microcontaminants varied widely and were generally lower than in secondary municipal effluent, with the highest tile-drainage concentrations (above 1000~ng~L$^{-1}$) for ibuprofen, naproxen, triclosan, gemfibrozil, and carbamazepine, and with liquid biosolids moving more rapidly and producing higher, earlier tile-drain concentrations than dewatered biosolids \parencite{Ghirardini2019}. Seed-treatment neonicotinoids showed clothianidin in 88\% of tile-drain and 95\% of ditch samples, generally higher in ditch than tile water especially in the first five weeks after planting, with an approximately 40- to 50-fold dilution reported between ditch and stream water \parencite{Schaafsma2019}. Finally, at regional scale, glyphosate use in the western Lake Erie basin was significantly correlated with rising annual dissolved reactive phosphorus (DRP) loads---which increased from 101 to 566~Mg~P~y$^{-1}$ between 1994 and 2020---and with flow-weighted mean DRP concentrations, though not with total phosphorus loads \parencite{Spiese2023}.
