### Material: Controlled Drainage Effects on Flow and Losses

\subsection{Overview}

A large and internally consistent body of evidence establishes that the way a
subsurface drainage system is designed and operated governs both the volume of
water it discharges and the partitioning of that water among competing
pathways---drain flow, surface runoff, evapotranspiration, and deep
seepage---and that these hydrologic shifts in turn control the losses of
nitrogen, phosphorus, sediment, and salts to receiving waters. Three design
levers recur throughout the literature: drain spacing, drain depth, and outlet
(water-table) control. Controlled drainage (CD), also termed drainage water
management (DWM), raises the outlet elevation to hold a higher water table
during non-cropping or low-demand periods, thereby storing additional water in
the field and reducing cumulative drain outflow \parencite{Singh2020,Strock2011,Blann2009}.
The dominant, well-supported mechanism is a coupling between flow and load:
because most nitrate leaves drained fields dissolved in drain water, reductions
in nitrate-N loss under controlled or shallower drainage are of roughly the same
magnitude as the reductions in outflow, with concentration changes secondary
\parencite{Wesström2007,Frankenberger2024,Lalonde1996,Tolomio2018}.

The headline magnitudes are quantitatively convergent across meta-analyses and
syntheses. Across 15 peer-reviewed studies, conventional drainage volumes
exceeded controlled drainage volumes by an average of 40\%, and controlled
drainage reduced nitrogen loss by 40--50\% and phosphorus loss to surface water
by 25--35\% \parencite{Strock2011}. A meta-analysis of 61 studies (1050 data
pairs) found that controlled drainage decreased drainage volume by 19.2\%,
reduced NO$_3$-N concentration and load, and produced a slight overall crop
yield increase of 0.11\%, framing it as a ``win--win'' practice
\parencite{Wang2020c}. A synthesis of drainage water management drawing on 290
plot-years and 31 sites reported a mean NO$_3$-N load reduction of
13.3~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ (46\% removal efficiency) and a mean flow reduction
of 126~mm~yr$^{-1}$, with dissolved reactive and total phosphorus loads reduced
by 34\% and 36\%, respectively, and with the paired concentration reduction not
significant---confirming that load reduction arises mainly from reduced flow
\parencite{Frankenberger2024}. The MANAGE Drain Load database, compiling 91
publications and 1279 site-years spanning 1961--2012, further anchors the
baseline: the mean and median fractions of precipitation appearing as drainage
were 25\% and 20\%, and wet years produced significantly greater discharge and
nutrient loads than dry years \parencite{Christianson2015}. The evidence base
spans multi-year paired-field experiments, controlled tank and lysimeter
studies, and simulation with DRAINMOD, HYDRUS, SWAP, FLUSH, and SWAT-family
models, giving both process understanding and quantified outcomes across humid,
arid, and irrigated settings.

\subsection{Controlled Drainage and the Reduction of Drain Outflow}

The most robust and frequently replicated finding is that controlled drainage
reduces cumulative and annual subsurface drain discharge relative to free
(conventional) drainage. Field experiments report reductions that, while
site-specific, are consistently large. In Sweden, controlled drainage produced
much lower total drain outflow in both measured years, with the largest
reduction in the second year, and lowered nitrate and phosphorus losses in
proportion to the reduced outflow \parencite{Wesström2001,Wesström2007}. In
Quebec, controlled water-table treatments at 0.25 and 0.50~m above the drain
reduced total drain flow by 58.7\% and 65.3\% in 1992 and by 40.9\% and 95\% in
1993, with nitrate-loss reductions of comparable magnitude (up to 95.7\%), the
net benefit attributed primarily to decreased drain flow
\parencite{Lalonde1996}. Iowa comparisons found controlled and shallow drainage
reduced subsurface drainage volume by 37\% and 46\% (four-year averages of 23
and 20~cm versus 37~cm for conventional), and over a five-year study controlled
and shallow drainage cut annual subsurface flow by 60\% and 58\%
\parencite{Helmers2012,Schott2017}. Ohio field data showed 16--24\% reductions
in annual discharge and a statistically significant drop in mean monthly
discharge from 4.3 to 3.1~cm \parencite{Pease2017}, and across six northwest
Ohio sites the mean reduction in daily managed-zone drainage volume ranged from
40\% to 100\% (with no discharge at one site), the linear mixed models
explaining 57--92\% of the variance \parencite{Gunn2015}. Indiana paired-field
work found the strongest reductions in the dormant season (up to 100\%), with
annual nitrate-load reductions of 15--31\% \parencite{Adeuya2012}, and event
analysis showed controlled drainage reduced event drainage volume and peak flow
and increased time to peak, with antecedent conditions rather than precipitation
characteristics controlling controlled-quadrant response \parencite{Lahdou2019}.

Similar reductions recur worldwide. Italian water-table management reduced total
water discharge by 81\% \parencite{Tolomio2018}; Egyptian and Iranian studies
reported significantly reduced outflow under controlled drainage in semiarid
conditions \parencite{Wahba2001,Jouni2018}; and Indian saline Vertisols showed
controlled drainage cutting drainage water outflow by about 64\% (11.0 versus
30.2~cm) and average drain discharge from 3.10 to 1.12~mm~day$^{-1}$
\parencite{Karegoudar2019}. Michigan simulations projected controlled drainage
reducing annual discharge by 59--72\% depending on management aggressiveness and
period \parencite{BinShokrana2023}, and a Michigan paired-field saturated-buffer
study attributed most performance to the controlled-drainage component, which
reduced annual discharge by 47.1\% and nitrate-N load by 48.0\%
\parencite{Ghane2025}. In cultivated peat soils in Finland, controlled drainage
reduced drain discharge by 862~mm (thin peat) and 457~mm (thick peat), a strong
discharge response accompanied by only a modest groundwater-level rise
\parencite{Tähtikarhu2025}; other Finnish work reported 11--23\% reductions in
mean annual drain discharge and 17--36~cm higher water tables under controlled
drainage \parencite{Salla2022}. Dutch reviews reported reductions of 14\% in a
lysimeter and 16--85\% across clay, silty clay, and sandy loam soils, together
with increased water retention \parencite{deWit2022}. Tank experiments confirmed
the mechanism under strictly controlled boundaries: controlled drainage at 10
and 20~cm reduced cumulative drainage volume by 8\% and 6\% and extended
drainage duration by 17~h \parencite{Zheng2025}. Collectively, these results
establish outflow reduction, extended drainage duration, lower and later peak
flows, and increased in-field storage as consolidated, transferable effects of
outlet control.

\subsection{Nitrate and Phosphorus Loss Reductions and the Flow--Load Coupling}

Because nitrate is highly mobile and travels dissolved in drain water, the
reduction in nitrate-N load under controlled drainage tracks the reduction in
outflow closely. Swedish studies made this explicit: the relative decrease in N
and P loading in drain outflow was of the same magnitude as the reduction in
outflow rate, with high-risk N-loss periods coinciding with high outflow rates
and high soil mineral N \parencite{Wesström2007}. Global syntheses reinforce
that nutrient reduction is due mainly to reduced flow rather than consistent
concentration reductions \parencite{Frankenberger2024,Wang2020c}. Reported
load reductions are large and consistent: 61\% (controlled) and 49\% (shallow)
in Iowa \parencite{Schott2017}; 36\% (controlled) and 29\% (shallow) in Iowa
\parencite{Helmers2012}; about 47\% at the watershed scale in Indiana, where
drain flow fell from 11.0 to 5.9~cm and nitrate load from 236.3 to 126.4~ton,
with most reduction occurring in the November--March winter control period
\parencite{Ale2012}; and 54\% for the saturated-buffer system, dominated by the
controlled-drainage component \parencite{Ghane2025}. In India, controlled
drainage roughly halved seasonal NO$_3$-N loss (from 11.2 to
5.54~kg~ha$^{-1}$ per season) \parencite{Karegoudar2019}, and Italian water-table
management lowered annual N loss by 92\% (from 29 to 2~kg NO$_3$-N~ha$^{-1}$) and
P loss by 65\%, with cumulative nitrate loss strongly correlated with cumulative
outflow volume \parencite{Tolomio2018}. Chinese field and modeling studies found
controlled drainage increased soil and crop NO$_3$-N while reducing leaching and
drain nitrate loss by roughly 15--29\% \parencite{Dou2021,Dou2023}, and Iranian
work found lower flow-weighted nitrate concentrations and nitrate losses under
controlled drainage \parencite{Jouni2018}. A broad review of edge-of-field and
in-field practices placed controlled drainage among effective mitigation
options, with reported NO$_3$ loss reductions of 18--75\% for controlled
drainage, alongside 12--100\% for bioreactors and 25--78\% for wetlands
\parencite{Castellano2019}.

Denitrification provides a secondary but well-documented loss-reduction
mechanism where the water table is held high. Canadian farm-scale work found a
greater relative abundance of nitrate-reducing/facultative bacteria under
controlled drainage, supporting denitrification as a nitrate-loss pathway, while
also reducing total suspended solids and particulate phosphorus by damping
macropore flow \parencite{Tanga2025}. However, several syntheses caution that in
poorly drained soils the nitrate reduction is attributable mainly to reduced
tile effluent rather than to increased denitrification \parencite{THOMAS1995},
and Albanian field data found nutrient concentrations were not strongly affected
by drainage-system type, with outflow volume the dominant control on total
nutrient transport \parencite{Grazhdani1996}. Modeling in Belgium reproduced an
adverse relationship between denitrification and leaching---wider spacings and
higher water tables increase denitrification and reduce leaching but also reduce
plant uptake---so that total winter NO$_3$-N losses could be substantially
reduced under controlled drainage \parencite{ElSadek2002,ElSadek2002b}.

Phosphorus responses are generally favorable but smaller and occasionally
reversed. Controlled drainage lowered P loads in Sweden and Iran
\parencite{Wesström2007,Wesström2001,Jouni2018}, and the DWM synthesis reported
DRP and TP load reductions of about 34\% and 36\%
\parencite{Frankenberger2024}. In contrast, North Carolina simulations found
controlled drainage produced a small increase in phosphorus efflux even as it
cut nitrate efflux by as much as 34\% \parencite{DEAL1986}, and Iranian data
showed slightly higher P concentrations under controlled drainage though lower P
losses overall \parencite{Jouni2018}. Broader syntheses note that timing matters:
the most hazardous periods for surface-water pollution are autumn--winter, when
rainfall coincides with fertilizer on bare soil \parencite{Tolomio2018}, and P
peaks can shift from late winter under conventional drainage to periods of
highest outflow under controlled drainage \parencite{Wesström2007}. A recurring
caveat is that controlled drainage may increase nutrient loss through seepage
and surface runoff, a trade-off that partially offsets drain-flow reductions
\parencite{Frankenberger2024}.

\subsection{Repartitioning of Water Among Pathways}

A consolidated finding is that controlled and shallow drainage do not eliminate
the stored water but redirect it, most consistently into increased surface
runoff, and secondarily into evapotranspiration and seepage. DRAINMOD
simulations for Iowa showed that at a drainage intensity of 4.0~mm~day$^{-1}$,
shallow and controlled drainage each reduced average annual subsurface drainage
by about 25--27~mm (roughly 15\%), with most of that reduction reappearing as
increased surface runoff and little change in evapotranspiration
\parencite{Singh2007b}. Belgian, North Carolina, and Canadian studies
consistently report that controlled drainage decreases subsurface drainage and
increases surface runoff \parencite{ElSadek2002,ElSadek2002b,DEAL1986,Skaggs1995,Golmohammadi2016,Ndulue2024}.
At the watershed scale in Ontario, controlled drainage decreased subsurface
outflow and increased surface runoff, most often during the March--April
snowmelt period, while producing a shallower water table and only a slight
increase in evapotranspiration \parencite{Golmohammadi2016}. Polish simulations
projected controlled drainage and reduced subsurface outflow accompanied by an
approximately fivefold increase in surface runoff from drained arable land
\parencite{Sojka2020}. Finnish modeling similarly found controlled subsurface
drainage alone reduced drain discharge the most, whereas open-ditch damming
alone increased drain discharge and reduced groundwater outflow, and all
controlled-drainage methods increased topsoil-layer runoff while raising
evapotranspiration and reducing total runoff overall
\parencite{Isomäki2024}. Drainage-design choices are most clearly expressed in
the runoff outflow pathways \parencite{Isomäki2024}. Increased evapotranspiration
under controlled drainage---up to a 10\% ETact increase for corn---is a
recognized secondary sink that reduces total water lost to drainage
\parencite{deWit2022,DEAL1986,Ndulue2024}.

At the field-water-balance scale, tile drainage dominates the outflow partition
in humid Corn Belt settings. In Indiana, surface runoff represented only 1--10\%
of annual precipitation while subsurface drainage represented 26--45\%, with a
simulated balance of 7\% runoff, 34\% drain flow, and 51\% evapotranspiration,
and all ponding events generated by saturation excess rather than infiltration
excess \parencite{Saadat2020}. Iowa pothole modeling similarly found tile
discharge and evapotranspiration accounting for 52.4\% and 47.5\% of
precipitation, with drainage reducing but not eliminating ponding
\parencite{Amado2018,Martin2019}. In UK field-drained catchments, subsurface
field drains were the dominant pathway transferring runoff, sediment, and---the
authors emphasize---most sediment to the stream, with drainage responding in
every monitored event whereas surface runoff occurred in only some
\parencite{Deasy2009}. These partitions underscore that in artificially drained
landscapes the drain, not the soil surface, is usually the principal conduit.

Relative to undrained or predominantly surface-drained conditions, however,
improved subsurface drainage generally reduces surface runoff, peak outflow
rates, sediment, and phosphorus even as it increases nitrate delivery
\parencite{FAUSEY1995,SKAGGS1994,THOMAS1995,Gramlich2018}. In Oregon, tile
drainage lowered perched water tables and reduced watershed runoff and sediment
yield by about 65\% and 55\%, eliminated baseflow, increased lag time, and
lowered peak and average flow \parencite{ISTOK1983}. Albanian sites recorded
annual surface-runoff reductions of 30--56\% and reduced soil loss under
subsurface drainage, though total nitrogen losses rose 22--25\%
\parencite{Grazhdani1996}. Minnesota watershed modeling found tile drainage
reduced surface runoff, soil loss, and sediment yield, especially under no-till
\parencite{Maalim2013}. In North Carolina, improved subsurface drainage reduced
surface runoff by 192~mm~yr$^{-1}$ (66\%) on a Wasda soil while raising total
outflow by 10\%, and attenuated peak runoff strongly at the field scale (from
101 to 28~mm~day$^{-1}$ for a three-year storm) with differences persisting to
the 6216~ha watershed outlet \parencite{KONYHA1992}. Sandy loam soils in Canada
showed subsurface drainage minimizing surface runoff and total phosphorus loss,
whereas the reverse held on clay loam, and phosphorus speciation was mainly a
function of soil texture rather than transport pathway
\parencite{Eastman2010}. Mechanistically, good subsurface drainage increases
infiltration and drainage rates during rainfall and lowers solute concentrations
in surface runoff \parencite{Tong2016,Tao2017}, and by lowering the water table
it limits saturation-excess runoff and surface sealing \parencite{Augeard2005}.

\subsection{Drain Depth and Spacing as Design Levers}

Beyond outlet control, spacing and depth systematically shift the flow--loss
balance, and the evidence is coherent across field and simulation studies.
Increasing drain spacing reduces subsurface drainage and nitrate leaching to
drains but increases surface runoff, denitrification, and nitrate losses in
runoff, while decreasing plant uptake---defining an optimum-spacing trade-off
between crop production and environmental impact \parencite{ElSadek2002,ElSadek2002b}.
DRAINMOD-N simulations in North Carolina found that increasing spacing from 20
to 40~m decreased predicted NO$_3$-N losses by 47\% while still meeting
production goals \parencite{Skaggs1995}, and the MANAGE database confirmed that
wider spacing and shallower placement tend to reduce dissolved N loss, with
deeper drains showing disproportionately higher N loads at a given discharge
\parencite{Christianson2015}. Shallower drains, correspondingly, move less water
and nutrient offsite: reducing drain depth from 120 to 90~cm produced roughly
20\% reductions in annual drainage volume and nitrate-N over six years in
Minnesota (and in a 90-year simulation), and a 76~versus~120~cm comparison
yielded about 40\% reductions \parencite{Strock2011}. Regional syntheses agree
that shallow drains contain less sediment and nutrients than deeper drains and
that narrow spacing moves more water, nutrients, and pesticides offsite than
wide spacing \parencite{FAUSEY1995}. Chinese salinity studies identified
site-specific optima---8~m spacing at 0.7~m depth for a greenhouse soil
\parencite{Shao2012} and 0.9~m depth with 10~m spacing for chloride removal
\parencite{Yan2013b}.

Because different spacing--depth combinations can achieve similar drainage
intensity, design can be tuned to jointly meet productivity and water-quality
goals. Latvian field experience over 16--17 years on about 200 fields showed
that similar drainage intensity can be provided by different combinations of
spacing and depth, favoring the most economical choice, and that deep systematic
drainage (1.3--1.5~m) gave the best draining and economic indices in sandy and
loamy soils \parencite{SHKINKIS1979}. Belgian economic optimization identified a
conventional 50~m spacing at 1.25~m depth as the profit optimum, but showed that
widening spacing from 50 to 100~m reduced total nitrate-N loss by 20\% (from 37
to 29~kg~ha$^{-1}$) at only a small profit cost, and that different
depth--spacing scenarios can yield similar nitrate-loss reductions
\parencite{ElSadek2002b}. North Carolina simulations found deeper drains
increased profit but substantially increased NO$_3$-N losses, while improved
surface drainage allowed wider spacings and lower nitrate losses; the general
prescription is to use the minimum subsurface intensity required for production,
fit systems to soil properties, and apply controlled drainage when maximum
drainage is unnecessary \parencite{Breve1998}. Irrigated-system reviews likewise
conclude that deep drains are often unnecessary for salinity control and that
shallower drains with better operational management can reduce design discharges
without sacrificing yield \parencite{Ritzema2011}.

\subsection{Shallow, Multi-level, and Enhanced Drainage Configurations}

Shallow drainage emerges as a design that reproduces much of the flow-- and
load-reduction benefit of controlled drainage. Iowa studies found shallow
drainage reduced subsurface volume by 46--58\% and nitrate loads by 29--49\%,
generally without reducing crop yield (unlike controlled drainage, which
sometimes did) \parencite{Helmers2012,Schott2017}. A multi-state eastern-USA
analysis showed shallow drains produced quicker water-table drawdown, reduced
year-to-year corn-yield variability, and cut total flow by 3.4~cm versus deep
drains (with discharge reduction intensifying with annual precipitation), though
they increased surface runoff and had a lower benefit--cost ratio and longer
payback than deep drains \parencite{Ghane2021b}.

In saline irrigated settings, drain depth strongly controls the salinity of
exported water. Australian experiments found deep drains flowed continuously and
exported the largest salt loads (5867~kg~ha$^{-1}$), whereas shallow drains
flowed only after irrigation or rainfall, had low salinity, and produced the
greatest salt-load reduction while still providing adequate waterlogging control
\parencite{Christen2001b}. Multi-level (bilevel) systems combining deep and
shallow drains improved on single-level systems: they cut the time the water
table exceeded 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0~m by 84\%, 67\%, and 41\%, leached the root
zone faster, and produced much lower shallow-drain salinity (4.7 versus
31.7~dS~m$^{-1}$) while removing similar total salt
\parencite{Hornbuckle2007}. Analytical theory shows that adding shallow drains
between deep drains raises system drainage rate, produces shallower and more
evenly distributed flow paths, and allows wider deep-drain spacing for equivalent
total drainage \parencite{Hornbuckle2012}. Managed deep drains occupy an
intermediate position, reducing volume and salinity relative to unmanaged deep
drains \parencite{Ayars2006b,Christen2001b}.

Engineered enhancements to the drain itself further alter flow. ``Improved''
subsurface drainage that adds a permeable filter increased discharge---about 1.9
times conventional discharge at equal ponding depth---accelerated water-table
drawdown (over 15\% faster to reach 40~cm), and reduced surface runoff more
effectively, especially under high-intensity rainfall
\parencite{Tao2016,Tao2017}. Combined open-ditch plus subsurface designs and
improved filters increased flow rates and flooding/waterlogging removal
efficiency, with medium--medium and shallow--deep combinations offering good
performance at lower construction difficulty \parencite{Ren2021,Tao2025}.
Siphon-type subsurface drainage raised flow rates by roughly 27--90\% over
conventional and improved variants and shortened flooding and waterlogging
removal times substantially, verified against HYDRUS-2D
\parencite{Ren2023}. Artificial macropore flow tubes generated drain flow where
none occurred in a base treatment, increased drainage volume with tube density,
and promoted a more uniform, desalinated soil profile \parencite{Chen2021}.
Supplementary drainage of a clay field increased mean annual drain discharge by
a factor of 2.6, decreased topsoil-layer runoff by 75\% and groundwater outflow
by 18\%, and improved spring drainage efficiency without affecting
evapotranspiration \parencite{Häggblom2019}. Controlled drainage combined with
subirrigation raised groundwater levels, supplied 170--410~mm of infiltration,
and increased yields by up to 64\% in Dutch pilots, though supplies were
insufficient in extreme dry seasons \parencite{deWit2022,deWit2024}, and
subirrigation reduced nitrate-N loading and concentration in earlier North
American work \parencite{FAUSEY1995,Almen2021,Madramootoo2007}. Land retirement
with drains closed can eliminate drainage entirely, with downgradient retirement
generating larger reductions than upgradient \parencite{Purkey2001}.

\subsection{Salinity, Salt Load, and Trade-offs in Irrigated Systems}

In arid and irrigated regions the flow--loss coupling extends to salts, and
controlled drainage again reduces exported volumes and loads but introduces an
in-field salinity trade-off. Controlled drainage reduced total drainage outflow
and salt load in the United States and Australia \parencite{Ayars2006b}, cut
cumulative salt discharge by 5--7\% in tank experiments (while raising drainage
water salinity, especially late) \parencite{Zheng2025}, and reduced salt loads
in Iranian managed paddy systems, where free drainage produced salt loads 4--6
times higher than managed treatments \parencite{DarziNaftchali2018b}. Indian
saline-Vertisol trials found controlled drainage saved about 17\% of irrigation
water and roughly halved nitrogen loss, but reclaimed soil salinity more slowly
than conventional drainage \parencite{Karegoudar2019}, and Iranian work reported
soil salinity decreasing under free drainage but increasing slightly under
controlled drainage---an explicit productivity--salinity trade-off
\parencite{Jouni2018}. Reviews of Australian systems highlighted that many
subsurface systems removed more water than needed, mobilizing stored subsoil
salt, and that managing horizontal drains to avoid full drawdown could reduce
salt loads significantly \parencite{Christen2001}. Egyptian controlled drainage
reduced outflow, nitrate, and phosphorus losses in semiarid conditions, with
potential to lower the cost of pumping drainage water to the sea
\parencite{Wahba2001}.

The counterpart consolidated finding is that subsurface drainage is a proven,
economically favorable technology for reclaiming waterlogged and saline
irrigated land. Indian pilot areas recorded rice-yield increases of 30--120\%
and average increases of 54--136\% across crops, with cost--benefit ratios of
2.5--4.6 and payback periods of 2--3 years \parencite{Satyanarayana2007}, and
long-term monitoring found water tables 25\% deeper and soil salinity 50\% lower
in drained fields, with root-zone salinity falling from 11.5 to 6.0~dS~m$^{-1}$
and favorable cost--benefit ratios of 1.2--3.2 \parencite{Ritzema2008b}. Deep
drains proved unnecessary for salinity control, with shallow drains (0.8--1.0~m)
sufficiently effective \parencite{Ritzema2011,Satyanarayana2007}. Egyptian
drainage lowered water tables to 0.8--1.0~m within days of irrigation and
reduced saline soils from 40\% to 5--8\% (new non-saline areas) within four
years, proving highly profitable \parencite{Ali2001}. Comparable reclamation
successes and adequate water-table and salinity control were documented in
Mexico, Turkey, and China \parencite{MartínezBeltrán2007,Güngör2023,Shao2012,Yan2013b}.
Italian analysis showed drainage altered redox conditions above the drains,
favoring nitrification and confining active water circulation to the zone above
the drains \parencite{Mastrocicco2013}. At the basin scale, however, persistent
waterlogging and salinity in Central and South Asia reflect deteriorated
infrastructure and inadequate saline-effluent disposal, underscoring that
drainage must be managed within an integrated salt balance
\parencite{Dukhovny2007,Qureshi2008,Ritzema2016,SKAGGS1994}.

\subsection{Crop Yield, Water Use, and Agronomic Outcomes}

The agronomic evidence supports the conclusion that controlled and shallow
drainage can reduce losses while maintaining---or modestly improving---yield and
water productivity. The 61-study meta-analysis found an overall yield increase of
0.11\%, with dryland crops increasing slightly and paddy rice decreasing
\parencite{Wang2020c}, and North Carolina studies indicated an average yield
increase of about 5\% when controlled drainage is well managed
\parencite{Strock2011}. Dutch reviews reported yield gains of 2--5\% for corn,
with one instance of a 3\% decrease when the water table was too shallow early
in the season \parencite{deWit2022}. Controlled drainage improved nitrogen
efficiency in Sweden through lower drain N loads and higher crop uptake
\parencite{Wesström2007}, increased yield and water-use efficiency in Iranian
and Chinese systems \parencite{Jouni2018,Dou2021,Dou2023}, and increased water
productivity and yield in Iranian managed paddy fields with quick payback
\parencite{DarziNaftchali2018b}. Not all yield effects are positive: an Iowa
comparison found controlled drainage produced a significant yield reduction
relative to conventional drainage even as shallow drainage did not
\parencite{Helmers2012}, whereas a five-year Iowa study found yields similar
among conventional, controlled, and shallow drainage, with only the undrained
treatment reducing corn and soybean yields by 6\% \parencite{Schott2017}.

Drainage per se---versus no drainage---consistently stabilizes and improves
agronomic performance. Iowa nitrogen-response work found controlled drainage had
the highest agronomic efficiency at the agronomic optimum N rate and the least
interannual variability, while no drainage had the highest optimum N rate, the
greatest N surplus, and the most variability \parencite{Maas2022}. A conceptual
analysis showed drainage in continuous maize lowered the optimum N rate from 214
to 189~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$, raised zero-N yield by 10\% and agronomic efficiency by
14\%, and reduced global warming potential by 56\% through lower fertilizer and
N$_2$O emissions, positioning controlled and shallow drainage as designs that
reduce leaching while maintaining yield and conserving soil water and carbon
\parencite{Castellano2019}. Drainage also advances field trafficability: Canadian
work found subsurface drainage advanced spring trafficability by 2--5 weeks and
recommended a 30.5~m spacing, with intensity mattering most at wide spacing
\parencite{PAUL1983}, and Iowa analysis showed drained treatments enabled earlier
target planting dates than undrained soils in wet years, with controlled and
shallow drainage reducing nitrate loss without delaying planting or reducing
yield \parencite{Schott2017b}. Managed subsurface drainage accelerated ponded-
and groundwater removal and enabled timely second-crop establishment in
Bangladesh, India, and Iran \parencite{Okwany2016,Prathapar2018,Rahimi2024}.

\subsection{Persistence of Benefits and Behavior under Climate Change}

Climate-projection studies consistently conclude that the hydrologic and
water-quality benefits of controlled drainage persist, and in some seasons
strengthen, under future conditions. In Ohio, conventional discharge was
projected to decline in all seasons (most in autumn), while controlled-drainage
annual discharge reduction remained near 4--6\% and summer growing-season
reductions increased, indicating greater warm-season value
\parencite{Pease2017}. Michigan simulations projected free-drainage discharge
rising 20\% by mid-century while controlled drainage continued to reduce
discharge by 63--72\%, with future and historical performance statistically
indistinguishable \parencite{BinShokrana2023}. Finnish future scenarios projected
controlled drainage effects on groundwater and discharge remaining similar to the
historical interval, with annual drain-discharge reductions of roughly
21--46~mm depending on management \parencite{Salla2022}. Canadian projections of
warmer, wetter conditions found controlled drainage reducing subsurface drainage
more strongly than free drainage and raising evapotranspiration, though rising
temperature drove dry-stress yield reductions, motivating capture, storage, and
reuse of drainage water as an adaptation \parencite{Ndulue2024}. Chinese and
Polish studies found that appropriate surface-depression and outlet-blocking
strategies (e.g., starting regulation on 1~March) could reduce future runoff and
maintain groundwater at present-day levels \parencite{Awad2021,Sojka2020}.
Field monitoring in Norway demonstrated that subsurface systems react rapidly to
precipitation-induced groundwater rise and can handle high precipitation amounts
similar to climate-change projections, with two-exponential recession behavior
capturing fast and slow flow \parencite{Deelstra2015}, and Polish work quantified
seasonal retention and outflow factors, showing the greatest flood risk under
rapid snowmelt on near-saturated soil \parencite{Szejba2020}.

\subsection{Landscape Extent, Monitoring, and the Evidence Base}

Quantifying where and how much land is drained frames the significance of these
flow and loss effects. Regional mapping in Germany estimated drained proportions
of about 25\% overall, highest on hydromorphic groundwater- and
stagnant-water-influenced soils (up to 30--40\%) and lowest on leachate soils,
with the method transferable to river-basin and national scales
\parencite{Hirt2005,Hirt2011}. Multiple United States drainage-extent datasets
diverge substantially at the watershed scale, indicating that the best estimate
depends on local drainage conditions and dominant practice
\parencite{Jame2022}. Regional drainage-requirement and monitoring studies in
India, Turkey, Egypt, and elsewhere documented drainable-surplus estimates,
recommended design rates (e.g., 2~mm~day$^{-1}$), and generally good system
performance, with water tables dropping below the root zone within days of
irrigation \parencite{Kumar2006,Güngör2023,Ali2001,MartínezBeltrán2007,Rimidis2003}.
Setback analyses show drain depth is the key determinant of lateral setback
distance for protecting wetlands \parencite{Tangen2018}, and vulnerability
indices require distinguishing surface from subsurface artificial drainage to
avoid mis-classifying runoff and leaching risk \parencite{Baffaut2020}. Runoff
from small catchments and drainage fields agreed well, supporting regional
prediction \parencite{Veinbergs2022}, while watershed models such as SWAT were
found to under-represent artificial subsurface drainage processes without
explicit adaptation \parencite{Manguerra1998,Golmohammadi2016}. Broadly,
subsurface drainage relative to surface drainage lowers sediment, phosphorus, and
pesticide concentrations but carries much greater nitrate loads, and its
hydrologic and water-quality effects vary widely with soil, topography, land use,
and system design---so conservation designs such as controlled drainage offer
substantial potential to reduce nitrate losses and improve water quality where
appropriately sited \parencite{Blann2009,Gramlich2018,FAUSEY1995,SKAGGS1994}.
Socio-economic and landscape considerations temper further expansion in some
contexts: Finnish analyses found replacing remaining open ditches with subsurface
drainage could reduce social welfare through biodiversity and landscape losses,
so surface drainage remained socially optimal for lower land qualities
\parencite{HietalaKoivu2004,Saikkonen2014}. The overarching, well-supported
conclusion across this literature is that drainage systems can be deliberately
designed and managed---through spacing, depth, and outlet control---to satisfy
agricultural production and environmental objectives simultaneously
\parencite{Skaggs1995,Breve1998,ElSadek2002b,Strock2011,Ritzema2016}.

### Material: Subsurface Drainage for Soil Salt Leaching

\subsection{Overview}

Subsurface (tile) drainage is one of the best-established engineering interventions for leaching accumulated salts out of irrigated and waterlogged soil profiles, and the assembled evidence---spanning field experiments, lysimeter and soil-column studies, long-term monitoring, and calibrated numerical models across arid, semi-arid, monsoonal, coastal, and cold-climate settings---converges on a coherent picture of what the technology accomplishes and how. Three headline conclusions are strongly supported. First, installing subsurface drainage reliably lowers the water table below the root zone and removes salt from the upper profile, converting saline and waterlogged land into productive cropland, with topsoil salinity reductions commonly of 35--80\% and corresponding crop-yield gains; a global meta-analysis of 891 experiments confirms that drainage significantly reduces soil salinity and increases yield relative to no drainage \parencite{Yang2025}, and a full production-scale field study validates outcomes previously seen only at small scale \parencite{Derby2024}. Second, the rate and completeness of desalinization are governed by a well-characterized set of design and operational parameters---drain spacing, drain depth, envelope/filter, leaching-water quantity, and complementary practices---such that closer spacing, greater depth (up to soil-specific limits), and larger applied leaching volume shorten the desalinization period. Third, the salt exported in drainage water is initially high and declines over years as the profile is flushed, so that salt-balance accounting (salt removed versus salt added) and the temporal trajectory of effluent salinity are central to both agronomic and environmental evaluation. The remainder of this section synthesizes the consolidated findings on reclamation efficacy, the design parameters that control salt removal, drainage-water salinity and salt balance, the vertical and ionic structure of leaching, the hydraulics of efficient leaching and enhancement techniques, saline/brackish water reuse, integration with complementary practices, and the modeling and monitoring tools that underpin these conclusions.

\subsection{Reclamation Efficacy: Soil Desalinization and Crop Response}

Across widely differing environments, subsurface drainage produces substantial and repeatable reductions in root-zone salinity, typically concentrated in the surface layers, together with improved crop performance. In the Northern Great Plains, topsoil salinity in a tile-drained area declined over time while an adjacent undrained area showed little change or a slight increase; drainage alleviated both salinity and wetness constraints, and soybean and corn yields improved within three years, with continued soybean gains expected over at least eight years \parencite{Derby2024}. Comparable field-scale reclamations are documented in India, where average soil salinity in drained blocks fell from 7.18 to 4.62~dS~m$^{-1}$ (an average 35.7\% decrease, ranging 16.0--66.3\% across blocks) while the undrained area rose from 9.00 to 9.54~dS~m$^{-1}$, and crop yields rose by 18.75--27.62\% for wheat, rice, pearl millet, and sorghum \parencite{Sharma2006}. In monsoonal India, drainage combined with monsoon leaching brought initially crop-limiting salinity down to levels suitable for irrigated agriculture, with weighted-average ECe after 13 monsoon leachings of 1.4, 2.1, and 3.4~dS~m$^{-1}$ at 25, 50, and 75~m spacing \parencite{Sharma2000}. Rolling-topography reclamation under unusually wide drain spacing lowered topsoil salinity from 8.4 to about 2.1~dS~m$^{-1}$ and raised paddy yields, with cropping intensity increasing from 143\% to 191\% \parencite{Manjunatha2004}, and multi-scenario evaluation of the Tungabhadra command reduced head- and middle-reach topsoil ECe from roughly 9~dS~m$^{-1}$ to 1.9--3.8~dS~m$^{-1}$, with weaker effect in the tail reach \parencite{Karegoudar2022}.

Reclamation magnitudes are similarly large in China's arid and coastal saline lands. Subsurface pipe drainage reduced 0--80~cm salinity from 35 to 15~g~kg$^{-1}$, whereas open-ditch controls remained at 25--35~g~kg$^{-1}$ \parencite{Wang2020}; a drip-irrigation-plus-subsurface-pipe system cut soil EC from 14.42--15.74 to 2.86--6.91~dS~m$^{-1}$ over three leaching cycles and raised seed cotton yield 52\% \parencite{Heng2021}; and after four years the salinity of the 0--1.0~m layer fell from 27.6 to 4.1--7.2~dS~m$^{-1}$, reclaiming extremely saline soil to moderately saline \parencite{Zhang2021}. One-year monitoring by fixed electromagnetic-induction profiles showed soil apparent EC falling 40.0--62.0\% across depths (mean 49.5\%) and a sharp reduction in spatial heterogeneity \parencite{Yu2025}. Surface-concentrated desalinization is a recurrent signature: 0--20~cm desalination rates exceeded 80\% while deeper layers lagged or even accumulated salt \parencite{Han2023}, and the meta-analysis found the most substantial reduction at depths of 20~cm or less, diminishing with depth and becoming nonsignificant below 80~cm \parencite{Yang2025}. Reductions of comparable magnitude are reported for Turkey (topsoil salinity from 10.3 to 2.0~dS~m$^{-1}$, about 80\% at 20~cm) \parencite{Bahçeci2009}, Tunisia (mean ECe decrease 23.3\%) \parencite{HajAmor2019}, and semi-arid Canada, where combined drainage and windbreak treatment lowered 0--450~mm ECe from 14.08 to 9.58~dS~m$^{-1}$ and raised barley yield up to twentyfold \parencite{Steppuhn2006,Steppuhn2017}. Spatial reclamation is accompanied by homogenization: leaching plus drainage shifted salinity from strong to moderate variability (coefficient of variation from 48.13\% to 28.14\%) and expanded mildly saline area to 76\%, with 7~g~kg$^{-1}$ identified as the critical salinity for the homogenizing tendency \parencite{Yu2015}.

Improved soil physical condition and productivity accompany desalinization. Drainage increased porosity, available water, and infiltration rate (from 5.3 to 13.6--16.0~mm~h$^{-1}$) while lowering bulk density, and reduced SARe and ESP \parencite{Sharma2000}; the meta-analysis reports increased soil permeability and organic matter and decreased CO$_3^{2-}$, HCO$_3^-$, Cl$^-$, SO$_4^{2-}$, Na$^+$, and Mg$^{2+}$ \parencite{Yang2025}. In Egypt, drainage raised yields by 13--16\% across wheat, rice, cotton, and maize and lifted net farm income by 9--44\% depending on region \parencite{Kotb2000}. Where drainage is absent or the water table stays shallow, combined waterlogging and salinity are more damaging than waterlogging alone, and yields fail above 12~dS~m$^{-1}$ \parencite{Kahlown2002}.

\subsection{Design Parameters Governing Salt Removal}

A consistent, quantitatively supported relationship links subsurface-drainage intensity to desalinization: closer drain spacing and greater drain depth shorten the desalinization period and increase salt removal, though depth benefits saturate at soil-specific limits and shallow drains lose effectiveness. A DRAINMOD-based analysis showed the desalinization period was negatively related to drainage intensity; at 1.2~m depth, salinity fell 55.5\% in the first two years at 20~m spacing but only 5.9\% in five years at 200~m spacing, and reaching a slightly saline target within a decade required spacing below 30~m \parencite{Chen2025}. The meta-analysis generalizes this: salinity reduction improves with smaller spacing, greater depth, and longer operation time, with climate- and groundwater-specific layout recommendations---spacing $>$30~m and depth $>$1.2~m in humid climates, 10--20~m spacing with depth $>$1.2~m in arid climates with water table $\leq$1~m \parencite{Yang2025}. Field and column studies repeatedly identify optimum spacings: 15~m gave the best desalination (effective rate 65.8\%, salt-removal ratio 69.6\%) among 10--25~m spacings \parencite{Wang2020}; 20~m spacing at 1.5~m depth optimized root-zone desalination (39.02\% reduction versus control) by multi-objective optimization \parencite{Wang2025c}; 6~m spacing with 40~cm outlet depth maximized average desalination \parencite{Han2023}; and 30~cm partition spacing outperformed wider spacings \parencite{Chen2022}. Smaller spacing also lowers the leaching quota needed for a given efficiency and deepens and stabilizes the water table \parencite{Yang2022c}, while desalination benefits decay once spacing exceeds about 30~m, with some wide-spacing/deep-burial treatments even showing negative desalination \parencite{Zhu2025b}.

Drain depth effects are pronounced but bounded. Pipes shallower than 1.0~m were essentially invalid for discharging salt or controlling the water table \parencite{Qian2021b}, and desalination and salt discharge indices correlated positively with depth \parencite{Qian2021b,Tian2023c,Tian2024}. Yet SaltMod and SahysMod simulations show diminishing returns beyond a threshold: root-zone salinity decreased with depth up to 1.4~m with no further change beyond it \parencite{Bahçeci2007}, and increasing depth from 0.80 to 1.60~m only slightly reduced salinity while increasing discharge \parencite{Bahçeci2006}. For crop-yield optimization under saline irrigation, an intermediate depth is frequently preferred---0.8~m gave the best leaching and highest maize yield \parencite{Feng2017}, relative yield and water-use efficiency peaked near 90--100~cm \parencite{Feng2019}, and reducing depth from 140 to 100--120~cm saved 10--15\% of irrigation water with only a small yield penalty and no significant long-term salinity increase \parencite{Muhammad2021}. Envelope and filter design is a first-order control on discharge and salt leaching: the envelope factor dominated over pipe material, with spun-bonded non-woven geotextile, single layer, and 3.0\% perforation optimal \parencite{Li2022}; double-layer geotextile integrated filtration, anti-clogging, and drainage best (all scenarios $>$95\% salt removal), while triple layers caused filter-cake clogging \parencite{Wang2024d}; and the O$_{90}$/d$_{90}$ ratio predicts clogging and soil loss into pipes \parencite{Elzoghby2021}. Complex stratigraphy modifies the optimum: low-permeability silty interlayers reduce leaching, and pipes should be placed below such interlayers, with desalination increasing linearly with depth and interlayer conductivity and exponentially as spacing decreases \parencite{Guo2024b}; a shallow red-clay layer likewise reduces drainage and salt-removal efficiency \parencite{Tian2024}. Long-term modeling indicates deeper, wider drains desalinize the whole profile better whereas shallow, close drains act mainly in the top 80~cm \parencite{KAMRA1991}, and economic analyses in multiple settings converge on roughly 20~m spacing at 1.2~m depth as the maximum-return layout \parencite{Chen2025}.

\subsection{Drainage-Water Salinity, Salt Balance, and Temporal Decline}

Because tile drains intercept the salt-laden water flushed from the profile, drainage-water salinity is characteristically much higher than irrigation-water salinity and declines over years as the reservoir of stored salt is depleted. Effluent EC exceeding irrigation EC has been documented repeatedly \parencite{Li2022,Karegoudar2022}, with ECdw/ECiw ratios of 11--17 and annual salt export near 29.4~t~ha$^{-1}$ in a high-percolation Iranian sugarcane district \parencite{Akram2013}, and 8{,}000~mg~L$^{-1}$ irrigation water producing 14{,}000~mg~L$^{-1}$ drainage water through evapotranspirative concentration in California \parencite{KARAJEH1994c}. Salt-balance accounting is the standard diagnostic of reclamation: salt removed exceeded salt added in the first year (125.4 versus 119.8~mg), although over the full record (August 1998--April 2002) cumulative salts added ($\approx$452.9~mg) exceeded salts removed ($\approx$372.9~mg) \parencite{Manjunatha2004}, an oasis system exported about 94\% of the imported salt load while discharging 42\% of irrigation water, yielding a positive salt balance \parencite{Askri2022}, and a straw-drainage module removed about 1.7 times the input salt mass \parencite{Lu2019}. Salt removal can, however, fall short: an oasis-scale system removed only 30\% then 15\% of salts owing to mineral and root clogging \parencite{BenAissa2013}, and a sand-clogged gravel filter drained no more than 27\% of the imported salt \parencite{HajAmor2019}. During the irrigation--drainage season, subsurface pipes discharged 3.12--4.29~t~ha$^{-1}$ of salt, accounting for 34--46\% of soil salt-storage change, the remainder leaching to deeper layers or groundwater \parencite{Liu2025c}; over four years pipes removed 40\% of total salt discharge \parencite{Heng2021}.

The temporal decline of effluent salinity is a robust, long-validated pattern. Long-term records from systems aged 0--40 years confirm that most residual salt is removed in less than ten years, with effluent-salinity--drain-age relationships stable across decades \parencite{JOHNSTON1993}. Modeling of Haryana sites indicates 15--50 years to reach equilibrium effluent salinity, so drains may yield poor-quality water during much of their service life; drainage-water salinity is more sensitive to leaching fraction than to drain spacing, and raising the leaching fraction from 0.2 to 0.4 halved the equilibration time \parencite{Kelleners2000}. Field observations show drainage-water salinity first rising (initial mobilization of stored salt) then declining with a lag of about two years \parencite{Manjunatha2004}, decreasing to a mean of 2.6~dS~m$^{-1}$ suitable for winter irrigation \parencite{Sharma2006}, and progressively falling across cropping seasons as profile salinity declined \parencite{Jayawardane2001}. A cumulative-drainage relationship governs leaching: significant linear relations link cumulative drainage water to salt leached (R$^2=0.970$) and an exponential relation links drainage/soil depth to remaining/initial salt (R$^2=0.764$), with about 70\% of salt at 1.0~m depth removed by 339~mm of drainage over three years \parencite{Bahçeci2009}. Salt-mass export is strongly conditioned by geometry and geochemistry: drainage-water salinity and cumulative salt load increase with both drain depth and spacing, depth having the larger effect where groundwater quality declines with depth (an 80~m/4~m system exported roughly 36\% more than a 20~m system) \parencite{GRISMER1993}; deeper impervious layers and aquifer layering raise effluent salinity \parencite{KAMRA1991,KAMRA1994}. At watershed scale, region-wide tile drainage increased tile salt loading from 6 to 359~kg~ha$^{-1}$ while reducing groundwater salt loading, giving a modest net increase in total salt yield (1619 to 1651~kg~ha$^{-1}$) and a 12\% decline in aquifer SO$_4$ concentration, but producing episodic loading pulses that doubled or tripled baseline in-stream loads \parencite{Addab2022}. Nile Delta monitoring shows drainage-water salinity varying strongly in space and time, with secondary drains up to four times more saline than collectors \parencite{ElAgha2020}. The input/output salt-mass ratio stabilizes near 0.4, and higher application efficiency (lower leaching fraction) enhances salt buildup \parencite{KARAJEH1994c}.

\subsection{Vertical Redistribution and Ion-Specific Leaching}

Leaching under subsurface drainage is vertically stratified and ionically selective, a structure with direct implications for where and how fast reclamation occurs. Desalinization is strongest at the surface and weakens with depth: upper $>$ middle $>$ lower layer ordering is reported for continuous-head leaching \parencite{Chen2022}, deeper layers may resist or reverse (negative desalination in the 60--80~cm layer even as 0--20~cm exceeds 80\%) \parencite{Han2023}, and reduction magnitude is consistently greater in surface than subsoil layers \parencite{Narjary2024,Tian2024}. Soil texture modulates this: permeable sandy-loam and loam profiles sustain EC reduction while clayey sublayers limit leaching and can show negative EC change at depth \parencite{Qin2025}. Ion mobility follows characteristic sequences. Under heavy rainfall the order was Na$^+$ $>$ Cl$^-$ $>$ SO$_4^{2-}$ $>$ Mg$^{2+}$ \parencite{Yu2016}, and leaching efficiency was highest for Cl and Na (2018 leaching ratios of 91\% and 88\% at 0--20~cm), intermediate and irrigation-sensitive for Mg and SO$_4$, and lowest for Ca, which persists largely as precipitated gypsum ($>$90\% of surface Ca), whereas $>$90\% of Na is free Na$^+$ \parencite{Qian2025}. The chemical composition of drainage water resembles groundwater more than the leached-out salt \parencite{Qian2025}, and drainage significantly reduced chloride and sulfate in the profile \parencite{Sharma2000}.

A frequently observed complication is re-accumulation. Seasonal dynamics follow a three-phase ``wash-out--recovery--accumulation'' pattern, with re-accumulation positively correlated with reproductive growth (r~$=0.83$), Cl$^-$ and Na$^+$ leached downward, SO$_4^{2-}$ retained in the middle layer, and HCO$_3^-$ dominant near the surface; some wide-spacing treatments showed a 5--8~g~kg$^{-1}$ mid-layer increase signaling secondary-salinization risk \parencite{Zhu2025b}. Late-stage re-salinization occurred despite effective spring leaching, more severely under controlled than free drainage \parencite{Dou2022}, and surface salt accumulation reappears during dry, low-drainage periods \parencite{Yu2016,Shi2021b}. The direction of salt movement itself can be reoriented toward the drain, changing from deeper-zone or lateral movement to flow into the open drainage \parencite{Okuda2020}. Salinity distribution around drains is spatially organized: desalinization zones form within about 0.0--0.3~m of the drain \parencite{Lu2019}, the desalination rate is greatest immediately adjacent to the pipe and smallest farthest from it \parencite{Heng2021}, salinity increases with distance from the outlet \parencite{Han2023}, and normalized-salinity analysis places low-salinity zones within about 5~m and high-salinity zones beyond 7--8~m of the drain, with the greatest reduction within 7~m of a deep drain \parencite{Mirlas2003,Benyamini2005}.

\subsection{Leaching Hydraulics and Efficiency Enhancement}

A mechanistic body of work explains why leaching through subsurface drains is often inefficient and how efficiency can be raised, quantifying the non-uniform flow field between drains. Under complete ponding, flow concentrates near the drain: roughly 80\% of flow passes through 20\% of the soil volume, about 40\% of water after 450~h does not contribute to leaching, and desalinizing the full profile required 5400~h versus only 900~h for the top 1~m \parencite{RAO1991}. The flow fractions from successive strips fall steeply from the drain (0.819, 0.134, 0.035, 0.012) \parencite{Youngs2000}. Two families of remedies follow. Progressive/partial flooding leaches the central (poorly flushed) area first: sequential ponding achieved equal desalinization with about 50\% less water \parencite{RAO1991}, and a progressive four-strip schedule used only 1.7\% of the water and 8.3\% of the time of complete flooding (16.4\% water and 25.0\% time for infinitely deep soil), while also cutting evaporation \parencite{Youngs2000}. Low-permeability barriers or surface mulches over the drain redistribute flow toward uniform downward leaching: a barrier reduces wasted near-drain flow, with a 10~m barrier saving 52--82\% of leaching water and a half-extent barrier optimal \parencite{Yu2018}; a low-permeability surface mulch cut leaching timeframe and effluent volume by 64\% and 81\% and raised leaching efficiency, outperforming a buried barrier \parencite{Zhang2022}.

Soil structure and heterogeneity strongly govern efficiency. Macro-pores bypass surface infiltration and weaken the gradient toward the drain, increasing the time to leach to 0.6~m about twelvefold and water use roughly eighteenfold, with the effect worsening at greater macro-pore depth and density \parencite{Xin2016}. Layering can improve leaching relative to uniform soil, yet macro-pores still raise water use by more than an order of magnitude \parencite{Xin2016}. Mass-transfer limitations in dual-domain soils extend the leaching timeframe as trapped salt slowly diffuses from immobile domains; lower mass-transfer coefficient, larger capacity ratio, lower conductivity, wider spacing, and shallower depth all prolong leaching (immobile-domain leaching took 186 days versus 58 for the mobile domain in one case) \parencite{Zhang2024}. Salt movement in these systems is largely advection-dominated \parencite{Yu2018}. Applied-water strategy matters: at equal cumulative leaching water, multiple low-ponding cycles matched fewer high-ponding cycles, desalination increased with repeated cycles (about 64\% per saline-saturation drainage, reaching about 96\% after the fourth cycle), and deeper ponding (300~mm) improved desalting relative to shallow ponding \parencite{Jia2023}; deepening the pipe improved desalination more than widening the rice-husk zone \parencite{Jia2023}. More salt discharged under flood than drip leaching at the same discharge rate, and salt load rose linearly with discharge (R$^2>0.99$) \parencite{Li2022}. In fine-textured soils leaching efficiency is intrinsically low and declines with time, making salt removal a slow process \parencite{MANN1982}. Ditching and deep vertical rotary tillage enhance infiltration and leaching: ditching raised drainage per unit area by 14.65\% and surface (0--20~cm) desalination by 13.40\% where burial depth was under 1.3~m \parencite{Tian2023}, and deep vertical rotary tillage lowered bulk density, enhanced infiltration, and improved desalinization and yield \parencite{Li2026}.

\subsection{Saline and Brackish Water Reuse under Drainage}

A consolidated line of evidence establishes that subsurface drainage enables the safe agricultural reuse of saline and brackish water, provided salt balance is maintained by adequate leaching. Blended saline drainage water up to 9~dS~m$^{-1}$ produced about 90\% wheat yield in semi-arid Indian soils, with cyclic canal/drainage use generally outperforming continuous drainage-water use and long-term use causing only slight soil changes after monsoon leaching \parencite{Sharma2004}. Five-year data show relative wheat yields of 95.8\%, 90.3\%, 83.7\%, and 77.8\% at 6, 9, 12, and 18.8~dS~m$^{-1}$, with monsoon rain and pre-sowing irrigation keeping post-leaching root-zone salinity below 4~dS~m$^{-1}$ and no significant adverse effect on hydraulic conductivity \parencite{Sharma1998}. Conjunctive-use economics favor blending: mixing drainage water (12~dS~m$^{-1}$) with canal water optimized wheat yield at 4~dS~m$^{-1}$ and raised farm income \parencite{AGNIHOTRI1992}. In China, brackish-water leaching under drainage discharged salt exceeding the salt imported in favorable treatments (26.2--153.8~g replacement), with burial depth and irrigation amount controlling replacement and brackish-water salinity having little effect \parencite{Li2023}; saline (6~g~L$^{-1}$) irrigation matched freshwater wheat yields with no annual salt accumulation in the 0--45~cm layer and continued year-on-year salinity decline \parencite{Gao2023}; and drainage kept saline irrigation viable up to 6.25~dS~m$^{-1}$ for maize at 0.8~m depth \parencite{Feng2017}, 4.0--4.4~dS~m$^{-1}$ at 80~cm depth for grain \parencite{Feng2021}, and 8.0~dS~m$^{-1}$ under cut-soiler drainage without extra salt loading \parencite{Neha2022}. Optimal cotton outcomes occurred at brackish salinity of 2--4~g~L$^{-1}$ with 30~m spacing, the preferred depth increasing with salinity \parencite{Li2024}, and maximum acceptable seawater-mixture rates rose with leaching amount (37.27--52.57\% for 80--160~mm) \parencite{Lu2019}. Reuse nonetheless carries sodicity and accumulation risks: cyclic reuse supplying 70\% of summer irrigation caused profile salinity fluctuations and sodic surface soils (SARe~$>$~11), with EC rising from 2 to 6~dS~m$^{-1}$ despite winter-rain leaching \parencite{Mitchell2000}, and drainage-water SAR exceeded effluent SAR during reuse \parencite{Jayawardane2001}. Modeled reuse of poor-quality drain water over subsurface drains caused only marginal deterioration of shallow groundwater \parencite{KARAJEH1994c}. Blending criteria and rice-based reclamation for saline water are codified for the Nile Delta \parencite{Kotb2000}, and drainage/reduced-evaporation/low-EC-water combinations are highlighted as core salinity-control components in a global SICS review \parencite{Cuevas2019}.

\subsection{Integration with Complementary Practices}

Subsurface drainage performs best when integrated with irrigation, tillage, cropping, and hydraulic complements, and several such combinations are well documented. Controlled drainage conserves water and raises yield but trades off leaching: it reduced shallow ditch drainage by about 60\% and buffered salinity via stable deep drainage \parencite{Jia2007}, reduced subsurface drainage from 44.5 to 16.8~cm and the leaching factor to about 0.3 \parencite{Jia2007}, and improved crop water productivity while increasing late-stage re-salinization, with a 50~cm growth-stage depth optimal for sunflower \parencite{Dou2022}. Combined subsurface-pipe-plus-open-ditch systems desalinated effectively (drainage volume ratio about 9:1 favoring ditches), reaching 170\% of a single ditch's efficiency, with optimal pipe depth 90--110~cm, ditch depth 180--210~cm, and collection length 750--850~m \parencite{Yao2024}. Timing drainage to snowmelt and the rainy season kept the water table within 1~m, cut waterlogging by over two-thirds, more than doubled surface salt leaching, and produced residual benefits beyond active drainage periods \parencite{Yu2016}. Autumn/pre-planting irrigation coupled with drainage optimized emergence and raised sunflower yield 9.68--14.70\%, with a recommended 180~mm autumn quota \parencite{Liu2023,Liu2025c}. In cold semi-arid Canada, tall-wheatgrass windbreaks increased snow water equivalent (72.8 versus 25.5~mm) and, combined with drainage, gradually reduced root-zone salinity and multiplied barley yield, while pre-seeding irrigation lifted alfalfa emergence \parencite{Steppuhn2006,Steppuhn2017}. Cut-soiler-based preferential shallow drainage cut salinity 50--60\% over two years and raised pearl-millet and mustard yields, with drainage and salt removal greatest in the rainy season \parencite{Neha2022,Neha2022b}. Gypsum and cover-crop amendment improved soil physical properties and reduced crust strength under saline reuse, though gypsum did not lower EC \parencite{MANN1982,Mitchell2000}, and balanced N--K fertilization (150~kg~N and 50~kg~K~ha$^{-1}$) optimized wheat yield as drainage progressively lowered salinity \parencite{Singh2001b}. Where upward hydraulic pressure or perched conditions dominate, drainage design must adapt: combined drainage with relief wells was the most effective configuration for reducing salinity and lowering the water table within 2--3 years, whereas shallow drainage alone failed under upward pressure \parencite{Benyamini2005,Mirlas2003}. Low-cost alternative technologies---straw-drainage modules performing comparably to conventional pipe systems \parencite{Lu2019} and cut-drain shallow drainage that reduced remaining salt to a third or quarter of the control and raised cotton yield about 18\% \parencite{Okuda2020}---extend the approach to resource-limited settings. Regional coupling of optimized drip irrigation with tailored subsurface drainage reduced district-wide irrigation by 10\% and soil salinity by 15\% while raising yields \parencite{Liu2026}.

\subsection{Modeling, Prediction, and Monitoring Tools}

The quantitative understanding above rests on a mature toolkit of validated models and monitoring methods, which is itself a consolidated finding. Physically based drainage--salinity models reproduce field water tables and salt dynamics well: DRAINMOD and DRAINMOD-S captured water-table fluctuations (NSE up to 0.84) and soil salinity (NSE 0.40--0.92) and were used to derive spacing/depth recommendations \parencite{Chen2025,Tian2023,Tian2023c,Tian2024,Davoodi2019,Li2024}. HYDRUS variants (1D/2D/3D) and their couplings simulated water--salt transport, drainage mass, and crop response accurately across brackish-leaching, forest, tillage, and controlled-drainage settings \parencite{Li2023,Dou2022,Wang2024c,Li2026,Jia2023,Liu2022}, and coupled HYDRUS--EPIC and AquaCrop/DRAINMOD-S frameworks reliably reproduced soil water--salt, yield, and evapotranspiration for evaluating long-term saline-water irrigation \parencite{Feng2019,Feng2021,Li2024}. Salt-and-water-balance models---SaltMod, SahysMod, SWMS-2D, and SALTIRSOIL\_M---predicted root-zone salinity, drain discharge, and effluent salinity closely enough for management use \parencite{Bahçeci2006,Bahçeci2007,Yao2017,Qian2021b,Abdelkrim2021}, and finite-element and analytical solutions for reactive-solute transport, dual-domain mass transfer, and barrier/mulch schemes matched observations and yielded design guidelines \parencite{KAMRA1991,KAMRA1994,Zhang2022,Zhang2024,Youngs2000}. Watershed-scale SWAT-Salt tracked ion loading through tile, groundwater, and runoff pathways and linked local tile behavior to downstream salinity \parencite{Addab2022,Liu2026}, while long-validated empirical effluent-quality relationships remain reliable across decades \parencite{JOHNSTON1993,Kelleners2000}. On the monitoring side, electromagnetic-induction and apparent-EC surveys with geostatistical and time-specific-regression methods enable rapid, nondestructive, depth-resolved assessment of drainage-system efficacy and salinity mapping \parencite{Narjary2024,Song2023,Yu2025,Singh2020d,Mirlas2003}, and emerging IoT-plus-LSTM digital-twin systems achieved high-accuracy soil-EC and water-content forecasting (test R$^2$ up to 0.97) for coordinated irrigation--drainage control \parencite{Qin2025}. Comprehensive field characterizations of irrigation-induced salinity and shallow-groundwater conditions provide the baseline against which these tools are calibrated \parencite{Gates2002}. Collectively, this modeling and monitoring capability is what allows the design and salt-balance principles summarized above to be transferred with confidence to new sites and management scenarios.

### Material: Drainage Management and Crop Yield Improvement

\subsection{Overview}

Across a broad range of climates, soils, and crops, the evidence consistently establishes that agricultural subsurface drainage and its managed variants (controlled drainage, subirrigation, and drainage water recycling) raise crop yields relative to undrained or poorly drained conditions, primarily by relieving excess-water (waterlogging) stress early in the season and, where water tables can be managed, by alleviating water-deficiency stress later on. The strongest, most transferable conclusions come from long-term field experiments, multi-site and meta-analytic syntheses, and calibrated simulation studies. A United States meta-analysis of 154 pairwise comparisons found that controlled drainage and subirrigation increased crop yield by an average of \(8.0\%\) relative to conventional free drainage \parencite{Singh2022b}, while a combined experiment-and-producer study reported soybean yield gains of \(275\ \mathrm{kg\ ha^{-1}}\) (\(8\%\)) in experiments and \(157\ \mathrm{kg\ ha^{-1}}\) (\(4\%\)) in producer fields under artificial drainage \parencite{Mourtzinis2021}. The authors of that study framed subsurface drainage explicitly as an effective practice for increasing yields in environments prone to excess water early in the season \parencite{Mourtzinis2021}.

Several headline findings recur throughout the literature. First, yield benefits are typically larger in wet years and on poorly drained, fine-textured or naturally waterlogged soils, and they can be modest, absent, or even negative where existing drainage is already adequate or where over-drainage induces drought stress \parencite{JORJANI1991,Wiersma2010,Cordeiro2015,Kladivko2005}. Second, the response depends strongly on drain spacing, depth, and design, which mediate how quickly the water table is lowered \parencite{HARDJOAMIDJOJO1982,Skaggs2006,Rui2024,Acharya2019}. Third, in arid and semi-arid irrigated systems the yield benefit is often inseparable from salinity control and improved irrigation water-use efficiency \parencite{ELMOWELHI1988,Mathew2001,Yang2022,Liu2025}. Fourth, controlled drainage and subirrigation can retain water for the crop and deliver additional gains when precipitation is limiting \parencite{Kaur2021,Gunn2018,Gunn2019,Nelson2011}. This section synthesises these consolidated findings, the mechanisms behind them, and the quantified outcomes reported across the source studies.

\subsection{Magnitude of yield gains from subsurface drainage}

The clearest body of evidence concerns direct comparisons of drained versus undrained (or surface-drained/control) systems, which repeatedly document substantial yield increases. In a long-term Indiana experiment on Clermont soil, corn yields remained stable in the undrained control but progressively increased in all three drained treatments across 24 corn years, with the drainage effect becoming more pronounced in the last ten corn years than in the first ten; soybean yields, by contrast, were not significantly affected by drainage spacing \parencite{Rui2024}. A companion long-term Indiana dataset found whole-plot average corn yields of 9.8, 9.7, 9.5, and \(9.2\ \mathrm{Mg\ ha^{-1}}\) for the 5-, 10-, and 20-m spacings and the nondrained control, respectively, and concluded that drainage improvements are a long-term investment that may not yield benefits in every year \parencite{Kladivko2005}.

The largest relative gains are reported in heavily stressed environments. In the Nile Delta, subsurface drainage at 20-m spacing and 1.5-m depth doubled cotton and rice yields and increased wheat and clover yields by \(50\%\) over ten years relative to no subsurface drainage \parencite{ELMOWELHI1988}. In the acid saline "kari" soils of Kuttanad, India, subsurface drainage up to 30-m spacing raised average grain yield by \(1.36\ \mathrm{t\ ha^{-1}}\) (\(61\%\)) over the control, with grain yield and 100-grain weight significantly superior \parencite{Mathew2001}. In waterlogged saline clay soils of Bangladesh, drainage increased sunflower achene yield by \(30\textrm{--}64\%\) across drained treatments in two experiments, while in a third experiment the individual surface-drainage (SD) and subsoil-drainage (SSD) treatments increased yield by \(91\%\) and \(81\%\), respectively, and the combined subsoil-plus-surface drainage (SSD+SD) added only a further \(24\%\) and \(31\%\) increase relative to SD and SSD; an earlier companion study reported up to a \(95\%\) increase \parencite{Islam2025,Islam2022}. In Alberta, a shallow subsurface system on land out of production for 20 years raised barley grain yield from \(1310\ \mathrm{kg\ ha^{-1}}\) in 1977 to \(3900\ \mathrm{kg\ ha^{-1}}\) in 1978, with plant stand up \(43\%\) \parencite{BENNETT1982}.

More moderate but statistically robust gains dominate temperate rainfed systems. Soybean yield was \(8\%\) higher under artificial drainage in experiments and \(4\%\) higher in producer fields across the United States \parencite{Mourtzinis2021}, while shallow drainage in North Carolina increased corn yield by \(0.4\ \mathrm{Mg\ ha^{-1}}\) (\(4\%\), or \(6.6\%\) excluding an anomalous 2016 season) and soybean yield by \(0.46\ \mathrm{Mg\ ha^{-1}}\) (\(14.3\%\)) relative to free drainage \parencite{Watkins2024}. In Iranian subsurface-drained paddies, all drainage plots exceeded the control grain yield by \(13.4\textrm{--}31.3\%\) for rice \parencite{DarziNaftchali2017b}, and separate rice experiments reported grain yields \(22\textrm{--}66\%\) higher (2011) and \(32\textrm{--}70\%\) higher (2012) than the control, with the Bilevel treatment producing the highest yields \parencite{DarziNaftchali2014}. In China, blind-ditch and pipe drainage raised rice yield by \(8.0\%\) and \(6.0\%\) over the control, with the 3-m blind-ditch spacing producing the maximum yield of \(7.501\ \mathrm{Mg\ ha^{-1}}\) \parencite{Qi2025b}. Canola yield in Iran improved by roughly \(60\%\) under subsurface drainage relative to surface drainage, with mean grain yield higher by 697 and \(952\ \mathrm{kg\ ha^{-1}}\) in the two seasons \parencite{Asgari2021}. Cotton, sunflower, and potato experiments likewise reported increased actual yields under subsurface drainage \parencite{Yang2022,Zhang2024c,SerranoCoronel2018}.

\subsection{Controlled drainage, subirrigation, and drainage water recycling}

Managed drainage practices deliver yield benefits beyond free drainage when they retain water for crop use during dry periods. The U.S. meta-analysis quantified an \(8.0\%\) average yield increase from controlled drainage and subirrigation over conventional free drainage across 154 comparisons, drawn from 30 peer-reviewed articles and combined with fuzzy-logic mapping to identify suitable areas, including regions outside the well-studied Midwest \parencite{Singh2022b}. In Missouri, free drainage and drainage water recycling increased corn grain yield by \(20\%\) and \(26\%\), respectively, over a nondrained control averaged over 2015--2020, with recycling gains reaching \(17\%\), \(34\%\), and \(48\%\) in individual years and drainage water recycling positioned as a means to avoid drought losses in tile-drained landscapes \parencite{Kaur2021}. In North Carolina, drainage water recycling increased corn and soybean yields in all study years by \(0.52\ \mathrm{Mg\ ha^{-1}}\) (\(8\%\)) and \(0.45\ \mathrm{Mg\ ha^{-1}}\) (\(17\%\)); the soybean response was significant, subirrigation raised the groundwater table by an average of 15 cm, and the reservoir met irrigation demand in three of four seasons \parencite{Moursi2024}.

Subirrigation performance is strongly site-dependent, governed by lateral hydraulic conductivity. Across three Ohio sites, subirrigation raised the water table effectively at Fulton and Van Wert (growing-season water-table differences of 17--52 cm) and improved relative yield to \(87.3\textrm{--}93.2\%\) and \(80.4\textrm{--}90.8\%\), but had almost no hydrologic or yield effect at Defiance because of very low lateral hydraulic conductivity, where yields stayed near \(63\textrm{--}64\%\) \parencite{Gunn2018,Gunn2019}. Historic maize relative-yield responses to subirrigation ranged from essentially none at Defiance to \(26.5\%\) at Fulton, and simulations projected that subirrigation would increasingly outperform free drainage under future climate, raising its relative-yield advantage by \(2\%\), \(10\%\), and \(8\%\) at the three sites \parencite{Gunn2018}. In Minnesota, controlled drainage and subirrigation kept soil water content in optimal ranges, with the largest benefits observed in both a wet year (2013) and a dry year (2017), and subirrigation producing yields above the county average in a dry year \parencite{Niaghi2019}. In claypan-soil soybean systems, drainage plus subirrigation improved yield at both 6.1- and 12.2-m spacings with no difference between spacings, reduced interannual yield variability relative to drainage-only or nondrained controls, and allowed earlier planting where the nondrained control was delayed up to 17 days \parencite{Nelson2011}. Combining drainage water management with foliar pyraclostrobin produced synergistic soybean yield increases of up to \(1070\ \mathrm{kg\ ha^{-1}}\), with drainage-only or drainage-plus-subirrigation gains of \(18\textrm{--}22\%\) over the nondrained, nonfungicide control \parencite{Nelson2011b}. In Uzbekistan, controlled drainage managed the groundwater table so as to reduce water stress between irrigation events and increase grain yields, supporting the view that controlled drainage delivers good short-term returns through reduced water-deficiency stress \parencite{Dukhovny2018}.

\subsection{Drain spacing, depth, and system design}

Yield response is mediated by drainage intensity, set by spacing and depth. In the long-term Indiana experiment the narrowest 5-m spacing produced the highest average corn yield, followed by 10-m and 20-m spacings, with the undrained control lowest; narrower spacing also reduced the negative effect of post-planting rainfall on corn \parencite{Rui2024}. Distance-from-drain effects were more common at 20-m than at 10- or 5-m spacings, generally showing greater growth and yield near the tile \parencite{Kladivko2005}. On a Missouri claypan, the highest soybean yield occurred directly above the 6.1-m drainage tile line, all tile spacings and distances outyielded the nondrained control, and spatial yield variability was structured by distance from the tile line, suggesting narrower spacing to reduce variability in low-yielding environments \parencite{Nelson2017}.

Optimal design is soil- and rainfall-specific. Modelling for a Portsmouth sandy loam indicated that a maximum long-term relative corn yield of \(80\%\) required drain spacing of 40 m or less under good surface drainage but 30 m or less under poor surface drainage; at 100-m spacing, 25-year average relative yield fell to \(47\%\) (poor) versus \(61\%\) (good surface drainage) \parencite{HARDJOAMIDJOJO1982}. Across four soils, drain spacings that maximised returns ranged from 19 m (Normania) to 40 m (Portsmouth), with maximum returns near a drainage intensity of \(1.1\textrm{--}1.2\ \mathrm{cm\ day^{-1}}\) for most soils \parencite{Skaggs2006}. In Iowa, DRAINMOD predicted significant yield gains over undrained conditions for both Okoboji and Canisteo soils; Canisteo yields improved with closer spacing whereas Okoboji's largest improvement occurred at 18.3-m spacing \parencite{Arora2024}. In Minnesota, relative crop yield rose from \(43\%\) undrained to \(99\%\) at 23-m spacing and 135-cm depth, with profitability generally increasing with drain depth and spacing \parencite{Sands2015}. In the Canadian Prairies, however, over-drainage was a risk: a 15-m spacing could promote over-drainage and yield reductions, 40 m emerged as a threshold avoiding negative impacts on the sandy loam studied, and 60- and 90-m spacings were ineffective in wetter years \parencite{Cordeiro2015}. In North Dakota, spacing and depth effects depended on rainfall and crop: narrower (9 m) spacing gave the highest corn and soybean yields in the wet year and 3-year average, wider (15 m) spacing suited the dry year and sugarbeet, and a 1.2-m drain depth outperformed 0.9 m for sugarbeet \parencite{Acharya2019}. In China, deeper pipe burial and closer spacing generally raised yield, desalination, and irrigation water productivity, with 1.4-m burial identified as optimal in one region and D2 (45 cm) or a 45--60 cm range optimal under greenhouse conditions in another \parencite{Liu2025,Yang2022,Guo2025,Hou2018}. For sugarcane, a system able to maintain the water table between 0.3 and 1.5 m was concluded to increase cane and sugar yield \parencite{CAMP1982}.

\subsection{Mechanisms: waterlogging relief, aeration, roots, and physiology}

The proximate mechanism is relief of excess-water stress and restoration of root-zone aeration. Excess water quickly removed from the root zone provided sufficient aeration for barley growth \parencite{BENNETT1982}, and mid-season subsurface drainage lowered the water table in the rice root zone and improved aeration relative to the control \parencite{DarziNaftchali2014}. Growth-stage sensitivity matters: for cotton, the flowering and boll-forming stage was most waterlogging-sensitive and the budding stage most waterlogging-prone, and surface waterlogging caused greater yield loss than subsurface waterlogging, with combined stress less than additive \parencite{Qian2021}.

Improved aeration propagates through crop physiology and growth. Subsurface drainage increased cotton seedling emergence, root vigour, chlorophyll, plant height, leaf area, dry matter, and reproductive-organ weight, and path analysis identified seedling emergence rate as the largest direct contributor to yield—so drainage raised yield mainly by improving emergence through reduced soil salinity \parencite{Yang2022}. In sunflower, drainage decreased leaf \(\mathrm{Na^{+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Na^{+}/K^{+}}\) ratio and raised leaf \(\mathrm{K^{+}}\), stomatal conductance, and chlorophyll—all positively associated with achene yield—with early gains driven by waterlogging/salinity relief and late-season gains by improved water availability and solute potential \parencite{Islam2022}. In canola, subsurface drainage raised leaf area index, total dry weight, relative growth rate, and crop growth rate \parencite{Asgari2021}. In rice, subsurface drainage shifted biomass partitioning toward panicles, increased N uptake and translocation to reproductive tissue, and improved nutrient-use efficiency, raising NUE for grain by \(16.1\%\) and KUE by \(16\%\) \parencite{DarziNaftchali2022b}. Path analysis of blind-ditch rice systems attributed the largest direct contribution to yield to root biomass \parencite{Qi2025b}. A wheat root-trait review noted that dense adventitious roots, thin sparse roots, and aerenchyma-forming laterals are superior under waterlogging combined with other stresses, and that integrated subsurface tile-drained systems improve rhizosphere water status in poorly drained clay soils, with drainage among the agronomic strategies that relieve multiple-stress yield loss \parencite{Chen2025b}. Greenhouse and field work on root rot showed that watering intensity increased root rot and that no-drainage and shallow-drainage plots had greater root rot than conventional drainage in a wet year \parencite{Han2017}.

Drainage also raises crop water use where growth improves. In North Dakota, subsurface drainage increased peak growing-season evapotranspiration (e.g., 239 vs \(183\ \mathrm{mm}\) for July--August corn) and seasonal ET by \(16\%\) (2009) and \(7\%\) (2010), with higher crop coefficients in the drained field \parencite{Rijal2012}. Regionally, tile drainage slightly decreased annual cumulative ET (mainly early season) but drained areas showed slightly higher peak-season ET and generally higher LAI, indicating better crop growth \parencite{Yang2017,Yang2022}. Boundary-line analysis of cranberry fields identified an optimum water-table drawdown rate near \(1.25\ \mathrm{cm\ h^{-1}}\), with yield declining when drawdown deviated from that optimum, and soil hydrodynamic properties explaining nearly half the variance in drawdown and yield \parencite{Bulot2017}.

\subsection{Salinity control and reclamation in irrigated arid systems}

In arid and semi-arid irrigated agriculture, subsurface drainage improves yield largely by leaching salts and reclaiming soils. In the Nile Delta, one year of drainage reduced soil salinity in the top 1.5 m from 5.3 to \(2.2\ \mathrm{dS\ m^{-1}}\) without water beyond normal irrigation and rainfall \parencite{ELMOWELHI1988}. In Kuttanad, drainage leached sodium, calcium, and magnesium, drastically reduced chloride, lowered acidity, and homogenised soil chemistry within two years, alleviating the acidity, salinity, and cation/anion toxicity that had depressed rice yields \parencite{Mathew2001}. In Alberta, a shallow system controlled the water table (receding to pre-irrigation levels within 48 h) and reduced ECe and SAR mainly in the upper 0--0.3 m, removing \(2.2\ \mathrm{t\ ha^{-1}}\) of salt from the surface layer \parencite{BENNETT1982}. In Chinese saline systems, subsurface drainage improved root-zone desalination (\(13.54\%\) higher for the same irrigation), improved moisture uniformity, and raised sunflower and cotton yields, with deeper burial and closer spacing enhancing desalination \parencite{Zhang2024c,Yang2022,Liu2025,Guo2025}. Crop-straw drains (maize stalk, rice straw) removed salt and water more efficiently than perforated plastic pipes, improved desalination and root development, and improved maize grain yield—suggesting a low-cost organic substitute during initial reclamation of saline coastal soils \parencite{Lu2018}. Salinity management also has limits and risks: deficit irrigation in South Africa salinised upper soil layers, root-zone ECe exceeded the sugarcane threshold at all sites (implying \(>25\%\) yield reductions), and observed water-table depths and drainage discharges fell short of design, underscoring that installation alone is insufficient without maintenance \parencite{Malota2016}. Modelling for cotton showed that supplemental irrigation of \(\geq 100\ \mathrm{mm\ yr^{-1}}\) kept the root-zone salt balance favourable under a 1.2-m water-table control, whereas lower irrigation allowed salinity to accumulate \parencite{Li2018}. In Pakistan, water quality governed outcomes: good canal water maintained relative transpiration above 0.90 for wheat and cotton, whereas poor tubewell water dropped it as low as 0.60, with wheat more affected than cotton \parencite{Sarwar2001}.

\subsection{Water-use efficiency and irrigation water savings}

Controlled drainage repeatedly improves irrigation water-use efficiency and saves water without sacrificing yield. In Egypt, simple management concepts for existing subsurface systems, evaluated with DRAINMOD-S, improved irrigation water-use efficiency by \(15\textrm{--}20\%\) without yield reduction; crop-specific WUE gains were about \(16\%\) (wheat), \(23\%\) (maize), \(15\%\) (alfalfa), and \(20\%\) (cotton), with potential water savings of hundreds to over a thousand \(\mathrm{m^{3}\ ha^{-1}}\) per crop and roughly \(0.4\ \mathrm{BCM}\) over a two-year rotation in the Western Delta \parencite{Wahba2005}. A separate Egyptian study reported that controlled drainage cut applied irrigation water by \(15.6\%\) (wheat) and \(14\%\) (maize), raised water productivity to 1.03 and \(1.33\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-3}}\), increased average maize relative yield by about \(6\%\), reduced drainage discharge by \(24.5\%\), and could save roughly \(1.33\ \mathrm{BCM}\) for El-Beheira governorate without affecting soil salinity \parencite{Mahmoud2021}. Modified drainage layouts reduced applied irrigation water by about \(30\textrm{--}40\%\) while restricted outflow had no adverse effect on soil salinity or crop yield for rice, maize, and cotton \parencite{ELATFY1991}. Controlled-water-table wheat in Egypt achieved the highest crop water productivity and large water savings at \(50\%\) available-water depletion with a 60-cm water table (identified as the best management practice) \parencite{ElHassanin2022}. In Iranian paddies, subsurface drainage raised water productivity and economic water productivity, with WUE increases of up to \(0.49\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-3}}\) for one cultivar, while controlled subsurface systems increased water productivity without markedly harming irrigation application efficiency \parencite{DarziNaftchali2017b,Asgari2021}. In Pakistan, controlled subsurface drainage maintained groundwater near design depth, produced a maximum wheat yield of \(6.5\ \mathrm{t\ ha^{-1}}\) with water-use efficiency of \(0.93\ \mathrm{kg\ m^{-3}}\), and allowed large irrigation savings (23--129\% of the required depth) that could be reserved for drought \parencite{Khan2003}, while a water-conservation schedule could save up to \(25\%\) of scarce canal water \parencite{Sarwar2001}. In China, buried pipes raised irrigation water productivity over the control, highest at 1.4-m burial \parencite{Liu2025}.

\subsection{Weather dependence, timing, and earlier planting}

A consolidated finding is that drainage yield benefits scale with excess-water pressure and thus with weather. In Indiana, only the undrained control showed a significant negative corn-yield response to rainfall in the 14 days after planting, and growing-season rainfall was positively related to yield in drained systems, especially at 5-m spacing \parencite{Rui2024}. Wet years tended to favour narrower spacing while dry years favoured intermediate or wider spacings \parencite{Kladivko2005,Acharya2019}. Simulated yield reductions in the Canadian Prairies were more strongly associated with excess moisture early in the season than with late-season drought \parencite{Cordeiro2015}. For sugarcane, wet years favoured subsurface drainage whereas a dry year favoured a high, constant water table \parencite{CAMP1982}. In Japan, yield declined with the number of heavy-rain days (\(>30\ \mathrm{mm}\)) in fields without subsurface drainage but showed no such relationship where drainage was present, and mean buckwheat yield was much higher with subsurface drainage; raised-bed planting compensated for the absence of drainage except under exceptionally heavy rainfall \parencite{Takeshima2023}. Historically, comparisons on undrained versus artificially drained land showed corn yields more sensitive to precipitation on undrained land, with the largest physical yield gains from drainage on naturally poorly drained soils \parencite{JORJANI1991}.

Drainage also captures value through earlier field operations. Under producer conditions, artificial drainage was associated with sowing about 7--8 days earlier than natural drainage; combined with sowing-delay yield penalties of \(13\ \mathrm{kg\ ha^{-1}\ d^{-1}}\) (subsurface) and \(18\ \mathrm{kg\ ha^{-1}\ d^{-1}}\) (surface), one week's earlier sowing implied a \(91\textrm{--}126\ \mathrm{kg\ ha^{-1}}\) benefit \parencite{Mourtzinis2021}. Subsurface drainage similarly allowed earlier soybean planting and avoided the \(29\textrm{--}52\%\) harvest plant-population losses seen in nondrained controls in wet years \parencite{Nelson2011}.

\subsection{Modelling, design tools, and economics}

Simulation models—principally DRAINMOD—are well established for predicting drain flow, water-table depth, and relative yield, providing a validated basis for design. Reported performance is generally good: a modified DRAINMOD explained \(63\%\) of corn-yield variation over 12 years with close agreement on water-table elevations \parencite{HARDJOAMIDJOJO1982}; Indiana calibration/validation achieved Nash--Sutcliffe efficiencies of 0.70--0.85 for drain flow, with monthly flow predicted well in most plot-years and relative-yield patterns generally reproduced \parencite{Ale2009,Wang2006b}; Chinese predictions matched field water tables with a correlation of 0.97 \parencite{Li2018}; and Canadian Prairie simulations of water table and drainage outflow were rated good to excellent \parencite{Cordeiro2015}. In South Dakota, DRAINMOD reproduced measured subirrigated corn yields (representative \(R^{2}=0.53\)) and was judged the only assessment of DRAINMOD corn yields under predominantly irrigated conditions \parencite{DeBoer2006}. A simple steady-state Hooghoudt-based method gave reasonable estimates of drain depth and spacing to optimise profits, with a pooled drainage-rate regression (\(R^{2}=0.63\)) and soil-specific relationships up to \(R^{2}=0.94\) \parencite{Skaggs2006}. Detailed water-flow models (D-HYSAM) using a macroscopic root-extraction term reproduced soil-water pressure, moisture, and phreatic-surface positions in California \parencite{KARAJEH1994,KARAJEH1994b}. Design tools such as spreadsheet performance-index calculators enable multi-objective design that balances profitability against drainage volume and runoff \parencite{Sands2015}. Historically, the optimal water-table depth was recognised to depend on soil profile, crop, and permeability, and drain spacing could be indicated satisfactorily from soil permeability and root-zone conditions \parencite{HOOGHOUDT1952}.

Economically, drainage investments are frequently justified but soil- and crop-dependent. Iowa payback periods were seven years or less for all spacings, varying with soil type, spacing, and corn price \parencite{Arora2024}. Returns to drainage solely for higher corn yield were low on heavy soils (high installation cost) and highest on lighter soils, with the biggest payoff coming from enabling a shift from oats to corn on poorly drained land—so drainage is most attractive when it enables a switch from low- to high-value crops \parencite{JORJANI1991}. In Iran, installation costs were readily justified by annual increases in rice and canola yields, and subsurface drainage even enabled canola after rice (impossible under waterlogged surface drainage), raising both yield and cropping intensity \parencite{DarziNaftchali2014}. Combination subsurface irrigation-and-drainage systems in South Dakota appeared economically viable on suitable soils, with expected corn-yield increases of roughly \(40\textrm{--}100\%\) over dryland yields \parencite{DeBoer2006}. Managed strategies that minimise pumped water (e.g., continuous subirrigation from a defined growth stage with an optimised weir setting) reduced water use and labour costs while sustaining yield, enhancing the economic case for subirrigation \parencite{Ale2009,Gunn2019}.

\subsection{Agronomic side effects, integrated management, and adoption}

Beyond grain yield, drainage management affects grain quality, nutrients, and disease. Grain nutrient removal was greater under free drainage and drainage water recycling, though grain quality concentrations were largely unaffected in Missouri corn \parencite{Kaur2021}. In rice, milling recovery and protein content varied modestly across drainage treatments \parencite{DarziNaftchali2017b}. Notably, subsurface drainage that did not raise grain yield still increased grain protein: in spring wheat and soybean, protein rose with drainage coefficient even though yields were unchanged \parencite{Wiersma2010}. Foliar pyraclostrobin reduced Septoria brown spot and frogeye leaf-spot severity by \(2\textrm{--}8\%\) and interacted synergistically with drainage water management, while drainage itself did not affect disease severity \parencite{Nelson2011b}.

Drainage is increasingly embedded in integrated soil-and-water conservation. Reviews conclude that no single practice resolves difficult (e.g., duplex) soils, which require systems approaches combining tillage, rotations, ameliorants, and subsurface drainage; drainage was reported to eliminate waterlogging and deliver the largest, most reliable production gains on soils with stable subsoils \parencite{GARDNER1992}. Waterlogging-mitigation syntheses place combined drainage and crop management as the foremost step for severe waterlogging, with tolerant varieties and agronomic practices for minor cases, and the best strategy depending on severity, soil, crop, and local conditions \parencite{Manik2019}. Landscape-integrated frameworks combine tile drainage with terraces, water-retention structures, supplemental irrigation, and conservation tillage to improve field drainage while storing and reusing runoff and tile water \parencite{Li2021b}. Efficient drainage is also framed as a precondition for realising the benefits of conservation practices and improved genetics \parencite{Rui2024}, and cover crops or perennial forage can modestly reduce early-season drainage flow (e.g., a \(32\%\) May reduction under orchardgrass) with implications for flood mitigation in drained landscapes \parencite{Goeken2015,Daigh2014}. Broader land-use hydrology remains less certain, with catchment-scale flood impacts of drainage and land-use change identified as poorly understood and needing systems-thinking and connectivity-based upscaling \parencite{Rogger2017}.

Adoption evidence confirms drainage as a leading adaptive practice. Roughly three-fourths of surveyed U.S. producers reported at least some artificially drained cropland (22.6\% fully drained), and warm-season precipitation and experience with saturated soils were positive predictors of drainage adoption \parencite{Morton2015}. Finnish farmers prioritised well-functioning subsurface drainage and soil organic content as their main climate-adaptation measures—ahead of irrigation—with close to \(90\%\) rating subsurface drainage important, though many considered maintenance too expensive and called for subsidies \parencite{PeltonenSainio2021}. Japan's evolving rice-field drainage standards illustrate how design and policy co-evolve, including new uses of subsurface facilities for irrigation and ecosystem restoration \parencite{Ogino2007}.

\subsection{Boundary conditions and limits}

The evidence also delineates where drainage yields little or must be managed carefully. In the Red River of the North Basin, subsurface drainage did not improve spring wheat or soybean grain yield across drainage coefficients tested—only protein rose—because existing land leveling and surface drainage were already adequate, indicating limited applicability there \parencite{Wiersma2010}. Yield response also varies by soil texture: the meta-analysis found medium-textured soils most responsive to controlled drainage and subirrigation, with fine- and coarse-textured soils showing no clear effect, and the largest benefits under drier growing seasons in coarse and medium soils \parencite{Singh2022b}. Over-drainage can reduce yield through induced drought, as seen where 15-m spacing promoted yield loss and closer spacings and deeper weirs required careful management to avoid excessive water table lowering \parencite{Cordeiro2015,DeBoer2006}. Site hydraulic properties can nullify subirrigation benefits entirely, as at Defiance, Ohio \parencite{Gunn2018,Gunn2019}. Historic water-table experiments established the trade-off directly: yields increased as the water table was lowered from 40 to 150 cm, higher water levels produced earlier ripening and lower yield, and gains diminished at greater depths—so an optimum depth exists that varies with soil, crop, and permeability \parencite{HOOGHOUDT1952}. Finally, installation is necessary but not sufficient: without timely maintenance, observed water-table depths and drainage discharges can fall well short of design and fail to control shallow water tables \parencite{Malota2016}. Where soils are already well aerated—as in newly constructed sand rootzones—additional drainage produced no meaningful improvement in quality or rooting \parencite{Bigelow2001}. Collectively, these limits reinforce that the well-supported yield benefits of drainage are contingent on excess-water stress being present, on appropriate spacing and depth for the soil and climate, and on sustained system function.

### Material: Socio-Economic Benefits of Drainage Investment

\subsection{Overview}
Investment in agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage is well documented to generate substantial, multi-dimensional socio-economic returns, spanning higher crop yields, greater cropping intensity, increased farm and household income, higher land value, employment, reduced rural out-migration, and agro-industrial development \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007,AbdelDayem2007}. The evidence base assembled here draws on several complementary lines: (i) national and regional programme evaluations documenting yield and income gains after drainage installation \parencite{AbdelDayem2007,Gopalakrishnan2007,Datta2000,Datta2002,Ghumman2011,Wichelns2002}; (ii) formal financial analyses reporting benefit--cost ratios, internal rates of return, net present values, and payback periods \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007,Datta2000,Datta2002,Wichelns2002,Ghumman2011,ROBINSON1992,Bozic2014}; (iii) cost-effectiveness and least-cost design studies for drainage networks, evaporation basins, and disposal options \parencite{Gabr2024,Singh2001,Bakour2021,Bethune2008,HANSON1994,DINAR1989,Napierala2024,Imran2021}; (iv) analyses of how applied research, improved materials, mechanization, and capacity development modernize practice and deliver considerable savings \parencite{Ritzema2007,Ritzema2008,Ritzema2006,AbdelDayem2007,TeKrony2004,Yannopoulos2020}; and (v) regional decision-support and environmental-co-benefit assessments that frame investment and planning \parencite{Burkhalter2006,ROBINSON1992,Quinn2009,Letey1999,McDowell2021,Upadhyay2019,Ayars2015}. Headline findings recur across regions: drainage typically raises average crop yields by roughly 10--30\% and lifts net farm income, while its financial returns are generally favourable where leaching water and sustainable effluent disposal are assured \parencite{AbdelDayem2007,Datta2000,Wichelns2002}.

\subsection{Yield, income, and productivity gains}
The most consistently reported socio-economic benefit is a marked improvement in crop productivity following reclamation of waterlogged and salt-affected land. In Egypt, drainage implementation was associated with crop yield increases of 10 to 30\% on average, higher net annual farm income, and improved gross production values \parencite{AbdelDayem2007}. Using the Nile Delta example, \textcite{Wichelns2002} report that annual gross production values increase by 500 to 550 per hectare in both saline and non-saline areas after drainage installation, that annual net farm incomes rise by 375, 200, and 180 per hectare in non-saline, saline, and rehabilitation areas respectively, and that yield improvement accrues progressively---50\% of the potential improvement in the first year, rising to 75\% in year two, 90\% in year three, and 100\% in year four.

In India, drainage provisions in irrigated areas were reported to deliver many positive socio-economic impacts, including increased crop yields, higher cropping intensity, greater employment, reduced migration to urban areas, higher land value, and development of agro-industries \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}. For the Uttar Pradesh Land Reclamation Project, more than 175{,}000 ha of unproductive sodic land were reclaimed, of which over 110{,}000 ha were brought under cultivation; annual farm income increased by over 150\%, cropping intensity rose from 49\% to over 200\%, and annual household income increased by 53\% \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}. Small-scale pilot projects in Haryana produced a considerable increase in cropping intensity, a shift toward more remunerative crops, and a remarkable rise in crop yields and farm incomes \parencite{Datta2002}. At Ismila, drainage sharply reduced fallow land and increased annual cropping intensity relative to both the pre-drainage condition and the non-affected control, with wheat yields increasing by about 50\% at Bhana-Brahamana-I and even more strongly at Ujhana \parencite{Datta2002}; in the same project wheat yields in drained fields rose by more than 200\% over 1986--87 to 1992--93, as formerly fallow land was cultivated and barley was replaced by more remunerative wheat in the rabi season \parencite{Datta2000}. This yield response tracks rapid desalinization: at Sampla farm topsoil salinity declined from about 50 dS\,m$^{-1}$ in June 1984 to about 5 dS\,m$^{-1}$ by November 1985 \parencite{Datta2002}, consistent with the fall from about 50 to about 5 dS\,m$^{-1}$ between 1984 and 1991 reported by \textcite{Datta2000}. For the Haryana Operational Pilot Project, subsurface drainage on 1946 ha raised cropping intensity from 140\% in 1991--92 to 185\% in 1995--96 \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}.

Long-term subsurface drainage also improves the soil resource base that underpins these gains. In waterlogged saline Vertisols, drainage reduced salinity and improved multiple physico-chemical and biological properties, raising organic carbon, soil organic carbon stock, dehydrogenase and alkaline phosphatase activity, bulk density, and saturated hydraulic conductivity, with sugarcane yield increasing substantially and positively associated with the soil quality index \parencite{Mukhopadhyay2023}. Across three sites, drainage reduced bulk density from 1.58 to 1.52 Mg\,m$^{-3}$ and ECe from 9.72 to 2.18 dS\,m$^{-1}$, increased saturated hydraulic conductivity from 3.19 to 5.07 cm\,day$^{-1}$, soil organic carbon from 0.22 to 0.34\%, dehydrogenase from 15.44 to 31.65 $\mu$g\,g$^{-1}$\,24\,h$^{-1}$, and alkaline phosphatase from 16.86 to 40.11 $\mu$g\,P-NP\,g$^{-1}$\,h$^{-1}$ \parencite{Mukhopadhyay2023b}. Surface ECe (0--30 cm) fell to 4.23, 1.00, and 1.31 dS\,m$^{-1}$ at Kahni, Siwana Mal, and Jagsi under drainage, versus 13.86, 7.13, and 8.17 dS\,m$^{-1}$ in the controls, and the soil quality index improved by 76\%, 136\%, and 114\% respectively (overall 102\% above the control value of 0.34) \parencite{Mukhopadhyay2023b}. Rice--wheat system yield (rice-equivalent) rose from 1.89 to 8.01, 1.55 to 8.77, and 0.88 to 6.72 Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ across these sites, corresponding to increases of 328\%, 465\%, and 665\% \parencite{Mukhopadhyay2023b}. These gains carry additional carbon co-benefits: soil organic carbon stock increased from 36.49 to 47.91 Mg\,ha$^{-1}$ (0--90 cm), carbon sequestration potential reached up to 4.43 Mg\,C\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ at Kahni, and the authors position drainage as supporting land degradation neutrality and the Sustainable Development Goals \parencite{Mukhopadhyay2023b,Mukhopadhyay2023}.

Broader socio-economic uplift is also documented in Pakistan, where a drainage project lowered the groundwater table by an average of 40\% between the pre-project (1996) and post-project (2006) years, raised annual cropping intensity from 102\% to 157\% (a net 55\% increase), and increased the socio-economic standard of living of the project population by 30\% \parencite{Ghumman2011}. At the aggregate scale, \textcite{Valipour2014} notes that in India, despite less than 10\% of cultivated area being drained, salinity has not increased while waterlogging has decreased by more than 70\%, and in Australia limited drainage nonetheless coincides with low salinity and waterlogging.

\subsection{Financial and economic returns}
Formal appraisals confirm that these physical gains translate into favourable financial performance. For the RAJAD project in India, subsurface drainage was installed on 14{,}925 ha using over 3.04 million metres of locally produced corrugated perforated PVC pipe; the financial analysis reported a cost--benefit ratio of 1.28 to 2.87, a net present worth of 12{,}780 to 66{,}170 Rs\,ha$^{-1}$, a payback period of 4.5--6.9 years, and an internal rate of return of 18--35\% \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}. The Ismila case provides especially strong evidence because investment costs and benefits over the first seven years were measured and the remainder assessed conservatively \parencite{Datta2000}: it yielded an internal rate of return of 8\% with actual historical costs and 9\% under an alternative benefit scenario \parencite{Datta2000,Datta2002}, rising to almost 10\% and 10.5\%, and 10.5\% and 11.5\%, under present and future investment levels \parencite{Datta2000}. Constructing farm ponds to temporarily store drainage effluent on 5\% of the farm area would reduce the internal rate of return by 1.5\% \parencite{Datta2000}. The Ismila results are presented as hard evidence of the financial feasibility of small-scale, manually installed subsurface drainage for reclaiming salt-affected areas, provided sufficient water is available for leaching and irrigation and effluent can be disposed of sustainably \parencite{Datta2000}.

From both farm-level and public perspectives, returns to Nile Delta drainage investment appear substantial: the present value of enhanced net returns is 2895 per hectare in non-saline areas and 1544 per hectare in saline areas, against a present value of annual payments of 280 per hectare, giving farm-level benefit--cost ratios of 10.3 and 5.5 respectively \parencite{Wichelns2002}. Not all appraisals are uniformly high; in the Pakistan FDP case the benefit per hectare recovered in 10 years was only 17\% of the investment, or 1.7\% per year, even though cropping intensity and living standards rose markedly \parencite{Ghumman2011}. At the project-planning scale, applying the Agricultural Benefit Evaluation model to Cameron County, Texas, indicated substantial agricultural benefits: the present value of project benefits was 83.1 million dollars at the 50\% flood-frequency protection level, rising to 88.2 and 90.9 million dollars at the 20\% and 10\% levels; benefits from lowering the water table alone were about 75 million dollars, with flood-protection benefits of about 8, 13, and 16 million dollars \parencite{ROBINSON1992}. County-wide agricultural gross returns increased by about 23--24.5 million dollars, and for the 20\% protection level the projected regional impacts included a 43.4 million-dollar increase in household income, a 55.4 million-dollar increase in regional economic activity, and over 46 new jobs \parencite{ROBINSON1992}. In Serbia, MODFLOW combined with benefit--cost analysis served as a decision-making tool: any project phase could be accepted, but Phase 5 yielded the greatest benefit-to-cost multiple, and complex protection of Pancevacki Rit demanded high investment yet gave the lowest unit costs and best benefit--cost ratio while reducing the threatened area by up to 40\% \parencite{Bozic2014}.

\subsection{Scale of national and regional drainage programs}
The socio-economic importance of drainage is reflected in the scale of national programmes. In Egypt, drainage expansion became a large national programme, with improved main drainage serving about 2.4 million hectares, subsurface pipe drainage about 1.9 million hectares, and rehabilitated systems about 310{,}000 hectares \parencite{AbdelDayem2007}; the achievements of the Egyptian Public Authority for Drainage Projects are described as a success story, reflecting continuous commitment to economic growth, livelihood and poverty reduction, and environmental challenges \parencite{AbdelDayem2007}. India faced roughly 4.5 million ha of waterlogged and around 6.73 million ha of salt-affected area, of which about 1.39 million ha were reported reclaimed countrywide (1.3 million ha sodic, 0.04 million ha saline-sodic, 0.05 million ha waterlogged), and under the Centrally Sponsored CAD Programme about 57{,}123 ha from 446 schemes in nine states were approved, of which 44{,}135 ha were reclaimed by 2004 \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}. In Maharashtra, two pilot schemes covered 2000 ha and about 200 ha of subsurface drainage had been installed by farmers on their own without government support \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}. Field experiences from the UP Land Reclamation Project, HOPP, and RAJAD collectively demonstrate that the technology can be successfully adopted to reclaim saline and waterlogged lands in India \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}. In the United States, Reclamation constructed over 20{,}930 km of surface and subsurface drains in its first century to protect an estimated 400{,}000 ha of irrigated land from waterlogging and salinity \parencite{TeKrony2004}. Globally, the percent of total cultivated area drained is 12\% and 17\% based on FAO (2013) and IDD (2013) data respectively, with the USA reporting about 32\% drainage together with low salinity \parencite{Valipour2014}.

\subsection{Cost-effectiveness of design and technology choices}
A large body of work shows that careful design and site selection can sharply reduce the cost of achieving drainage benefits. For subsurface drainage networks in the old lands of the Nile Delta, \textcite{Gabr2024} developed novel design charts spanning lateral diameters of 2.5--12.5 cm, hydraulic conductivities of 0.5--4 m\,day$^{-1}$, lateral lengths of 100--300 m, a constant 1000 m collector, recharge of 1--5 mm\,day$^{-1}$, and excavation depths of 1.5--2.0 m. Minimum network costs at a 300 m lateral length were 205--374 per hectare across diameters for baseline conditions, rising to 274--489, 420--717, and 598--984 per hectare under higher recharge and lower conductivity combinations \parencite{Gabr2024}; critically, the maximum lateral length of 300 m decreased cost by 37\% relative to 100 m, and the charts give lower overall costs than conventional design approaches \parencite{Gabr2024}. In Syria, an optimization model coded in MATLAB with an interior-point algorithm found vertical drainage economically better than horizontal drainage for the case study, with the optimum favouring the largest possible spacing between pipes and wells (the dominant cost factor) and the minimum feasible pumping duration over the project lifespan \parencite{Bakour2021}.

Disposal infrastructure follows similar economies of scale. For evaporation basins in Australia, cost per hectare and per Ml of storage declined with increasing basin size, with earthworks, geotechnical investigation, and interception of lateral leakage as the major cost constituents \parencite{Singh2001}. Full geotechnical assessment increased net present cost by about 8--10\% over minimum investigation, while choosing a site that avoids leakage control can roughly halve the cost of a large basin; square or rectangular basins outperformed triangular ones, greater cell size and bank height reduced unit cost, and pipe drains were cheaper than open drains for smaller basins \parencite{Singh2001}. Site selection, basin geometry, and leakage control are therefore identified as critical to cost minimization \parencite{Singh2001}. Comparing drainage-management options in the Shepparton region, conversion from border-check to sprinkler irrigation had the lowest net present cost and, together with tubewell drainage with farm reuse, produced positive financial outcomes, whereas tubewell or horizontal pipe drainage discharging to evaporation basins were much more costly and became still more expensive as deep percolation increased \parencite{Bethune2008}.

Field-level water management also modulates drainage volumes and costs. Reducing field length and irrigation set time was the most effective surface-irrigation upgrade for drainage reduction, in one example lowering subsurface drainage from 63 to 25 mm in clay loam and from 84 to 20 mm in sandy loam, while increasing furrow flow rate had only limited benefit and surge irrigation reduced drainage by about 30--40\% \parencite{HANSON1994}. Economic outcomes were strongly site-specific: traditional furrow irrigation was often more profitable when disposal costs were zero, but higher disposal costs favoured LEPA, linear-move, or drip systems, with break-even disposal costs of 263--1{,}042 \$/ha-m for the shift from traditional to improved furrow and 3{,}852--32{,}470 \$/ha-m for the shift to drip \parencite{HANSON1994}. Technology can also lower the cost of active water-table control: a new flashboard-riser design cut total operation time from 701--982 s to 136--144 s, sped up stop-log handling five- to seven-fold, reduced total working time by 16--23\%, and had the lowest annual amortization, giving an average controlled-drainage implementation cost of 12.93 per year per hectare \parencite{Napierala2024}. Redesigning the Paharang system to allow continuous gravity discharge of subsurface effluent---by lowering channel bed levels (11 of 24 reaches by about 3 m or more) and requiring 1435 and 1030 m$^{3}$ of earthwork for the main and branch drains---was intended to reduce dependence on pumping, electricity, labour, and maintenance and to make budget, theft, and law-and-order constraints irrelevant \parencite{Imran2021}. Modernization of Egypt's systems is likewise motivated by high maintenance costs and limited lifespan, with controlled drainage reducing over-drainage and improving irrigation efficiency, and switching from gravity to pumped drainage cutting open-drain size and operation-and-maintenance costs while supporting reuse \parencite{Ritzema2023}.

\subsection{Materials, mechanization, and modernization}
Technological change has been a persistent driver of lower drainage costs and higher quality. Since the 1950s, and especially since the 1960s, revolutionary installation equipment, materials, and techniques improved drainage-system quality and durability, with corrugated plastic pipes becoming the preferred standard and factory pre-wrapping of synthetic envelopes improving quality control while reducing installation costs by roughly 50\% \parencite{Yannopoulos2020}. The shift from manual to mechanized subsurface drainage required new materials, quality-control systems, and training: plastic PVC/PE pipes and synthetic envelopes lowered transportation and installation costs and improved construction quality relative to clay or concrete pipes and organic or gravel envelopes, while total quality-control systems and laser grade control maintained installation quality at high speed \parencite{Ritzema2006}. In Egypt, research and development led to adoption of corrugated PVC and HDPE pipes, synthetic envelopes, and region-specific design criteria, and trenchless drainage machines improved construction productivity and reduced installation costs relative to trencher machines \parencite{AbdelDayem2007}. New installation materials and methods---plastic pipes, laser grade control, synthetic envelopes, trenchless drainage, and improved quality control---together increased installation rates and reduced installation costs, while improved operation and maintenance, including modified layouts for rice areas and farmer participation, reduced maintenance and labour costs \parencite{Ritzema2007}. Over its first century, Reclamation's drainage practice evolved from an art into a science that removed most technical unknowns, with the development of reliable design drain spacing cited as probably its most significant contribution to the international knowledge base \parencite{TeKrony2004}.

\subsection{Applied research and capacity development as ``value for money''}
A distinct and strongly supported conclusion is that linking applied research and capacity development to drainage implementation is highly cost-effective. Applied research on drainage in developing countries was reported to have ``value for money,'' with benefits that easily outweigh costs when research is linked to implementation \parencite{Ritzema2007}. Over 40 years of partnership among institutions in Egypt, Pakistan, and India with Alterra-ILRI led to modernization of practice and considerable cost savings, and to some of the largest, most modern, and most effective subsurface drainage programmes \parencite{Ritzema2007}. Several concrete savings are documented: measuring soil salinity with the EM38 lowered costs and improved data quality relative to traditional sampling and laboratory analysis; a three-step performance assessment ensured rehabilitation was initiated only when confirmed necessary, producing considerable savings; interceptor drains were shown not to justify their large investment and their unnecessary implementation was prevented; cancelling negligibly beneficial canal lining saved substantial investment; groundwater modelling showed subsurface drainage was needed in only part of a project area and allowed reduced design discharge; and overly conservative design discharge rates and drain depths were reduced, lowering installation costs and effluent salt loads \parencite{Ritzema2007}. Controlled drainage was reported to maintain or increase yields while raising irrigation water-use efficiency and reducing downstream impacts, and modified layouts for rice areas cut irrigation water use, pumping costs, and design discharge \parencite{Ritzema2007}.

The complementary capacity-development literature reaches the same verdict. Five examples show that capacity development is strengthened when research, education and training, and advisory services are applied in an integrated manner to link tacit and explicit knowledge, supported by recurrent tailor-made courses and a publication series of more than 50 titles \parencite{Ritzema2008}. This process helped Egypt develop one of the largest and most modern subsurface drainage programmes in the world, produced site-specific recommendations for different agro-climatic regions in India, and shifted the focus toward farmers' participation in Pakistan \parencite{Ritzema2008,Ritzema2006}. Capacity development is characterized as a dynamic, long-term process that builds mutual trust through repeated engagement, and the combination of capacity development with applied research and application is explicitly judged ``value for money,'' having modernized practice and achieved considerable savings through new methods of investigation, design, planning, installation, and operation and maintenance \parencite{Ritzema2008}. In-service training and the Drainage Training Centre contributed substantially to mechanized implementation in Egypt \parencite{Ritzema2006}.

\subsection{Economic instruments and policy dimensions}
Because drainage water carries disposal costs and externalities, its economics interact with policy design. In California, under an unregulated policy applied water and drainage volumes exceeded economically efficient levels (for example, 138 cm applied water and 67 cm drainage at one price combination), whereas a direct drainage fee induced efficient volumes---at CUC = 72, Pw = 1, and Pd = 3 US\$/ha-cm the efficient solution was 97 cm applied water and 29 cm drainage, with a society net benefit of 1132 US\$/ha \parencite{DINAR1989}. A flat fee on irrigation water could induce efficient applications but generated excess revenues (361 US\$/ha in one case), while tiered pricing induced efficiency with deficient revenues (32 US\$/ha); results were highly sensitive to irrigation uniformity, justifying investment in uniformity-improving technology, especially as disposal costs rise \parencite{DINAR1989}. The San Joaquin Valley experience underscores that economics alone do not govern outcomes: at Kesterson Reservoir, selenium from drainage water caused very high bird mortality and deformities (20\% of nests with deformed birds, about 40\% of eggs with dead embryos), and subsequent drainage management was shaped by political and social constraints, leading to the central conclusion that factors other than scientific facts bear on decisions and that successful management plans must be technically sound, economically viable, and socially acceptable \parencite{Letey1999}.

\subsection{Regional benefit assessment, decision support, and co-benefits}
Finally, a set of regional-scale and modelling studies frames drainage investment within basin-wide productivity and environmental accounting. In Colorado, regional solution alternatives were predicted to substantially improve waterlogging and salinity, with combination alternatives producing the largest benefits: a maximum average water-table deepening of 1.93 m, a maximum average soil-salinity decrease of 950 mg/L, salt-load reductions to the river of about 30\% and 20\% for recharge and seepage reduction individually (reaching roughly 40--45\% for combinations), and relative crop-yield increases of up to 9.6 percentage points \parencite{Burkhalter2006}. Major benefits were expected as increased agricultural productivity, enhanced river water quality through reduced salt loading, and in some cases water conservation, with combined interventions producing the greatest gains \parencite{Burkhalter2006}. The Agricultural Benefit Evaluation model was proposed as an effective tool for estimating benefits and economic impacts while handling data inadequacy and post-treatment changes in productivity and crop distribution \parencite{ROBINSON1992}. Environmental co-benefits are also quantified: the Grasslands Bypass Project achieved average selenium-load reductions of 60\% relative to pre-project means over nine years and is described as one of the most successful non-point-source management initiatives undertaken by the US Bureau of Reclamation, with real-time monitoring and adaptive management as key contributors \parencite{Quinn2009}. At national scale in New Zealand, established mitigation actions could reduce N and P loads by 16\% and 23\% (larger on dairy land), and developing actions could reduce pastoral N, P, and sediment loads by 34\%, 39\%, and 66\% by 2035, informing rural-sector investment and planning \parencite{McDowell2021}. However, artificial drainage shifts diffuse phosphorus load from overland to subsurface flow, so a Bayesian-network analysis found only a modest net reduction in total phosphorus loss relative to an undrained farm \parencite{Lucci2014}, and tile drainage is identified as a connectivity pathway that can transport phosphorus carried downward by preferential (crack and burrow) flow to lake tributaries \parencite{Allaire2011,Dadfar2010}. Land-management scenario modelling of pothole wetlands provides a policy-support tool for alternative management and investment decisions \parencite{Upadhyay2019}.

Synthesizing across these strands, the design and operation of drainage systems increasingly must reconcile agronomic production with environmental values: drainage water quality is advanced as a necessary design criterion, and active management of groundwater-table position and discharge, improved field characterization, and decision-support systems are identified as key future directions for sustainable, cost-effective drainage \parencite{Ayars2015}. Deeper drains and wider spacing raise drain-water salinity, flow, and salt load, so system design itself carries downstream economic and environmental consequences that modern, decentralized, location-specific approaches seek to manage \parencite{Yannopoulos2020,Ritzema2023}. Taken together, the evidence establishes that well-designed subsurface drainage investment, supported by applied research, appropriate materials, and capacity development, reliably delivers strong agronomic, financial, and social returns while its net benefits are maximized when disposal costs, water-use efficiency, and environmental externalities are explicitly incorporated into design and appraisal.
