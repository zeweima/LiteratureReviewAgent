### Material: Agricultural Contaminant Loss via Tile Drainage

\subsection{Overview}

Agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage is one of the most consequential
anthropogenic modifiers of the field-to-stream pathway for nitrogen (N),
phosphorus (P), agrochemicals, and microbial contaminants. A large,
geographically diverse body of evidence---continuously monitored field
plots, drainage-district and watershed outlets, isotopic and gaseous
tracer studies, plot lysimeters, soil micromorphology, dye and
smoke-injection experiments, and process-based and data-driven
models---now supports a well-consolidated picture of what drainage
controls and how strongly. Several conclusions recur across settings and
methods. First, in artificially drained cropland the subsurface pathway is
the dominant hydrologic and nitrogen-loss route: it typically carries the
majority of runoff and the great majority of total-N export, with nitrate
the overwhelmingly dominant species and its transport tightly coupled to
discharge \parencite{Bechmann2014,Raymond2012,Williams2015,Grose2022}.
Second, phosphorus, though smaller in mass, moves through both surface and
subsurface routes at concentrations well above eutrophication thresholds,
and subsurface drainage---long assumed ``clean'' for P---frequently
dominates edge-of-field P loads, often as particulate P delivered by
preferential flow \parencite{Pease2018,VanEsbroeck2016,Uusitalo2001,Laubel1999}.
Third, a small volume of continuous macropores---earthworm burrows,
shrinkage cracks, root channels, and installation scars---governs the rapid
delivery of water and surface-applied solutes to drains, and sustained
drainage measurably reorganises soil structure, aeration, and pedogenesis
\parencite{Shipitalo2004,Villholth1998,Montagne2008}. Fourth, losses are
governed first by hydrology: annual and event loads scale with drainage
flow, so most export occurs during high-flow periods of the non-growing
season \parencite{Kladivko2021,Ikenberry2014,King2016}. Fifth, a suite of
mitigation strategies---drainage water management, edge-of-field wetlands,
bioreactors and buffers, and in-field cover crops and nutrient
management---produces quantified, mechanistically coherent reductions,
albeit with scale attenuation and N--P trade-offs
\parencite{Skaggs2010,Wu2025,Tanner2005,Jaynes2008,Hanrahan2018}. Finally,
process models reproduce drainage flow and nitrate well after calibration
and are established tools for scenario evaluation
\parencite{Youssef2018,Negm2014,Moriasi2013}. This section synthesises the
magnitudes, mechanisms, and management responses that the literature has
established, together with the pesticide, microbial, trace-element,
salinity, greenhouse-gas, and ecological dimensions of drainage water
quality.

\subsection{Subsurface Drainage as the Dominant Nitrogen Pathway}

\subsubsection{Pathway dominance}

Multiple independent studies establish that in tile-drained landscapes the
subsurface pathway carries most of the nitrogen. In intensively monitored
Norwegian catchments, subsurface drainage carried $76$--$91\%$ of runoff
and $87$--$95\%$ of total-N loss, with drainage total-N concentrations
$2$--$4$ times those in surface runoff, over $90\%$ of TN loss occurring
via drains, and nitrate-N constituting $71$--$85\%$ of TN
\parencite{Bechmann2014}. New Zealand mole-pipe systems captured about
$62\%$ of surplus rainfall as drainage and routed roughly $80\%$ of soluble
N, while surface runoff dominated P, sediment, and \emph{E.\ coli}
\parencite{Monaghan2016}. At watershed scale, tile drainage accounted for
$56\%$ of annual discharge and $44$--$82\%$ (mean $62\%$) of watershed
NO$_3$-N export in Ohio \parencite{Williams2015}, averaged $57\%$ of
streamflow and dominated nitrate export in Indiana \parencite{Grose2022},
and in Iowa the largest drains contributed $70$--$87\%$ of exported water
and nitrate \parencite{Coupe2020}. In flat Illinois terrain, subsurface
stations lost $13$--$30$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ of nitrate-N
($16$--$26\%$ of applied N) with more than $70\%$ of samples exceeding the
$10$~mg~L$^{-1}$ maximum contaminant level (MCL), while surface runoff was
negligible \parencite{Dattamudi2020}.

\subsubsection{Loads, concentrations, and variability}

Reported magnitudes span a wide but coherent range. Field- and
watershed-scale nitrate-N yields commonly reach or exceed
$20$--$60$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ in maize--soybean systems
\parencite{Gentry2009,Gentry2014,Dattamudi2020,Tomer2008}, and tile
concentrations frequently approach or exceed the $10$~mg~L$^{-1}$
drinking-water limit. In three Iowa drainage districts, tile water averaged
about $13$~mg~L$^{-1}$ NO$_3$-N and exceeded the standard $85$--$90\%$ of
the time \parencite{Schilling2012b}, and across three catchments the
flow-weighted mean was $17.1$~mg~L$^{-1}$ with $74.2\%$ of samples above the
MCL \parencite{Ikenberry2014}. New Zealand tile drains exported
$14$--$109$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ TN ($58$--$90\%$ nitrate)
\parencite{Tanner2011}; Illinois riverine and tile yields averaged
$30$--$32$ and ranged to $57$--$59$~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$
\parencite{Gentry2014}; and very high concentrations characterise some
European and irrigated systems, with Italian drainage averaging
$94$--$216$~mg~NO$_3$~L$^{-1}$ \parencite{ROSSI1991} and Polish water
usually exceeding $70$~mg~L$^{-1}$ \parencite{Dragon2016}. A key
methodological finding is that interannual variability in load greatly
exceeds that of concentration: across 29 Iowa watersheds, flow-weighted
concentration ($0.6$--$15.5$~mg~L$^{-1}$) served as a more robust indicator
of management change than load or yield \parencite{Danalatos2022}. Early
Iowa work already characterised nitrate loss as ``highly variable and
strongly dependent on the amount of water lost'' \parencite{BAKER1975}, and
long-term records document real declines: German drainage N discharge fell
from $33$ to $10$~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ between the 1980s and 1990s,
driven mainly by reduced N surplus \parencite{Hirt2005b}.

\subsubsection{Fertilizer input and hydrology as joint drivers}

At regional scale, fertilizer input and watershed hydrology jointly
regulate export. About $34\%$ of applied fertilizer N was exported to
streams in the Mississippi basin, wetter watersheds exported a larger
fraction, and tile drainage acted as a hydrologic modifier that increases
throughput and shortens residence time, raising nitrate loss to the Gulf of
Mexico \parencite{Raymond2012}. Direct comparison confirmed the causal
link: nitrate load from an unfertilized Minnesota field was $58\%$ of that
from a fertilized field \parencite{Ghane2016b}. Multivariate models
combining water yield, prior corn yield, and fertilizer sales explained
$96\%$ of annual nitrate-yield variation, and shifting fertilizer to spring
was estimated to reduce nitrate yield $17$--$20\%$ \parencite{Gentry2014}.
Losses stem partly from asynchrony between mineralization and crop uptake
and from large mineralizable soil-N pools, so land-management effects may
only become visible after years to decades owing to soil-N legacies
\parencite{Cambardella1999,Bauwe2020}.

\subsection{Hydrologic Control of Nitrate Export}

\subsubsection{Flow--load coupling, high flows, and seasonality}

The single most robust cross-scale finding is that nitrate load is
controlled by drainage discharge. Annual nitrate-N loss related strongly to
annual flow, with differences among drain spacings explained by flow
\parencite{Kladivko2021,Bauwe2020}; in Italy NO$_3$ loss correlated with
drainage water ($r=0.93$) but only weakly with precipitation ($r=0.45$)
\parencite{ROSSI1991}. High flows dominate export: more than half of Iowa
catchment nitrate export occurred during the highest $10\%$ of daily flows
\parencite{Ikenberry2014}, and $79$--$95\%$ of Indiana NO$_3$-N export
occurred at the 60th--100th flow percentile \parencite{Speir2021}. Because
loads track flow they are strongly seasonal: about $70\%$ of annual drainage
and nitrate load occurred in the November--April fallow season in Indiana
\parencite{Kladivko2021,Kladivko2004}, and $81\%$ of Ohio tile discharge
occurred in the nongrowing season so nitrate and TN loads were greater then
despite higher growing-season concentrations under corn
\parencite{King2016}. Short episodes can dominate: $21$~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ was
lost in only $11$ days during snowmelt in Japan \parencite{Hayashi1999}.

\subsubsection{Concentration--discharge behaviour and rapid response}

High-frequency records reveal characteristic concentration--discharge (C--Q)
signatures. Annual nitrate export is largely chemostatic---yields rise
proportionately with runoff---and persistent chemostasis has been documented
in Illinois and Ohio tile drainage
\parencite{Speir2021,Hu2024,Williams2015}. Storm-event hysteresis is
predominantly counterclockwise ($77\%$ of Indiana storms; $>75\%$ of a
long-term Ohio record), indicating dominance of distal nitrate sources
during delivery \parencite{Speir2021,Liu2022b}. Analysis concluded that
intensive subsurface drainage, rather than deep groundwater, was the
dominant factor shaping nitrate C--Q relationships
\parencite{Liu2022b}. Tile nitrate responds within hours of water inputs:
after irrigation it rose sharply within hours then dropped abruptly, with
preferential flow intercepted by the drain originating close to and above
the tile \parencite{Mohanty1998}, and bypass (macropore) flow carried most
leached N in Japan \parencite{Hayashi1999}. Threshold behaviour is common:
tile discharge began once soil-moisture deficit exceeded $-17\pm3$~mm and
the water table rose above $0.84\pm0.03$~m \parencite{Williams2022}, and
antecedent moisture modulated discharge and solute pulses
\parencite{Trentman2020,Kennedy2012}.

\subsubsection{Sources, transformation, and denitrification}

Dual-isotope studies indicate that drainage nitrate is often derived largely
from organic sources (manure and soil organic matter) via nitrification,
with denitrification not always dominant. In Ontario, $\delta^{15}$N and
$\delta^{18}$O relationships lacked the 2:1 denitrification slope, pointing
to organic sources and continued nitrification through all seasons
\parencite{Smith2011,Smith2011b}, and rapid nitrification of applied
ammonium ($>90\%$ within 72~h) supplies ready nitrate for leaching
\parencite{Hu2024}. Where denitrification is active it is detectable:
along Polish flow paths nitrate declined while sulfate, excess N$_2$,
$\delta^{15}$N, and $\delta^{18}$O increased \parencite{Dragon2016}, and
nested French watersheds implied $34$--$82\%$ nitrate retention dominated by
underground denitrification \parencite{Billy2011,Billy2013}. In Iowa
drainageways, denitrification in slowly permeable soils could remove
essentially all upland groundwater nitrate, functioning as a natural
treatment system \parencite{Schilling2007,Cambardella1999}. Tile discharge
is dominated by pre-event water---three-component separation gave $65\%$
groundwater and $29\%$ shallow soil water---yet tile drains carried the
highest NO$_3$-N of any source and contributed over $99\%$ of watershed
nitrate export \parencite{Williams2022,Grose2022}.

\subsection{Phosphorus Loss Pathways and Fractions}

\subsubsection{Water and phosphorus partitioning}

Tile drainage commonly captures the largest share of field water export:
subsurface discharge accounted for $81\pm23\%$ of annual discharge in the
Eastern Corn Belt \parencite{Pease2018} and $78$--$90\%$ in Ontario
\parencite{VanEsbroeck2016}, and installing drainage re-routes water from
surface- to drainage-dominated flow \parencite{Turtola1995,BENGTSON1995}. A
recurring distinction is that surface runoff usually carries higher P
\emph{concentrations} whereas tile drainage---carrying more water---can
dominate annual \emph{loads}. In Ohio, flow-weighted DRP and TP
concentrations were greater in surface runoff, yet subsurface discharge
supplied $71\%$ of DRP and $69\%$ of TP load \parencite{Pease2018}; the same
contrast appears in long-term Swedish monitoring \parencite{Norberg2022}.
Tiles exported $40$--$77\%$ of TP and $19$--$67\%$ of DRP load in Ontario
\parencite{VanEsbroeck2016}, $21$--$52\%$ of TP in Wisconsin
\parencite{Madison2014}, and $65.4\%$ of annual TP at a southern-Ontario
field \parencite{Qi2018}. Models corroborate the partitioning, attributing
$66\%$ of DRP and $76\%$ of TP loss to subsurface drainage against observed
$70\%$ and $80\%$ \parencite{Grewal2025,Sadhukhan2019b}. Tile dominance is
not universal: in snowmelt-affected clay landscapes of the northern Great
Plains, overland flow contributed $72$--$89\%$ of runoff and $>95\%$ of SRP
and TP because tiles were decoupled from the surface by frozen ground
\parencite{Kokulan2019b,Kokulan2021}, and surface runoff prevailed under
some tillage, pasture, and flooded-paddy systems
\parencite{Zhao2001,Melland2008,DarziNaftchali2016}.

\subsubsection{Particulate versus dissolved phosphorus and bioavailability}

A central finding is that particulate P (PP) is frequently the dominant form
in tile drainage, contrary to the earlier presumption that the soil profile
filters subsurface water to dissolved-dominated. In Finnish clays PP was the
grand-mean $92\%$ of TP in both runoff and drainage \parencite{Uusitalo2001};
at Rothamsted's Broadbalk drains PP averaged $54\%$ of TP and was strongly
related to particulate matter \parencite{Lu2003,Lu2004}; and PP dominated
catchment losses in Denmark and elsewhere
\parencite{Grant1996,Brendel2019,vanderSalm2012}. Dissolved forms
nonetheless dominate in snowmelt-driven, grassland, high-STP, and
organic-soil systems: tile SRP averaged $60\%$ of TP across Michigan sites
\parencite{Clement2017}, and DRP dominated fertilised continuous-grass losses
\parencite{Zhang2015b}. Scottish work showed molybdate-reactive P was only
$42\%$ of subsurface TP loss, underscoring the need to measure all P forms
\parencite{Hooda1999}. Because algal availability, not total mass, governs
eutrophication risk, bioavailable-P estimates matter: desorbable PP and DRP
were of equal importance to algal-available P in Finnish clays
\parencite{Uusitalo2001,Uusitalo2003}, and only $8$--$18\%$ of grassland
lysimeter TP was molybdate-reactive \parencite{Bilotta2008}.

\subsection{Soil Controls on Phosphorus Release}

Dissolved P loss is tightly coupled to soil P status. Loss in surface runoff
and drainage related strongly to soil P content
($r^2 = 0.85$--$0.96$ for surface, $0.86$ for subsurface)
\parencite{Sharpley1995}, and higher soil-test P (STP) raised DRP in both
tiles and runoff \parencite{Osterholz2020}. Threshold behaviour is common: a
change point in STP marks where extractable P and DRP rise more steeply
\parencite{McDowell2001b}, and rainfall sharply increased subsurface DRP
where STP exceeded $41$~mg~kg$^{-1}$ \parencite{Ni2020}. Where soils sorb P
weakly, dissolved losses rise: A-horizon degree of P saturation (DPS) alone
explained $83\%$ of tile TP variance, rising to $95$--$96\%$ with
water-extractable P and sorption grouping \parencite{Beauchemin2003}, and a
DPS threshold near $0.25$ predicted sandy-soil SRP \parencite{Siemens2004}.
The highest-leaching profiles combined large labile topsoil P pools with
low-sorption subsoils, so subsoil characteristics must be included in
risk assessment \parencite{Liu2024b}. Subsurface P losses are generally
small in fine-textured soils at moderate STP but become significant in
sandy, organic, and very high-P soils from long-term fertilisation or
manuring \parencite{Sims1998}. Sequential fractionation links pools to loss,
with P lost to water associated with short-term ($<24$~h) exchangeable pools
tied to Al, Fe, and Ca \parencite{McDowell2001}. Legacy P is manifest at
watershed scale: cumulative SRP loads in Lake Erie tributaries rose
$94$--$253\%$ after 2002 even as cropland P balances were drawn down,
$63$--$66\%$ of the increase attributable to greater delivery consistent
with increased tile drainage and connectivity \parencite{Jarvie2017}.

Organic and drained peat soils are distinctive high-risk sources, where
wetting/drying, organic-matter degradation, and redox chemistry drive
transport \parencite{Grenon2021}. Flooding organic soils increased P release
$4$--$8$-fold, and organic-P mineralisation can supply up to
$160$~kg~P~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ in organic soils \parencite{Sharpley1995}.
Very large filterable P loads were measured from an Organic soil
($86.7$~kg~P~ha$^{-1}$) versus Podzol ($1.7$) \parencite{McDowell2015b}, and
degraded drained peatlands were the highest-risk eutrophication source in a
German catchment, governed by seasonal, redox-mediated remobilisation
\parencite{Tiemeyer2009,YliHalla2022}. Storm-triggered ochre flocs from tile
drains carried $27$--$141$~mg~L$^{-1}$ TP with Fe and P co-distributed
\parencite{Zimmer2016}.

\subsection{Preferential Flow, Macroporosity, and Drainage-Induced Structure}

\subsubsection{Biopores as the dominant conduits}

Rapid delivery of water and surface-applied solutes to drains is governed by
a small volume of continuous macropores rather than uniform matrix flow. In
a Finnish clay, at $10$~cm above the tile movement occurred exclusively
through earthworm burrows ($106$ dyed burrows~m$^{-2}$), and the base of the
plough layer acted as a hydraulic barrier; infiltration above the tile lines
was about twice that between them and correlated with \textit{Lumbricus
terrestris} numbers \parencite{Shipitalo2004}. Worm burrows were the only
structures present at tile depth in a Swiss loam \parencite{Stamm2002}, and
roughly $50$ continuous burrows~m$^{-2}$ reached from surface to $90$~cm at a
German site, their abundance the most important precondition for preferential
flow of isoproturon \parencite{Zehe2001}. Smoke-injection and dye studies
directly confirmed continuous surface-to-drain pathways clustered within a
$\sim$1-m band over the drain, their number increasing as the water table
dropped \parencite{Nielsen2015,Petersen2012}. The hydraulically active pore
volume is tiny: field analysis indicated an active macroporosity of only
$\sim0.17\%$ of soil volume, yet this fraction produced abrupt drainage
concentration increases by factors of $20$--$400$ within hours
\parencite{Villholth1998}, and macropore area fractions peaked below
$0.009$, correlating strongly with saturated conductivity
\parencite{Frey2011}.

\subsubsection{Spatial organisation and static structure}

Two organising gradients recur: macroporosity and biopore abundance decline
sharply with depth, whereas lateral variation relative to the drain is real
but weaker. In a Finnish clay, macroporosity fell strongly with depth but
differed little with distance, though earthworm burrows were $44\%$ more
numerous above a drain and field saturated conductivity varied with drain
position \parencite{Alakukku2010,Frey2011}. Reviews report higher
infiltration and lower bulk density above drain lines and runoff reductions
of $34$--$55\%$ relative to undrained soils \parencite{Montagne2009}.
Sustained drainage consistently lowers bulk density and raises air-filled
porosity and gaseous diffusivity: Ohio drainage decreased bulk density and
increased air-filled pores \parencite{HUNDAL1976}, and relative gas-diffusion
gains correlated with corn yield \parencite{Nakajima2014}. Consistent with an
aeration mechanism, drainage raised soil oxygen, mineralisation, and
nitrification \parencite{GROFFMAN1984}, and dynamic aeration indicators
outperformed static bulk density in explaining productivity across a large
Canadian survey \parencite{Caron2025}. The effect is context-dependent,
however: some comparisons found lower bulk density under undrained or
subirrigated soils \parencite{LAL1993,Baker2004}, drainage reduced surface
organic matter and soil organic carbon in some Ohio studies
\parencite{FAUSEY1992,Kumar2014}, and in the most compacted soils aeration
control by drainage becomes impossible \parencite{SMEDEMA1993}.

\subsubsection{Hydraulic evolution, mole drainage, and pedogenesis}

Drainage and associated loosening raise infiltration and saturated hydraulic
conductivity, and these gains often develop over years. Fields drained
$>15$~years earlier had saturated conductivity $7.58$~cm~h$^{-1}$ faster than
recently drained fields, with preferential-flow development the likely
driver, implying that soil physical and biological properties evolve after
installation \parencite{Frankl2023,JafariTalukolaee2016}. Where matrix
permeability is inadequate, mole drainage and subsoiling create the effective
pore network: up to $90\%$ of flow occurred through mole drains on UK heavy
clays \parencite{HARRIS1993}, and moled structure persists as open cavities
for $3$--$7$ years and thereafter as zones of increased permeability
\parencite{MULLER1988,PLAMENAC1988,Castanheira2010}. Over years to decades,
redirected water fluxes accelerate pedogenesis: subsurface drainage
intensified eluviation and clay translocation, removed swelling clays and
$>$one-third of Fe oxides near the drain, and redistributed Mn oxides, with
eluviation restricted to the first metre either side of the drain and
redox-driven changes extending several metres
\parencite{Montagne2010,Montagne2008,Montagne2013,Mercier2000}. About
$70$~years of drainage formed an argic horizon in boreal Finland, a change
judged probably partly irreversible \parencite{YliHalla2009}.

\subsubsection{Macropore transport and its representation in models}

These structural findings manifest as rapid, connectivity-limited
preferential transport. Conservative tracers reach drains within minutes to
hours: chloride breakthrough increased by factors of $20$--$400$ within hours
\parencite{Villholth1998}; bromide appeared within $1$~h yet cumulative
capture was slow ($8\%$ in $48$~h, $\sim95\%$ only after months)
\parencite{Frey2012,Kladivko2023}. Liquid-manure constituents reached drains
within $5$~min, and soluble P moved through artificial macropores with little
sorption, identifying macropore flow as the primary rapid-P mechanism
\parencite{Stamm2002,Geohring2001}. These behaviours are captured only by
models representing two-domain flow or a direct macropore--drain connection:
a two-dimensional mobile--immobile model matched bromide dynamics far better
than single-porosity formulations \parencite{Köhne2006}; adding a macropore
domain was essential to match drainage chloride \parencite{Villholth1998b};
an ``express fraction'' of $\sim2\%$ reproduced immediate tracer breakthrough
in RZWQM \parencite{Fox2004,Fox2007b}; and a macropore subroutine in APEX
raised DRP-load performance from unsatisfactory to very good, with median
annual macropore-flow and macropore-P contributions of $48\%$ and $43\%$
\parencite{Ford2017}. Fine-textured soils generated more macropore flow than
coarse-textured soils, so soil texture explained connectivity-driven seasonal
variation in flow partitioning \parencite{Poon2021,Holbak2021}.

\subsection{Temporal Dynamics of Phosphorus and Sediment Loss}

Phosphorus loss is highly concentrated in time. Most annual loss occurs in
the non-growing season, which accounted for $83$--$97\%$ of runoff,
$84$--$100\%$ of DRP, and $67$--$98\%$ of TP loss in Ontario
\parencite{VanEsbroeck2016,Qi2018}. Event dominance is stark: more than
$75\%$ of DRP and $66\%$ of TP export occurred in the top $25\%$ of flows,
and a single 6-day event supplied $46.6\%$ of TP, $84.0\%$ of DRP, and
$81.0\%$ of suspended solids in one subwatershed \parencite{Brendel2019}.
Concentration--discharge behaviour differs by pathway: tile TP and DRP
increased with discharge (chemodynamic) while surface runoff was more
chemostatic, and the tile DRP:TP ratio fell at high flow, indicating a
growing PP contribution \parencite{Osterholz2020}. In cold regions, snowmelt
and winter thaws dominate exports, predominantly in dissolved form---limiting
the value of particulate-focused practices \parencite{Baulch2019,Clement2017}.
A drought-then-deluge sequence in 2021 produced unprecedented P export
($43\%$ of a 12-year DRP load in nine months) \parencite{Millar2025,Mumbi2025b}.

Preferential flow reconciles substantial subsurface PP loss with the
filtering expected of matrix flow. $^{137}$Cs tracing shows drainage
particulate matter originates from topsoil (median grain sizes $2$--$5$~$\mu$m)
translocated within hours \parencite{Laubel1999,Uusitalo2001}, and PP loss is
episodic---nearly one-third of annual PP loss occurred during sampled storms
\parencite{Laubel1999,Grant1996}. Because of this episodicity, discrete
sampling underestimated annual P loss by $26\%$ to $>100\%$
\parencite{Grant1996}. At catchment scale, source-fingerprinting attributed
$28$--$55\%$ of sediment yield to field drains, with topsoil supplying
$70.9$--$88.6\%$ of the load and drains contributing over $50\%$ of the annual
sediment budget in one catchment \parencite{Walling2002,Chapman2005}.

\subsection{Drainage Design and Water Management}

\subsubsection{Drain spacing and depth}

Drain spacing and depth are established design controls on both volume and
nitrate loss. More closely spaced drains produce greater drainage and greater
nitrate-N loss than wider spacings, whereas nitrate \emph{concentrations}
generally do not vary consistently with spacing---load differences are driven
by flow \parencite{Kladivko2021,Kladivko2004}. Shallower drains remove excess
water effectively but can cause more nitrate loss, and in salt-affected clay
soils wider spacing enhanced nitrate-leaching risk while narrower spacing
removed nitrate faster \parencite{Hashemi2020,Singh2002,Singh2001c,Singh2007}.
Calibrated models consistently show shallower and wider-spaced drains reduce
tile flow and nitrate loss at modest yield cost: reducing depth from $1.5$ to
$0.9$~m lowered simulated nitrate loss from $34.0$ to $29.4$~kg~ha$^{-1}$, and
widening spacing from $27$ to $60$~m from $33.8$ to $28.4$~kg~ha$^{-1}$
\parencite{Moriasi2013,Nangia2010c,Davis2000,Hwang2024}. For P, increasing
spacing reduced subsurface volumes and TP loss (more strongly on finer soils)
while increasing surface runoff and its TP load \parencite{Morrison2013}.

\subsubsection{Controlled drainage and subirrigation}

Controlled drainage (CD) is among the best-established practices. A synthesis
found CD reduced drainage outflow by $17\%$ to over $80\%$ depending on soil,
intensity, control strategy, and location, with nitrogen-loss reduction
often about the same percentage as the volume reduction
\parencite{Skaggs2010}. A global meta-analysis of 84 publications and 1788
paired site-years confirmed that CD and controlled drainage with
subirrigation (CDSI) reduce subsurface discharge and nutrient losses while
modestly increasing yield; CDSI reduced nitrate and TN more, CD reduced TP
more, and CDSI (but not CD) significantly increased surface runoff and
surface nitrate loss \parencite{Wu2025}. Simulations across 48 Midwest
locations found CD reduced drainage and N losses everywhere, with efficacy
strongly climate-dependent \parencite{Youssef2018}. Field studies quantify
these effects concretely: Quebec CDSI cut drainage volume $24\%$, nitrate
concentration $25\%$, and nitrate loss $43\%$ \parencite{Ng2000}; a long-term
Canadian study found CD reduced total nitrate loss $\sim58\%$ and increased
yield $3\%$ without elevated greenhouse-gas emissions
\parencite{Madramootoo2024}; and nine years of North Carolina CD reduced
outflow $33\%$ and nitrate export $30\%$ while raising yields $10$--$11\%$
\parencite{Poole2018}. Growing-season controlled drainage reduced water flux
$\sim60\%$, nitrate $51\%$, ammonium $58\%$, DRP and TP $66\%$, and
\emph{E.\ coli} $76\%$, with watershed-scale deployment cutting stream fluxes
of water, N, and P \parencite{Sunohara2016,Sunohara2015}. In Illinois, managed
systems always exported less nitrate than free drainage, with flow reductions
of $35$--$96\%$ and load reductions of $37$--$79\%$ (averaging $61\%$)
\parencite{Cooke2012}, and syntheses report drainage water management reducing
tile flow $\sim46\%$ and nitrate loads $\sim45$--$48\%$, mainly by reducing
discharge rather than enhancing denitrification
\parencite{Ross2016,Ward2018}.

Subirrigation lowered soil-profile nitrate up to $50\%$ and drainage nitrate
concentrations $55$--$73\%$, with water-quality benefits accruing even when
outflow occasionally exceeded free drainage, implicating enhanced
denitrification \parencite{Elmi2004,Elmi2002,Noory2009}. Shallow drainage
reduced flow $38$--$64\%$ and had the lowest nitrate concentration and
loading among systems, though it sometimes reduced yields whereas CD trended
toward higher yields \parencite{Smith2019,Smith2011b}. Trade-offs and timing
matter: CD can increase surface DRP and TP that partly offset subsurface
benefits \parencite{King2022,Wu2025}, drainage management often fails to
lower concentrations even where it lowers loads via volume reduction
\parencite{Grenon2023,Wesström2014}, and DRAINMOD analysis identified early
March as the most effective control window (reductions up to $100\%$) whereas
April starts resembled free drainage \parencite{Kesicka2023c}. Greenhouse-gas
responses are mixed but generally not adverse
\parencite{Madramootoo2024,Smith2019}.

\subsubsection{Drainage water recycling}

Drainage water recycling (DWR)---capturing and storing drainage for
supplemental irrigation---benefits both production and downstream water
quality. Yield gains reach $61\%$ for corn and $31\%$ for soybean, with
Canadian N-loss reductions of $40$--$70\%$ and P-loss reductions of
$12$--$36\%$, and modelled downstream N-delivery reductions of $24$--$37\%$
\parencite{Hay2021}. A North Carolina reservoir reduced TN load $47\%$, TP
$30\%$, and sediment $87\%$, with hydraulic retention time the key control,
though it was a net source of particulate P \parencite{Moursi2023,Moursi2022}.
Multi-location synthesis of 53 site-years showed DWR is more resilient than
free drainage, protecting yield during drought and excess precipitation and
reducing yield variability $22\%$ \parencite{Willison2021}, and adoption
economics indicate crop insurance is not a major barrier \parencite{Sellars2022}.
In arid systems, integrated on-farm drainage management reused all but
$0.7\%$ of applied water \parencite{Ayars2014,Kosari2024}.

\subsection{In-Field Management: Cover Crops, Rotation, and Nutrient Management}

Cover crops consistently reduce nutrient loss where measured directly at the
tile. In Indiana, cover-cropped tiles had $69$--$90\%$ lower seasonal nitrate
loads \parencite{Hanrahan2018}, and independent monitoring found cover crops
cut tile discharge $28$--$65\%$ and nitrate loss $27$--$72\%$ annually, with
more variable SRP reductions \parencite{Speir2022,Trentman2020}. A rye winter
cover crop reduced flow-weighted nitrate $59\%$ and loads $61\%$ over four
years, chiefly by lowering concentration \parencite{Kaspar2007}, and reviews
report cover-crop nitrate reductions of $13$--$94\%$ \parencite{Ward2018}. At
the watershed outlet, however, the signal is weaker and scale-dependent:
annual, seasonal, and monthly nitrate export did not differ significantly
between pre- and post-cover-crop years despite strong tile-scale effects, and
monitored tiles contributed only $\sim7\%$ of watershed export
\parencite{Hanrahan2018,Speir2022}. Some cover-crop and relay systems showed
no benefit and higher N surplus or N$_2$O
\parencite{Emmett2022,Askar2023}. Cover-crop effects on P are inconsistent:
some studies found no significant DRP reduction or even increased leaching
\parencite{deOliveira2022,Liu2019}, while others modelled DRP and TP reductions
of $16$--$54\%$ \parencite{Grewal2025,Askar2021b}.

Nitrogen input rate is a strong lever on nitrate load but weaker on
concentration. Reducing N application from $200$ to $100$~kg~ha$^{-1}$ cut
simulated nitrate loss $67\%$ with no yield penalty \parencite{Moriasi2013},
and $20/40/60\%$ input cuts reduced loads $23/49/94\%$ in one model
\parencite{Chaplot2004}. Shifting applications from fall to spring or split
timing consistently reduced losses ($22$--$46\%$ depending on rate and
precipitation) \parencite{Wilson2020,Gowda2007,Nangia2010,Singh2020b}. Yet
several long-term records found reducing fertilizer rate did not immediately
reduce export, consistent with soil-N legacy effects
\parencite{Kelley2017,Cambardella1999,Siksnane2017}. Incidental loss within
$30$ days of application accounted for $52\%$ of cumulative tile nitrate load
at one Indiana site \parencite{Williams2022}. Cropping system matters:
replacing corn--soybean with pasture or perennials reduced nitrate loss, and
corn--soybean rotations lost less than continuous corn
\parencite{Chaplot2004,Ni2020,Arrueta2022}. In Ohio, alfalfa significantly
reduced subsurface nitrate, TN, and P loads, especially during large storms
\parencite{Arrueta2022,Arrueta2025}.

Long-term P inputs raise subsurface P losses, generally in the order
continuous grass $>$ rotation $>$ continuous corn, with fertilised grass
shifting from a P sink to a source \parencite{Zhang2015b}. Swine-manure
compost produced much greater DRP, PP, and TP than controls
\parencite{Zhang2015}, and about $60\%$ of DRP losses from a no-till
cover-crop site followed fall surface fertilisation, highlighting incidental
loss \parencite{Macrae2023,Mumbi2025b}. Across the MANAGE database, generally
less than $2\%$ of applied P was lost in drainage \parencite{Christianson2016}.
Tillage involves trade-offs: no-till and conservation tillage were associated
with higher subsurface DRP but tillage increased PP and sediment
\parencite{Ni2020,Macrae2023,Koskiaho2002}. In snowmelt regions, zero tillage
and perennial forages can \emph{increase} dissolved P despite reducing erosion
\parencite{Baulch2019}.

\subsection{Edge-of-Field and Beyond-the-Field Practices}

\subsubsection{Constructed wetlands}

Constructed wetlands treating drainage are extensively documented nitrate
sinks, with denitrification dominant. Across French watersheds, nitrate
removal averaged $\sim42\%$, dominated by denitrification ($93\%$), and
devoting $\sim1\%$ of contributing area can achieve $50\%$ removal, with
hydraulic residence time the priority control \parencite{Tournebize2017}.
Iowa wetlands reduced incoming nitrate $49\%$ \parencite{Drake2018}, a
Minnesota wetland removed $67\%$ over four years \parencite{Gordon2021}, and
Illinois wetlands retained $36$--$37\%$ of N with $\sim450$~ha estimated
necessary to cut watershed N loading $46\%$ \parencite{Kovacic2006}.
Performance is a strong function of hydraulic loading, residence time,
temperature, and inlet concentration, so no single-number design is adequate
\parencite{Kadlec2012,Steidl2019}. Wetlands remove nitrate reliably but
perform variably for P, sometimes exporting more TP than received
\parencite{Tanner2005,Tanner2011,Messer2021}, with plant harvest an important
P route in some systems \parencite{Lenhart2016}.

\subsubsection{Bioreactors, walls, buffers, and ditches}

Woodchip-based denitrification is well supported: denitrification walls
reduced flow-weighted nitrate $\geq55\%$ and mass loss $55\%$ over five years
with no yield penalty and no land taken out of production
\parencite{Jaynes2008}, and a rye cover crop and woodchip wall reduced N
leaching $58$--$59\%$, the wall more effective in wet years
\parencite{Rogovska2023}. A stacked drainage-management--bioreactor--saturated
buffer system reduced nitrate export $78$--$88\%$ \parencite{Singh2024b}, and
saturated buffers offer the largest climate-mitigation potential per unit area
among conservation drainage practices \parencite{Hall2025}. In-channel and
edge practices add interception: a low-grade weir reduced nitrate load $58\%$
\parencite{Strock2025b}, a two-stage ditch reduced TP load $40\%$
\parencite{Hodaj2017}, and riparian buffers are highly effective for nitrate,
with groundwater nitrate dropping from $7$--$8$ to $<0.1$~mg~L$^{-1}$ within
$16$--$47$~m via denitrification \parencite{JACOBS1985,Schilling2022}.
Redirecting tile flow into a buffer removed nitrate at
$1.01\pm0.07$~mg~N~L$^{-1}$~m$^{-1}$, with chloride confirming denitrification
rather than dilution \parencite{Jaynes2014,Carstensen2021}. Denitrification
beds followed Michaelis--Menten kinetics with very low greenhouse-gas
emissions \parencite{Ghane2015}. Where tile drains route water directly to
streams, however, they can bypass the riparian zone, limiting attenuation
\parencite{Puckett2005}.

\subsubsection{Comparative effectiveness and cost}

Comparative syntheses provide load-reduction benchmarks: denitrifying
bioreactors, wetlands, cover crops, rotation, and nutrient management each
reduced subsurface nitrate loads $23$--$40\%$ \parencite{Yuan2022}, and in the
Lower Mississippi Alluvial Valley nine BMPs delivered nutrient reductions of
$15$--$100\%$ \parencite{Kröger2012}. Cost-effectiveness analysis ranked
wetlands most cost-effective ($2.04$--$2.91$~\$~kg$^{-1}$~N), followed by
two-stage ditches and cover crops \parencite{Roley2016,Messer2021}. For P,
edge-of-field practices retain P chiefly by particulate sedimentation
($27$--$100\%$) but depend strongly on residence time and can flip from sink
to source during events \parencite{Mendes2020,Kröger2013,Singh2020c};
controlled drainage cut dissolved P $63\%$ and TP $50\%$ in Minnesota
\parencite{Kröger2013,Nash2015c}, and blind inlets and subsurface fertilizer
placement reduced DRP $65$--$72\%$ \parencite{Kröger2013,Wilson2019,Sharpley2019}.
Overall, effective P control depends on matching the right practice to site
hydrology, connectivity, weather, and legacy P, and on recognising N--P
trade-offs \parencite{King2015,Ni2020,Baulch2019}.

\subsection{Herbicides and Pesticides in Drainage}

The timing of runoff and drainage relative to application is the single most
powerful control on peak pesticide concentrations and losses. Synthesising
more than thirty North American studies, \textcite{Kladivko2001} established
that pesticides are commonly detected in drainflow during the first drainage
event(s) after application, concentrations decline rapidly thereafter, and
most mass losses are very small---generally less than $0.5\%$ and often less
than $0.1\%$ of applied---while concentrations and losses in tile drainage are
usually much lower than in surface runoff, frequently by an order of
magnitude. Site-specific values corroborate this: Illinois long-term atrazine
losses were $0.11$--$0.22\%$ of application \parencite{Kalita2006}; New York
2,4-D and atrazine losses were $0.08\%$ and $0.02\%$ \parencite{Shalit1996};
and less than $1\%$ of applied herbicide was recovered in tile drains over two
years \parencite{Fortin2002,Siimes2006}. Early events dominate: most atrazine
and metolachlor loss occurred within the first $61$ days
\parencite{SOUTHWICK1990}, and over $80\%$ of catchment losses occurred during
just two rain events \parencite{Leu2004}. In cold regions, most loss can occur
during snowmelt and thaw rather than immediately after application
\parencite{Siimes2006}, and two-phase seasonal behaviour is common
\parencite{Novak2001,Dousset2004}.

The mechanism reconciling near-immediate arrival with small overall losses is
preferential (macropore) flow. Tracer breakthrough curves were typical of
preferential flow, with herbicides arriving after less than $1$~cm of net
drainage, interpreted as transport through only $\sim3\%$ of pore volume
\parencite{Fortin2002,Dousset2004}. Soil structure, rather than sorption or
degradation, dominated between-soil differences, with greater metolachlor
losses in a clayey Vertic Cambisol than a loamy Luvisol
\parencite{Novak2001,Novak2003}. Although mass losses are small, transient
concentrations can exceed thresholds: Ontario maxima reached
$287$~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ (atrazine) in tile discharge, with the atrazine MCL
exceeded in $60\%$ of tile samples \parencite{GAYNOR1995}, and Iowa subsurface
drains had glyphosate maxima above $250$~$\mu$g~L$^{-1}$ \parencite{Coupe2012}.
Degradates contribute meaningfully and can be more mobile than parents:
deethylatrazine accounted for $10$--$27\%$ of combined loss
\parencite{GAYNOR1995,MUIR1978,JAYACHANDRAN1994}. Management levers include
banding, residue-conserving tillage with intercrops (reducing loss
$46$--$49\%$), and application-timing restriction via a soil-wetness index
\parencite{Gaynor2001,Julien2023,Moorman1999}, whereas corn-stover harvest can
worsen drainage quality \parencite{Shipitalo2016}. Woodchip and
biochar-amended bioreactors removed atrazine (up to $93\%$) mainly abiotically
without inhibiting denitrification \parencite{Hassanpour2019,Ilhan2011}, and a
substantial modelling literature (PESTDRAIN, HYDRUS-2D, RZWQM, SWAT+)
reproduces drainage pesticide dynamics when macroporosity and sorption
kinetics are represented
\parencite{Branger2009,Boivin2006,Shipitalo2016,Wendell2024,Novak2003}. One
explicit methodological gap remains: no reports quantify how flux-averaged
drainage concentrations differ from concentrations between drains, so
evidence-based conclusions on that comparison cannot be drawn
\parencite{Jacobsen2007}. Tile drainage also transports emerging
contaminants---PPCPs, hormones, PBDEs, perfluoroalkyl acids, and
neonicotinoids---whose behaviour mirrors the timing and preferential-flow
patterns above, generally at low, transient concentrations
\parencite{Gottschall2012,Gottschall2013,Gottschall2017,Ghirardini2019,Schaafsma2019}.

\subsection{Manure-Borne Microbial Contaminants and Resistance Genes}

Manure and organic amendments introduce fecal microorganisms, resistance
genes, hormones, and trace elements that drainage can rapidly route to
receiving waters, with application method and soil moisture---rather than
manure presence alone---largely governing transport. Curli-producing
\textit{E.\ coli} were far more abundant in tile drains from farms that
surface-applied manure than one that incorporated it, indicating application
method itself shapes the transported phenotype \parencite{Truhlar2015}.
Tillage governs pathogen delivery mainly through macroporosity: zero tillage
generally produced higher subsurface flow and greater \textit{E.\ coli} loads
than conventional tillage \parencite{Thiagarajan2007,Jenkins2009}. Antecedent
soil moisture and water-table position exert first-order control: effluent on
wet soil produced drain-flow \textit{E.\ coli} of $10^{5}$--$10^{7}$
per~100~ml versus $\sim10^{4}$ under a greater moisture deficit, and where
preferential flow occurred, drainage concentrations approached those of the
applied effluent \parencite{Collins2007,Ding2021}. Manure is a reservoir of
antibiotic resistance genes that persist through drained soils into drainage
water: shotgun metagenomics identified $320$ unique ARGs, with manure
treatment shifting soil and drainage-water resistomes and many manure-derived
genes associated with mobile genetic elements in food- and water-borne
pathogens \parencite{Smith2019b}. Steroid hormones were detected only
transiently and generally did not differ significantly among treatments
\parencite{Jenkins2009}. Engineered receiving features can attenuate
microbial loads: a constructed wetland reduced nitrate and \textit{E.\ coli}
mass loads $67.6\%$ and $63.3\%$ \parencite{Haverstock2017}, though some
wetlands act as net \textit{E.\ coli} exporters \parencite{Stott2023}. Drained
soils, bioreactors, and wetlands host distinct, dynamic microbial communities
structured by depth and season, and favourable contemporary conditions can be
engineered to develop effective denitrifier assemblages
\parencite{Andrus2014,Porter2015,Hathaway2015,Knorr2023,Schilling2017}.

\subsection{Salinity, Trace Elements, and Reclamation}

Beyond N and P, drainage governs salt and trace-element transport. Reducing
applied irrigation and the leaching fraction can cut salt discharge by $50\%$
or more, with reuse for salt-tolerant crops and evaporation basins practical
pathways, though basins accumulate extreme salinity and trace elements
\parencite{WESTCOT1988}. The Kesterson Reservoir remains the definitive case
of trace-element hazard: waterborne selenium averaged $0.122$~mg~L$^{-1}$
($\sim230$ times reference), biomagnified through the food chain, and caused
dead and deformed bird embryos, with integrated studies fulfilling the
equivalent of Koch's postulates and identifying selenium as the sole causative
agent \parencite{SAIKI1987,Ohlendorf2002,BENSON1993}. Consequently, open-water
evaporation is prohibited in California, requiring rock-media systems
\parencite{Ayars2014,BRADFORD1991}, and selenium-removal wetlands retained
about $59\%$ of inflow Se \parencite{Gao2003}. In evaporation ponds,
evapoconcentration and redox chemistry jointly control arsenic and uranium
mobility \parencite{Ryu2010,Gao2007,Duff1997}, and boron isotopes and other
tracers can distinguish contaminant sources \parencite{Tayfur2010,Komor1997,Zhou2023}.
Intensive drainage can drive negative micronutrient balances and net soil
depletion \parencite{Gul2013}, and central-Iowa drainage ionic composition was
dominated by bicarbonate, calcium, and magnesium \parencite{Zimmerman2017b}.

In salt-affected soils, drainage design governs both reclamation and nitrogen
dynamics. Sustained subsurface drainage lowered soil salinity from $35$ to
$4$~dS~m$^{-1}$ at $15$-m spacing and improved rice yield, with narrower
spacing favouring nitrate removal and reduced groundwater loading
\parencite{Singh2002,Singh2001c,Gafni2001}, and discontinuing drainage caused
nitrite to accumulate as salinity inhibited nitrification \parencite{Singh2007}.
Pairing biochar with subsurface drainage improves saline--alkali soils beyond
either practice alone, reducing electrical conductivity and raising nutrients,
enzyme activity, and bacterial diversity, with drainage a stronger salinity
control than biochar \parencite{Tian2023b,Liu2025f,Xiong2025}. Farm manure is
an effective saline--sodic amendment producing the best yields
\parencite{Qadir2001}, and combined subsurface drainage, no-till, gypsum, and
cover crops protected crops from salinisation through soil processes not
revealed by soil indices alone \parencite{Daigh2025,Zhang2024d,Zhu2025}.
Drainage of acid sulphate soils is a distinct hazard, causing stream pH
declines to near or below $4.0$ and elevating Al and metals, with controlled
pipe drainage recommended over in-stream liming
\parencite{Osterholm2005,Åström2005,Virtanen2017,Salo2023}.

\subsection{Greenhouse-Gas Dimensions}

Drainage nitrate is coupled to greenhouse-gas fluxes. Drainage water is
commonly supersaturated with dissolved N$_2$O, CH$_4$, and CO$_2$: in Japan,
indirect emissions via subsurface drainage were equivalent to $4.6\%$,
$58.3\%$, and $2.5\%$ of direct emissions, with N$_2$O-N/NO$_3$-N ratios below
the IPCC factor \parencite{Sawamoto2003,Minamikawa2010,Minamikawa2013}. In
Canada, dissolved N$_2$O losses approached surface emissions, were strongly
seasonal, and declined along tile length, implying an in-drain sink and likely
underestimation of the IPCC coefficient \parencite{Burton2024}, and a global
synthesis found subsurface drains had the highest mean emission factor among
artificial waters, with the stationary IPCC EF tending to overestimate N$_2$O
production \parencite{Webb2021}. Conversely, installing drainage sharply
reduces methane in paddy systems by improving aeration: drained fields emitted
$71\%$ less CH$_4$ than non-drained fields \parencite{Shiratori2007,Furukawa2008}.
Management modulates N$_2$O: controlled drainage kept cumulative N$_2$O below
$1\%$ of applied N, and saturated riparian buffers removed nitrate without
increasing N$_2$O \parencite{Smith2019,Davis2019,Nash2015e,Carstensen2021}.
Carbon export from drained fields is dominated by inorganic carbon through
tiles ($\sim96\%$ of total carbon export), so reducing water throughput
supports carbon retention \parencite{Schilling2023,Driscoll2025}.

\subsection{Modelling Drainage Flow, Nitrate, and Phosphorus}

Process models are the principal tools for quantifying drainage flow and
nitrate loss and extrapolating short experiments across weather, soils, and
management. After site-specific calibration, the DRAINMOD suite, RZWQM/RZWQM2,
SWAT/APEX, and cropping-system and biogeochemical models (APSIM, DNDC,
\textit{ecosys}, HERMES, HYDRUS) reproduce annual and cumulative drainage and
nitrate loss well---frequently within roughly $\pm5$--$20\%$---and capture the
direction of management effects even when absolute values are imperfect
\parencite{Youssef2018,Negm2014,Singh2020b,Dayyani2010,Ma2001}. A near-universal
pattern is that predictive skill improves with temporal aggregation: annual
and cumulative values are simulated well, monthly less so, and daily nitrate
concentrations often unsatisfactorily
\parencite{Liang2018,Ikenberry2017,Marjerison2016,Ojeda2018,Qi2025}. Nitrate is
typically predicted less accurately than flow, and crop N uptake, residual
soil N, and yield are recurrent weak points \parencite{Ma2007,Ahuja2007}.
DRAINMOD-N~II and RZWQM2 both satisfactorily predicted tile nitrate in Iowa,
DRAINMOD-N~II slightly better \parencite{Du2017,Qi2012}, and DRAINMOD-DSSAT
predicted drainage, nitrate, and yield very well
\parencite{Negm2014,Negm2017,Singh2022c}. Extensions to phosphorus are
advancing but remain limited: RZWQM2-P was the first to accurately simulate
daily DRP and reproduced pathway partitioning
\parencite{Sadhukhan2019,Grewal2025,BinShokrana2023b}, DRAINMOD-P satisfactorily
predicted discharge and TP but poorly predicted DRP
\parencite{Askar2021,Askar2025}, and few standard models yet represent tile P
\parencite{Radcliffe2015,Wang2020b}.

Cold-season snowmelt and freeze--thaw dynamics are a widespread source of error
that motivated dedicated developments (DRAINMOD~5.1, DRAIN-WARMF, RZ-SHAW)
\parencite{Wang2006c,Dayyani2010b,Li2024b}. Calibration studies repeatedly
identify soil-hydraulic parameters---especially lateral saturated conductivity
in the tile layer and field capacity---as controlling predictions, with
measured properties substantially outperforming default texture-class values
\parencite{Ma2007c,Ahuja2007,Malone2017}. Data-driven surrogates (neural
networks, Cubist, SVM, and parsimonious regressions such as NIT-DRAIN) can
match or exceed process models for flow while training far faster, though
process models remain preferable for extrapolation to future climates
\parencite{Sharma2003,Negm2016,Frederiksen2023,Chelil2022,Li2025}. Calibrated
models converge on robust management conclusions---N rate is the dominant
control on nitrate loss, shallower and wider-spaced drains and controlled
drainage reduce losses, and cover crops reduce nitrate export
\parencite{Moriasi2013,Fang2012,Malone2007c,Youssef2023}. Once validated,
these models project a coherent picture of climate-change impacts: warming
increases winter drainage and shifts its timing in cold regions, while future
nitrate and P responses diverge with local precipitation trends
\parencite{Li2025,Rasmussen2018,Jeantet2022,Haghnazari2020,Dialameh2025,Wang2021,Mehan2019,Adhikari2020}.
Ensemble analyses caution that climate-model choice can dominate uncertainty
in the direction of future nutrient change \parencite{Kujawa2020}.

\subsection{Watershed-Scale Integration and Reduction Strategies}

Tile drainage is the primary pathway controlling watershed water and nitrogen
export in drained landscapes, so downstream targets translate to large
required reductions at the tile outlet---estimated at about $55\%$ for one
Iowa river \parencite{Schilling2012b,Ikenberry2014}. Concentrations decline
with drainage area as export dilutes downstream \parencite{Schilling2012b,Billy2013},
and substantial in-network retention ($34$--$82\%$) occurs via underground
denitrification, though in-stream processing immediately below tile outlets
offers little potential because its timing is poorly matched to seasonal
loading \parencite{Billy2011,Schilling2012b}. Monitoring design strongly
affects inferred loads: high-frequency sampling captured storm dynamics missed
by grab sampling, which overestimated loads, and significant nitrate declines
are far more detectable in flow-weighted concentration than in load or yield
\parencite{Drake2018,Danalatos2022,Irvine2019}. Long-term records can detect
improvement, with Czech outlet nitrate declining over 2004--2018 and stacked
BMPs reducing nitrate load $60.3\%$ \parencite{Fraindová2022,Law2022}.

Watershed-planning studies show that meeting large reduction goals requires
combining in-field and edge-of-field practices. In the Mississippi basin,
nitrogen-management practices alone were insufficient, but N-management plus
N-removal practices reached the $45\%$ target while converting under $6\%$ of
cropland when targeted \parencite{McLellan2015}, and LiDAR-based planning
achieved $34$--$39\%$ nitrate reduction while removing $<5\%$ of cropland
\parencite{Tomer2015,Kvítek2023}. Watershed evidence also cautions that
traditional surface-water BMPs may be inadequate over tile drainage: seven
years of strip-till, grassed waterways, and stream buffers produced no
measurable change in nitrate, phosphorus, sediment, or hydrology because they
did not intercept subsurface flow, which must be a design priority
\parencite{Lemke2011}. Ecosystem-service framing further shows that drainage
modernization can sharply reduce migratory-bird habitat while paired
water-quality wetlands offset part of the loss
\parencite{Mitchell2022,Mitchell2025,Dollinger2015}.

\subsection{Ecological Responses to Subsurface Drainage}

Drainage reshapes agricultural ecology from soil fauna upward. \textit{Lumbricus
terrestris} abundance was typically higher above drains than between them
(median counts twice as high, fresh mass five times), so drainage pattern
affects population density and should inform sampling design
\parencite{Nuutinen2001,Nuutinen2011}. Drainage type is associated with
farmland-bird outcomes, with skylark and meadow pipit favouring open-drained
over subsurface-drained fields \parencite{Marja2013}, while tile-drainage
wetlands carried cumulative pesticide concentrations $4.7$ times higher than
reference wetlands (neonicotinoid-dominated), with measurable neurological
effects in juvenile amphibians \parencite{Campbell2023}. Subsurface drainage
of meadows was a leading cause of decline in semi-aquatic snails
\parencite{Dreyfuss2018}. Restored aquatic habitats can benefit: in restored
Iowa oxbows, tile drainage had negligible effects on physical or biotic
variables, providing promising evidence that multipurpose oxbows can enhance
biodiversity without ecological penalty, with restoration outcomes improved by
sediment excavation \parencite{Leberg2024,Winikoff2020,Harris2018}. Management
practices often exert clearer effects on soil structure than tile drainage
itself: no-till and diverse rotations altered bulk density, organic matter, and
aggregate stability while cover crops and drainage had minimal effect in some
settings \parencite{Saha2024,Hermawan1996,Sherbine2023}, and additional
practices such as polyacrylamide amendment and anti-weed mesh with drainage
provide further quantified nutrient-retention and production benefits
\parencite{Jiang2010,GiménezValero2024,GiménezValero2025}.

### Material: Drainage System Flow Modeling and Dynamics

\subsection{Overview}

The simulation, design and performance evaluation of agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage rest on several converging lines of evidence: site-scale field validations of water-table depth, drain discharge, salinity and solute fluxes; benchmark comparisons of analytical drain-spacing solutions against experiments, numerical codes and one another; pipe- and conduit-scale hydraulic experiments; watershed- and regional-scale studies of how drainage is conceptualised within distributed models; formal sensitivity, calibration and uncertainty analyses; and a large body of tracer, hydrograph-separation and monitoring work that quantifies how drainage reshapes streamflow. Across this literature a robust set of consolidated findings has emerged. The field-scale water-management model \textsc{DRAINMOD} is by far the most extensively tested and is repeatedly judged a reliable design and evaluation tool for poorly and artificially drained lands \parencite{Singh2019b,Gowda2012}. Model skill is strongly scale-dependent in time: monthly and annual drainage volumes and cumulative water balances are reproduced with high accuracy, whereas daily and sub-daily peak flows, low dry-season discharge and snowmelt events are the persistent weak points \parencite{Gowda2012,Dayyani2009,Morrison2014,Varvaris2018}. Treating drainage as purely saturated flow can over-predict discharge by up to an order of magnitude, so capillary and unsaturated-zone storage cannot be neglected \parencite{Xin2016b}. Saturated hydraulic conductivity and the evapotranspiration algorithm dominate model sensitivity and uncertainty \parencite{NabiSichani2012,Malakshahi2020,Wang2006}. Unsteady-state spacing equations yield narrower, and evapotranspiration-aware solutions wider, more economical spacings than classical steady-state theory \parencite{OOSTERBAAN1988,NIKAM1992}. At pipe scale, entrance resistance and effective radius are now quantified for a wide range of perforation and envelope geometries \parencite{Ghane2022,Ghane2022b}. At the catchment scale, tile drainage supplies a major share of streamflow (frequently 40--65\%), raises low flows and baseflow, damps intermediate flows, has modest effect on the largest floods, and homogenises hydrologic response, while how drains are represented can shift the simulated drain contribution to streamflow by a factor of two or more \parencite{King2014,Schilling2008,Sloan2017,BolandBrien2014,Karan2021,Boico2022}. Data-driven, remote-sensing and geostatistical methods now complement process models for prediction, mapping and design. This section synthesises the quantified performance, mechanisms and design implications these studies have established.

\subsection{Field-Scale Water-Management Models}

\subsubsection{DRAINMOD as the Benchmark Field-Scale Model}

\textsc{DRAINMOD} is one of the first widely adopted and most extensively used models for assessing drainage performance in tile-drained fields, and its process-based structure with independently measurable inputs allows good description of drainage hydrology across a wide range of climates when soil hydraulic properties are adequately characterised \parencite{Singh2019b,Gowda2012}. Across humid and temperate sites it reproduces water-table depth and drainage discharge closely, with accuracy improving markedly from the daily to the monthly and annual scale. In North Carolina, water-table-depth Nash--Sutcliffe efficiencies (EF) were 0.68--0.72, daily drainage EF 0.49--0.73, monthly drainage EF 0.77--0.87, and annual drainage volumes lay within 3~cm (5\%) of observations, with a two-year cumulative difference of 1~cm (1\%) \parencite{Gowda2012}. In Iowa, cumulative 1990--2003 drainage was within 2--10\% of measured values with a slight tendency to over-predict in low-drainage years \parencite{Singh2006b}, and two-year eastern-Canada evaluations reported simulated annual drainage about 30~mm below observed while capturing event timing and magnitude \parencite{Dayyani2009}. A recurring tendency is to underestimate daily peak flows even where general trends are captured \parencite{Morrison2014,Singh1996}. Uncertainty analysis in Indiana confirmed the scale dependence: 90\% confidence-interval widths grew from 0--0.6~cm~day$^{-1}$ (daily) to 7.6--12.4~cm~year$^{-1}$ (yearly), yet annual predictions fell well within the bounds \parencite{Wang2006}.

\textsc{DRAINMOD} has been validated well beyond its original humid setting. In cold eastern Canada it accurately simulated subsurface-drainage and water-table timing and magnitude including winter and spring snowmelt ($R^2$ up to 0.93 for water-table depth), with calibration improving monthly performance but surface runoff and daily peaks predicted less well, and better performance in uniform than in cracking soils \parencite{Dayyani2009,Morrison2014}. Under semi-arid Indian conditions, midspan water-table heights agreed to average absolute deviations of 14--24~cm, though the model responded too fast to rainfall and over-estimated depths in dry and under-estimated them in wet periods, yet was judged satisfactory for drainage design \parencite{GUPTA1993}. On Illinois claypan soils weekly cumulative SEW30 agreed at $R^2=0.91$, and subsurface drainage affected trafficability and SEW30 far more than surface drainage \parencite{MOSTAGHIMI1989}; further acceptable-to-good calibrations ($R^2=0.89$--0.93, NSE $=0.84$--0.89) have been reported for groundwater-table prediction \parencite{Abduljaleel2023,Awad2022b}.

The literature also delineates failure modes. At an Israeli site water-table differences reached 0.3--1.7~m in four of five replicates, attributed to a river boundary, permeable sandy layers below drain depth, and deep-plus-lateral seepage exceeding 50\% of annual irrigation plus rainfall \parencite{Sinai2006}. A structural drawback is the inability to represent time-variable surface depression storage, producing an unrealistic water fate and a marked observed--predicted gap during a mid-summer paddy drainage period \parencite{Abduljaleel2023}. The model over-predicts drainage in low-drainage years \parencite{Singh2006b} and, being built around parallel-drain geometry, requires plot-by-plot calibration in irregular landscapes, where equivalent drain-spacing parameters ranged from about 1700 to 4000~cm and organic layers strongly influenced water tables \parencite{He2002}.

Because site-specific hydraulic data are often lacking, pedotransfer estimation combined with automated calibration has been shown to drive \textsc{DRAINMOD} reliably. ROSETTA-derived properties with the PEST estimator produced calibrated Iowa applications \parencite{Singh2006b}, and three levels of soil hydraulic parameter sets all gave acceptable annual and monthly drainage, although calibrated parameters best in calibration performed worst in validation \parencite{Qi2015}. In South Africa, van Genuchten fits ($R^2>0.90$) supported validation NSE of 0.86 (water table) and 0.81 (discharge) \parencite{Malota2024,Malota2015}. Enhancements target the daily-peak weakness: incorporating macropore flow captured about 75\% of 60 peak events and predicted cumulative drainage within 8\%, though surface runoff was strongly over-predicted \parencite{Askar2020}. A neural-network surrogate (\textsc{DRAINMOD-ANN}) cloned daily water-table predictions across layouts without altering source code, GRNN outperforming feed-forward training \parencite{Awad2022,Awad2022b}. Sensitivity to the evapotranspiration algorithm is pronounced: replacing the default Thornthwaite method with Rohwer or pan-based estimates substantially improved water-table prediction in Iranian paddies, and the best ET methods were not necessarily best for water-table simulation \parencite{Malakshahi2020}; moving ET$_0$ from a daily to an hourly step shifted the water fate, raising runoff 4.8\% and lowering drainage 3.1\% \parencite{Awad2022b}.

\subsubsection{Variably Saturated, Comparative and Specialised Field Models}

Richards-equation codes complement \textsc{DRAINMOD}. HYDRUS (2D/3D) consistently predicts drainage fluxes and water-table depths under contrasting systems (EF 0.91--1; RMSE 0.2--6.4~cm) in Iranian paddies and is recommended for identifying optimal layouts \parencite{Karandish2018,DarziNaftchali2018}. It reproduced Canadian soil-water content (67--76\% of variance) and clarified mechanics, showing that in compacted histosols matric-potential drawdown was 58\% faster below than above a compact layer, so raising that layer's conductivity (by up to $50\times$) rather than narrowing spacing is the effective remedy \parencite{Mante2018,Guedessou2021}; it also simulated higher drainage than the Hooghoudt and Murashima--Ogino equations for paddies and highlighted the control of spacing, depth and cracks \parencite{Ebrahimian2015}. Related variably saturated codes include the CSUID 3D model, which reproduced 1D infiltration, 2D water-table and drainage benchmarks ($R^2=0.96$--0.99) while propagating parameter uncertainty to root-zone salinity and yield \parencite{Alzraiee2013b,Alzraiee2013}, and Richards/Darcy simulations of sheet-pipe systems showing strong textural control of drawdown \parencite{Saptomo2021,Li2021}.

Head-to-head comparisons generally find several field models perform comparably. In the Netherlands, WAVE, SWAP and \textsc{DRAINMOD} predicted lateral drainage flux equally reliably, \textsc{DRAINMOD} giving the most accurate discharge \parencite{ElSadek2001}. \textsc{DRAINMOD} and SWACROP predicted midspan water tables and outflow close to measurements without calibration, \textsc{DRAINMOD} slightly better, though both over-predicted outflow after dry spells \parencite{Prasher1996}. SWAP reproduced water-table behaviour and cumulative discharge in India (0.17--0.18~m; 7.8--39.4~mm) and was endorsed as a simple performance tool \parencite{Verma2014}. \textsc{WaSim} predicted water-table depth well ($R^2=0.86$) but discharge less confidently ($R^2=0.57$) under Rosetta-estimated conductivity \parencite{Malota2022}, yet was judged a workable design and reclamation tool elsewhere \parencite{Hirekhan2007,Malota2024}. Among distributed models, \textsc{DRAINMOD} tended to over-predict and CATHY to under-predict tile flow, both poor at low flow but good in wet periods, with the layer below the drains most influential \parencite{Muma2017}. For salinity, a saturated--unsaturated ``SD'' approach outperformed \textsc{DRAINMOD-S}, which over-estimated short-term and under-estimated long-term salinity \parencite{Nozari2021}, although \textsc{DRAINMOD-S} itself calibrated satisfactorily in coastal China \parencite{Feng2018}.

Broader agricultural models have been adapted with generally acceptable results. \textsc{RZWQM}/\textsc{RZWQM2} simulated weekly drain flow moderately well (NSE 0.57--0.64) and reproduced atrazine transport, macropore number and partitioning being key \parencite{Malone2014,Singh1996}; a modified version allowing redistribution and drainage during rainfall improved daily drainage at Iowa and Ontario sites to within 6--7\% cumulative \parencite{Xian2017}. \textsc{WEPP} enhancements improved uncalibrated drainage (daily NSE 0.27--0.47, rising to 0.59--0.73 calibrated) and reproduced storm and peak runoff around drainage installation \parencite{RevueltaAcosta2021,SAVABI1993}. \textsc{ADAPT} calibration reduced cumulative-drainage under-prediction from 14\% to 7\% in cold Minnesota \parencite{Mendez2004}, and \textsc{GPFARM-Range} simulated annual drainage acceptably (NSE $=0.76$) on drained pasture \parencite{Chen2016}. The \textsc{SIDRA} family, based on few parameters, has over three decades demonstrated predictive capacity for peak flows and midspan water tables and, when coupled (\textsc{SIDRA-RU}, \textsc{SIDRA-MAGE}), for catchment routing and solute transfer, with KGE $>0.81$ and a drain-flow-start median error of 5 days \parencite{Zimmer2023,Henine2022,LESAFFRE1988,Giraud1997}. Supporting these evaluations, automated logger--flowmeter systems measured drain flow to within 2\% of manual readings while resolving event dynamics \parencite{Kanwar1999}, and combined pressure-plus-discharge measurement diagnosed a tenfold decline in potential vertical flow in aged systems \parencite{Nishida2020,Gurklys2008}.

\subsection{Saturated versus Unsaturated Flow and Vertical Resistance}

A central mechanistic result is that treating drainage as purely saturated flow overstates discharge. A saturated-flow model over-predicted seepage and drainage time by roughly an order of magnitude relative to experiment, an analytical solution, and the SUTRA Richards-equation model, which agreed to within 20\% (seepage) and 40\% (drainage time); a vadose zone retaining substantial water above the phreatic surface persisted even at steady state, and performance depended strongly on capillary-fringe thickness, capillary rise, conductivity and drain elevation \parencite{Xin2016b}. Consistently, saturated--unsaturated drainage is governed by gravitational, pressure, hysteresis, capillary-barrier and air-pressure potentials, so saturated conductivity alone is an inadequate design basis; water-holding capacity, permeability and aeration jointly control efficiency, and preferential drainage can become discontinuous \parencite{Li2021}. Field diagnosis in Japanese rotational paddies showed that vertical, not horizontal, resistance limits the maximum drainage rate: potential vertical flow (2.1--5.7~mm~h$^{-1}$) was far below potential horizontal flow (6.1 to $>$79~mm~h$^{-1}$) \parencite{Nishida2020}. Accounting for vertical head loss improved paddy water-table prediction, differing from field profiles by 9--13\% versus 23--75\% for the conventional 2D solution \parencite{Talukolaee2022}. Neglecting unsaturated-zone buffering also underestimates response times: an analytical model retaining it matched a numerical model closely (steady heads within $0.6\pm2.0$~cm; real-case NSE $=0.80$ water level, 0.89 discharge), whereas ignoring the buffer distorted transients \parencite{VandeCraats2021}. Equivalent-medium and layered representations reproduce integrated outflow and mean water level well but can misrepresent internal dynamics, being least satisfactory where high unsaturated-zone conductivity permits fast vertical flow \parencite{Carlier2007}.

\subsection{Analytical Solutions for Drain Spacing and Water-Table Dynamics}

\subsubsection{Steady-State versus Unsteady-State Spacing}

Unsteady-state (transient) drain-spacing equations yield markedly narrower spacings than steady-state equations and are preferred where extreme short-duration water-table depths occur in high-storage systems \parencite{OOSTERBAAN1988}. In Iranian paddies, average computed spacing was 30~m for unsteady-state versus 55~m for steady-state equations, the unsteady solutions judged more suitable under variable rainfall \parencite{DarziNaftchally2014}. Steady Hooghoudt-based estimates matched observed spacings poorly in several settings, while among transient options the Glover--Dumm and van Schilfgaarde models were optimal for different rainfall durations \parencite{Moshayedi2020}. Design-criterion choice matters: for a 25~cm drop in 2 days a new equation estimated spacing closest to the actual 50~m (3.76\% deviation), whereas for longer periods the Glover equation was superior \parencite{Pali2014}. Nondimensional analyses show discharge following a common S-shaped curve against spacing ratio, with the median at a spacing-to-depth ratio of 2--5 \parencite{Nédélec2010}.

\subsubsection{Evapotranspiration, Recharge, and Sloping or Layered Aquifers}

Incorporating evapotranspiration and unsaturated-zone flow generally permits wider, more economical spacing. Analytical ET-aware solutions lower the water table faster and allow spacing increases of about 9--18\% (at $K=3$~m~day$^{-1}$, ET $=8$~mm~day$^{-1}$), applicable even to layered profiles \parencite{NIKAM1992,Singh2010,Upadhyaya2000}. Accounting for bare-soil evaporation widened calculated spacings by 23--74\% depending on texture (e.g. clay from 22.5 to 28~m) \parencite{AbdelFattah2005}. A spacing equation including horizontal unsaturated-zone flow gave up to 30\% wider spacing for coarse soils near an impervious layer, diminishing to $<$5\% when that layer lay 3~m below drain level, and agreed with HYDRUS-2D for non-submerged drains \parencite{Yousfi2014}. Bi-level solutions show the deep drain discharges more than the shallow one and that spacing is only slightly smaller than an equivalent conventional level system \parencite{Verma1998,Upadhyaya2000,Kankarej2023}. For sloping aquifers, water-table profiles become asymmetric with the divide shifting toward the lower drain, and hybrid finite-analytic solutions yield 12--17\% larger spacing than analytical ones \parencite{Singh2010,Upadhyaya2013,Hartani2001}. Dual-depth systems always remove water more effectively than single-depth systems (relative discharge 160--270\%), so retrofitting intermediate drains can raise effectiveness by 50\% or more \parencite{Kirkham1997}.

\subsubsection{Layered Soils, Ponded Drainage, and Benchmark Verification}

Analytical solutions for multi-layered and ponded-ditch systems have been verified against experiments, prior analytical work and MODFLOW, establishing that ponded drainage is strongly three-dimensional near the drains and highly sensitive to directional conductivities and anisotropy \parencite{Chakrabarti2020,Sarmah2015,SHARMA1991,Afruzi2014}. A plow-sole layer greatly restricts seepage and lengthens particle travel times, which can be reduced by staggered ponding and lower ditch levels \parencite{Chakrabarti2020,Sarmah2015}, and even a thin lower layer's conductivity significantly affects spacing \parencite{SHARMA1991}. Time-stepping linearisation of the Boussinesq equation outperformed earlier Werner and Baumann techniques \parencite{Lin2025}, and iteration schemes for non-homogeneous soils reproduced field water-table responses adequately \parencite{CHU1977}. On slopes up to about 0.15~m~m$^{-1}$, flat-land theory remains applicable for interior parallel drains, with deviations only at steeper slopes \parencite{FIPPS1989}; cross-model verifications show water-table shape-factor differences below 4\% for flat lands, with numerical instability for steep slopes \parencite{LORRE1994,Nédélec2010}. Critiques note, however, that a single linear-diffusion model cannot describe all stages of drawdown, since flow is radial near the tubes at early time and quasi-horizontal at late time \parencite{Kacimov2000}.

\subsubsection{Governing Equations and Single-Tile Flow}

The most physically complete descriptions solve the Richards equation, which fully represents saturated and unsaturated movement during drainage and subirrigation \parencite{TANG1977}. In sand-tank validation, predicted water-table drawdown and inflow/outflow rates and cumulative volumes agreed closely with observation, and, critically, approximate Boussinesq-based solutions agreed with the Richards and experimental results \emph{when a variable (nonconstant) drainable porosity was considered}; the Richards equation is more accurate, but corrected Boussinesq approximations remain useful for design \parencite{TANG1977}. Using an effective specific yield to mimic the vadose-zone effect, a new Boussinesq analytical solution removed large errors that arise when the unsaturated zone is neglected and gave good agreement with SUTRA and experiment for total drainage, though it predicted a quicker water-table decline and could not capture the dynamic drainable-porosity effect \parencite{Xin2016c}. Water-table recession following recharge begins only after vertical percolation ceases, with radial resistance appearing as a modified equivalent depth \parencite{VANDELEUR1959}, and classical transient-recharge Boussinesq solutions provide the theoretical basis for these dynamics \parencite{SINGH1980,Bouarfa2000}.

For flow to a single tile, analytical solutions closely agreed with finite-element simulations and, by replacing drain spacing with a variable \emph{region of influence}, became applicable to irregular or randomly spaced systems while eliminating the spacing parameter (RMS deviations 0.060--0.085~cm$^2$~day$^{-1}$) \parencite{Cooke2001}. Modified steady-state solutions of Ernst, Dagan and van Beers predicted transient midspan heads reasonably at 50--84~m spacings \parencite{SINGH1992}, and parameter-estimation methods agreed with Glover--Dumm to within 10\% for up to 15 days while providing high observed--predicted correlations (0.945--0.993) for the transmissivity-to-drainable-pore-space ratio \parencite{KUMAR1994}. A recurring obstacle is the drainable porosity: the Safaa equation avoids it via a new constant, giving spacing closer to actual and more economic than Glover's in the Nile Delta \parencite{FathAllah2025}, and an explicit minimum-cost ditch-spacing equation matched an optimisation program to within 7\% \parencite{Chahar2010}. Space-fractional Boussinesq formulations reduce to Glover--Dumm in homogeneous soil but track observed profiles more closely in heterogeneous soil, being most sensitive to the heterogeneity parameter, with elliptical forms fitting best \parencite{Mehdinejadiani2013,Mehdinejadiani2020}; a Bees-Algorithm inverse model gave repeatable conductivity estimates and the best simulation performance across datasets, improving on the Glover--Dumm criterion up to 34-fold \parencite{Mehdinejadiani2022}. Inverse frameworks using water-table-profile variation over time let all analytical models agree well with data, with variable effective porosity improving precision and the Topp--Moody model most accurate \parencite{Samani2007}; a Green-element solution resolved slope effects efficiently (2.5\% error at 30\% slope) \parencite{Onyejekwe1999}, and field determination yielded drainable pore space of 7.0--7.2\% and reaction factor 0.599~day$^{-1}$ consistent with reference values for silt loam \parencite{Bahceci2008}.

\subsection{Pipe-Scale Hydraulics and Structural Design}

The ease with which water crosses the pipe wall is characterised by the effective radius and entrance resistance. For Michigan pipes, 3--4-row regular-perforated pipes averaged 0.6~cm effective radius and 8-row reached 1.9~cm, while sand-slot pipes were lower; wrapping regular-perforated pipes in a knitted sock raised the effective radius dramatically to 5.7--6.0~cm and reduced entrance head loss to near zero \parencite{Ghane2022b}. Increasing the number of perforation rows was the most efficient way to raise effective radius, followed by perforation length, whereas width had minimal effect \parencite{Ghane2022b}. Sock-wrapped pipes allowed wider optimum spacing but higher initial cost than 8-row sand-slot pipes \parencite{Ghane2022b}; a companion study measured a sock-wrapped pipe's effective radius for the first time, validated the Dierickx theoretical method, and found the sock pipe functioned almost as a completely open pipe, with water entering only through bottom-half perforations under ponding \parencite{Ghane2022}. Numerical modelling calibrated to sand-tank experiments produced the first computation of entrance resistance for corrugated pipes with circular holes: because streamlines converge in the corrugation valley, resistance was higher (and delivery ratio lower) than in plain-wall pipes, and 51\% higher for circular holes than rectangular slots, making slots more hydraulically advantageous \parencite{Gaj2020}. Experimental validation of Dierickx's model confirmed that a screen envelope reduces the resistance factor and increases effective radius, that unenveloped pipes clogged almost instantly, and that smaller-diameter pipes with higher perforation density and a screen provided greater discharge capacity \parencite{Oyarce2017}. Bimodal (dual-mode) retention and conductivity functions predicted preferential flow to tile drains far better than unimodal van Genuchten--Mualem functions \parencite{Mohanty1997}, and a general discharge formula for improved drains under ponding was verified by experiment, field test and simulation \parencite{Tao2019}.

The structural counterpart shows that ductile yielding of non-perforated corrugated HDPE pipes is highly unlikely under field-traffic, soil and water-table conditions, with deflection within the 5\% limit; but circular-hole perforations substantially raise local wall stresses (5.6~mm holes exceeded yield strength shallower than 0.9~m in silt loam), whereas rectangular slots with width $\le 0.5w_v$ produce lower stress concentrations and lower failure risk---again favouring slots over holes \parencite{Gaj2021}.

Within-conduit flow with lateral inflow is a spatially varied flow (SVF) problem. An inverse-calibrated SVF model (Manning $n\approx0.02$) reproduced water-surface elevations, and ignoring local head loss under-estimated by up to 20\%; modified traditional relationships gave capacities two to four times the physically based SVF value \parencite{Bayat2011}. A generalized numerical solution for circular conduits established that Manning's equation alone is inadequate for drain capacity and that supercritical flow should be avoided \parencite{Graber2004}. Resistance depends on regime: reported Manning coefficients were 0.025/0.020 (GVF) and 0.035/0.030 (SVF) for two diameters, SVF increasing resistance 40--50\% over GVF \parencite{Kouchakzadeh2011}. Outlet studies identified ten flow patterns and non-negligible tail-water effects that can trigger free-surface-to-pressurised transitions \parencite{Nédélec2008}, and a 3D FLOW-3D model of an in-channel control module matched experiments to within 5\% while conserving mass \parencite{Abdurrasheed2019}. At watershed scale, a WASCoB routing toolbox reproduced flow and sediment through risers and berms, finding a 150~mm tile inadequate for design storms and recommending a 375~mm pipe \parencite{Gupta2019}. CFD of corrugated-pipe geometry (outside diameter dominant) matched sand-tank experiments to within 6\% \parencite{Wu2022}.

\subsection{Preferential Flow and Model Structure}

At field to hillslope scale, the representation of macropores and preferential flow is decisive for reproducing rapid drainage. Dual-permeability and dual-porosity models reproduced drainage hydrographs well (calibration $R^2=0.84$), the dual-permeability variant giving the best groundwater match ($r=0.98$), while a single-porosity model reached acceptable accuracy with far fewer parameters; all approaches failed to match low continuous dry-season discharge \parencite{Varvaris2018}. Dual-permeability formulations enable low matrix conductivities while still reproducing fast measured events through macropore flow, suiting complex topography and structured soils \parencite{Warsta2014,Salo2017}. In clay-dominated hillslopes, evapotranspiration and macropore flow dominate the water balance while Darcian matrix flow is negligible, though models can struggle to dry the profile in late summer \parencite{Koivusalo1999}. Adding macropore flow in MIKE SHE did not change total drainage but improved peak-flow simulation, with the drainage time constant (best value 5.6~h) most critical \parencite{Zhou2013}. Fully coupled surface--subsurface--tile models are recommended for field-scale dynamics, with discrete pipes most relevant for drain-flow dynamics and dual-permeability meshes reserved for detailed contaminant transport; representing the network as an equivalent porous medium or via seepage nodes can reproduce outflow almost exactly even when internal heads differ, offering a practical simplification for watershed integration \parencite{DeSchepper2015,Carlier2007,Boico2022}. Letting drainage occur simultaneously with rainfall and redistribution improves daily and peak-timing skill \parencite{Xian2017}.

\subsection{Tile Drainage Contributions to Streamflow}

\subsubsection{Annual and Event-Scale Partitioning}

Watershed-scale work establishes that tile drains route a large, measurable share of precipitation to streams. In an Ohio watershed, 21\% of precipitation (206~mm) was recovered through tile drainage, accounting for 41\% of annual and 47\% of monthly watershed discharge \parencite{King2014}. Equivalent-medium modelling of an Iowa watershed attributed 97~mm of tile drainage---13\% of precipitation and 39\% of streamflow \parencite{Thomas2016}. In the Boone River, end-member mixing (EMMA) and SWAT bracketed the tile contribution at roughly 46--54\% of discharge, SWAT estimating about 53.7\% (Boone) and 65.8\% (Lyons Creek) of annual yield \parencite{Schilling2019}. Snowmelt-dominated SWAT modelling found tile flow near 16\% of annual precipitation (about 37\% of yield), with tiling reducing surface runoff and increasing subsurface flow and yield \parencite{Rahman2014}. Field-scale export can be more tile-dominated: tile discharge accounted for $83\pm20$\% of Ohio water export \parencite{Williams2018}, and most discharge from an Indiana closed depression was delivered by tiles rather than surface flow \parencite{Williams2019}. Partitioning varies with hydrologic condition and area: in Iowa tile drainage averaged 30\% of streamflow during events but 61\% during non-event periods, event $Q_T/Q$ rose with drainage area while non-event $Q_T/Q$ fell, and annual $Q_T/Q$ rose log-linearly from 0.29 at 6~km$^2$ to 0.39 at 45~km$^2$ ($R^2=0.98$); the equivalent conductivity representing this scaled with drainage density from 164 to 530~m~d$^{-1}$ \parencite{Thomas2016,King2014}.

\subsubsection{Seasonal Dynamics}

Tile contributions peak when soils are wet and evapotranspiration is low. Ohio tile discharge was strongly seasonal (median monthly summer 0.9~mm versus winter 23.4~mm) \parencite{King2014}, and in the Boone River drainage dominated flow from March through June \parencite{Schilling2019}. \textsc{DRAINMOD} simulations indicated the largest baseflow effect from March to July---over 40\% more baseflow in drained watersheds and about 60\% more in April--June \parencite{Schilling2008}. Expanded drainage was projected to decrease winter flows and increase late-summer and fall flows \parencite{Rahman2014}. In cold regions tile flow tracked snowmelt with a seasonal absence from May to September \parencite{Kompanizare2024}, and water-table position relative to tile depth gates drainage---in Iowa a well-drained block's water table lay below tile depth 72\% of the time, steepening gradients during wet periods \parencite{Logsdon2020}.

\subsection{Modification of the Flow Regime}

\subsubsection{Low Flows and Baseflow}

A robust, repeated finding is that tile drainage elevates low flows and the baseflow fraction. Iowa modelling showed drained landscapes carried far higher baseflow (150~mm, 72\%, at 24~m spacing) than undrained (52~mm, 25\%), tile drainage primarily affecting the baseflow portion of the hydrograph \parencite{Schilling2008}. Ohio simulations found tiles greatly increased low flow while decreasing higher flows less, raised annual average daily flow about 5\%, and lowered the probability of the water table entering the root zone from about 50\% to about 5\% \parencite{Rathore2025}. Routing analysis reinforced that tiles increase low flows, decrease intermediate flows, and minimally affect the largest flows \parencite{Sloan2017}. In Latvia, drainage raised mean summer--autumn runoff by 132--186\% and winter low-water runoff by 53--286\%, with annual mean, minimum and maximum discharge up 46\%, 158\% and 26\% \parencite{Apsite2017}. Channelized Ohio headwaters showed far fewer zero-discharge days (8 versus 87.5~d~yr$^{-1}$ in natural streams), inverting part of the accepted headwater paradigm \parencite{King2009}.

\subsubsection{Peak Flows and Flood Response}

Effects on the largest peaks are consistently modest but scale- and condition-dependent for smaller ones. Routing modelling concentrated peakflow reductions at intermediate scales (100--1000~km$^2$), falling from 26\% at watershed to 11\% at basin outlet for one event, with negligible field-scale effect on the largest floods; placement mattered, draining headwaters producing the greatest reduction \parencite{Sloan2017}. Field-scale Iowa scenarios showed no significant difference in mean annual peak discharge for any of six soil types, indicating drainage chiefly alters pathway rather than the largest peaks \parencite{Sloan2016}. Ohio ATS simulations found tiles could enhance event peaks for sub-threshold or dry-condition events but reduce them for larger events, reducing streamflow in 23 of the 25 largest events \parencite{Rathore2025}. Under expanded tiling, smaller peaks became more frequent while larger peaks declined, steeper rising limbs implying reduced large-event peaks arose from reduced volumes rather than altered timing \parencite{Rahman2014}. Distributed modelling similarly found drainage decreases peak flow while increasing recession volume, reproducing observed behaviour with only 21\% of the area tiled \parencite{Fonley2021}.

\subsubsection{Flashiness, Drought and Long-Term Trends}

Flashiness responds to drainage in a soil- and slope-dependent way. A global meta-analysis of 464 watersheds found overall flashiness (Richards--Baker Index) similar regardless of drainage extent but diverging by setting: in flat watersheds ($<$2\% slope) medium/high drainage raised RBI, whereas in sloping watersheds low extent had the highest RBI, and drainage extent correlated positively with agricultural land use and clay content and negatively with slope \parencite{Zhou2025}. Field-scale Iowa simulations showed drainage decreased flashiness for low-conductivity soils and increased it for high-conductivity soils, homogenising response, with an optimal spacing near 15~m \parencite{Sloan2016}; Ohio modelling found overall flashiness decreased with tiles, minimised near 50~m spacing \parencite{Rathore2025}. Across 30 Nordic--Baltic catchments, flashiness related negatively to baseflow index and positively to drained share and intensity, and computing it from hourly rather than daily discharge increased it nearly threefold \parencite{Deelstra2014,Deelstra2014b}. At the regime scale, drainage significantly increased streamflow-drought duration and intensity while reducing frequency, each 10\% increase in tile-drained area lengthening drought duration by 0.03~d, raising intensity 12\%, and lowering frequency 0.10 events~yr$^{-1}$ \parencite{Adelsperger2024}. Indiana trend analysis found increasing low- and medium-flow trends with weakened precipitation linkage in heavily drained areas, suggesting a drainage influence \parencite{Kumar2009}, while a synthesis of seven Midwestern rivers concluded that inter-annual variability in streamflow, baseflow and N load was controlled primarily by current- and previous-year precipitation \parencite{Baeumler2020}.

\subsection{Scale Dependence, Homogenization, Travel Times, and Pathways}

Artificial drainage homogenises catchment response and reduces the scale dependence of hydrologic signatures. Non-tiled Iowa watersheds show decreasing area-normalized peak discharge and increasing baseflow ratio with scale, whereas tiled basins behave scale-invariantly; within-basin variability in recession coefficients declines as drained fraction rises, and the tiled/non-tiled distinction disappears above roughly 2200~km$^2$ \parencite{BolandBrien2014}. In Minnesota's Redwood basin, mean travel time fell 41\% (77 to 45~d), year-to-year variability was reduced, and travel time became independent of catchment size above about 200~km$^2$ \parencite{DaneshYazdi2016}; streamflow dynamics became more linear after 1976 despite little change in precipitation \parencite{FoufoulaGeorgiou2015}. Isotope and transit-time work in a UK catchment found short mean transit times ($<$1~yr, faster in artificial drains) and concluded that drainage homogenised distributed responses \parencite{DimitrovaPetrova2020}, and an Indiana watershed behaved as an effectively homogeneous unit describable by a single exponential travel-time distribution with a 2--3~d mean \parencite{Basu2010}. Even so, tile contributions remained detectable beyond 16{,}000~km$^2$ in the Boone River \parencite{Schilling2019}.

Modelling and tracers quantify how drainage shortens groundwater travel times. In Iowa, raising drainage density shortened mean travel time and raised the tile contribution to baseflow to an upper bound of 37\%, while increasing incision from 0.3 to 2.7~m lowered mean travel time near-linearly and raised tile contribution from 30\% to 54\% \parencite{Schilling2012}. In Bear Creek, mean groundwater travel time fell from 5.57 to 1.07~yr at high drainage intensity (versus 181.6~yr presettlement), short-circuiting flow to riparian buffers and cutting buffer residence time to 15.5--36.7~d, lowering nitrate-reduction effectiveness \parencite{Schilling2015}. Analytical solutions confirm that under tile drainage groundwater levels are lower and decline faster, total groundwater discharge to the river increases, and the tile contribution grows with density, though direct groundwater discharge during recession is reduced \parencite{Liang2015}; recession of heavily tiled watersheds is correspondingly more linear \parencite{Schilling2008}.

Runoff generation is governed by pathway-activation thresholds. In an Indiana closed depression, rainfall alone related linearly to cumulative flow with no threshold, but a clear threshold emerged when antecedent soil-moisture deficit and rainfall were summed, varying by pathway and landscape position; tile flow generally began earlier, peaked earlier and lasted longer than surface flow \parencite{Williams2019}. Isotopic work there showed tile celerity was controlled by antecedent wetness, with preferential flow more important when dry and stored pre-event water dominating when wetter, and groundwater the primary tile source (mean transit time to tile 245~d) \parencite{Williams2021}. In near-level Canadian vertisols, overland flow generally activated before tile flow, tile activation relating to maximum rainfall intensity and antecedent precipitation, with direct preferential flow into tiles uncommon \parencite{Kokulan2019}. Across 795 US--Canadian events, 43\% produced both surface and tile flow and 57\% tile flow only, surface flow contributing a median 12\% among dual-pathway events, with event-response types partitioning by texture and season \parencite{Macrae2019}. In Kansas tile-outlet terraces, baseflow contributed 38--51\% of discharge, antecedent conditions and storm size being stronger drivers than tile-inlet density \parencite{Stops2022}.

Tracer and geochemical studies partition tile flow into fast preferential and slower matrix components whose balance shifts with season and wetness. In a French field, dry-condition rainwater transferred directly through macropores whereas saturated conditions were dominated by pre-event soil water, with surface and tile drainage transferring 44\% and 20\% of annual effective rainfall and 65\% of infiltrating water not captured by tiles \parencite{Gaillot2023}; catchment monitoring identified slow saturated drainage and fast preferential flow, the fast peak contributing about 15\% of water and sediment fluxes \parencite{Grangeon2021}. Ohio master-recession analysis distinguished two reservoirs and apportioned annual tile discharge into 9\% quick-old, 39\% quick-new, 42\% slow-old and 10\% slow-new, showing new water routes through both reservoirs \parencite{Nazari2021}. Preferential flow accounted for a higher fraction in clay than loam and correlated with phosphorus loads \parencite{Pluer2020}, and Swedish modelling attributed autumn events to preferential flow and spring events to matrix flow \parencite{Salazar2011}. Flow and export are highly concentrated: in Ohio, surface runoff occurred $41\pm42$ and tile drainage $240\pm69$~d~yr$^{-1}$, 80\% of tile discharge in 21\% of the year; a 30\% cut in annual tile NO$_3$-N load would require treating tile flow 83\% of the time if targeting low flows but only 3\% if targeting high flows \parencite{Williams2018}. Managed-drainage monitoring reinforced this---287~mm (40.3\% of precipitation) of discharge, 94\% when the valve was open \parencite{Miller2022}.

\subsection{Watershed- and Regional-Scale Representation}

How drains and subsurface heterogeneity are represented strongly controls simulated catchment behaviour. Two grid conceptualisations of a Danish catchment produced drain contributions of 13\% versus 31\% despite both matching total streamflow within 0.1\% \parencite{Karan2021}. In seepage-node conceptual models, soil heterogeneity affected drainage-area simulations more than tile conceptualisation, while the reverse held at subcatchment scale; seepage-node models were about 35\% faster and better suited to automated calibration and unknown drain locations \parencite{Boico2022}. Distributed HydroGeoSphere models reproduced flashy heads and hydrographs better than MIKE SHE \parencite{DeSchepper2017}, and machine learning established that streamflow signatures (especially high-flow duration) predict simulated drain fraction across 73 Danish sub-catchments (test MAE $=0.126$) \parencite{Schneider2022}. Novel strategies embedding drainage in fully distributed integrated models reproduced streamflow without site-specific calibration, outperforming a calibrated SWAT model \parencite{Rathore2024}, while 3D CATHY simulations showed drainage raising cumulative outlet flow 3--22\%, lowering peaks and raising minima \parencite{Muma2016}. Water balances quantify drain contributions: subsurface drainage was the dominant source (about 68\% of total drainage) in a Chinese irrigation district \parencite{Zheng2025b} and a major precipitation fraction across Nordic catchments \parencite{Nousiainen2015,Turunen2013}. Equivalent-medium representations reproduced explicit tile simulations within 2\% of drained-volume targets \parencite{Thomas2016}, physically based cold-region modelling captured tile timing and the seasonal absence of summer flow \parencite{Kompanizare2024}, a modified ODE hillslope formulation improved recession and flood-threshold timing at drained gauges \parencite{Fonley2021}, and SWAT was tested against daily tile flow (NSE $=0.5$, PBIAS $=-1.4$\%) \parencite{Rahman2014}.

Distributed groundwater models, principally MODFLOW, are established tools for regional assessment, salinity-hazard mapping and optimisation. Calibrated models matched observed heads closely (e.g. 92\% correlation) and were used to design interceptor systems, quantify drawdown (average 0.53~m over three years) and predict discharge \parencite{Ghorbani2016,Mirlas2012,Mirlas2009,Mirlas2013,Mirlas2021,AbdElaziz2025}. Sensitivity analyses showed these models relatively insensitive to specific storage ($\pm$15\%) and aquifer conductivity ($\pm$50\%) but responsive to aquitard conductivity, and revealed existing drains sometimes intercepting only 9--30\% of groundwater flow \parencite{Mirlas2012,Mirlas2009,Mirlas2013}. Simulation--optimisation achieved equal or better water-table control with about 20\% less pumping \parencite{Rana2008}, a 90$^{\circ}$ alignment to groundwater flow was identified as optimal \parencite{AbdElaziz2025}, and coastal applications quantified sea-level rise raising drain discharge by 12.5--43.4\% \parencite{AbdElaty2019}. New MODFLOW subsurface-drainage (SDR) packages based on the Hooghoudt equation markedly outperform the conventional DRN package, reproducing steady-state and experimental drainage (RRMSE $<$5\%) by calibrating only conductivity, whereas fixed DRN conductance caused large errors and simulated 16.9--55.7 times more drainage than DRN \parencite{Yang2022b,Han2025}; an entrance-resistance modification corrected over-predictions for synthetic-envelope pipes (NRMSE $<$3.18\%; NSE, KGE $>$0.9) \parencite{Qian2025b}. Such frameworks quantified regional salt-discharge increases of 38--54\% and vadose-zone salt reductions exceeding 90\%, a critical drainage-to-irrigation ratio near 10--11\%, and a 1~m appropriate grid resolution \parencite{Yang2022b,Han2025}. Regional nitrate mapping showed high-drainage areas tend to have moderate-to-low groundwater reduction because drainage bypasses reducing zones \parencite{Hojberg2017,Hansen2019}.

\subsection{Controlled and Managed Drainage}

Controlled and managed drainage strongly modulate outflow volume and timing. In Sweden, controlled drainage reduced total outflow by 73--95\% relative to conventional drainage, concentrated more than 60\% of daily outflow in the 0--0.5~mm class, and produced lower retention constants and near-zero final storage \parencite{Wesström2003}. Raising the drainage outlet slowed water-table recession by 29\% and 62\% in two site pairs, confirming the effect was not seasonal \parencite{Saadat2017}. Agricultural drainage-water management in Ohio was most effective during low-intensity events and dry antecedent conditions, the valve open on average 18.6\% of each three-day event yet passing 96\% of discharge, though average groundwater and soil-moisture indicators did not differ significantly from free drainage \parencite{Miller2022}. Netherlands subsurface water-infiltration systems significantly influenced water-table depth, reducing fluctuations at four of five sites \parencite{vandeCraats2025}. Cross-drain spacing tunes recession, being most effective at 20~m (reducing recession 13--32\%) and negligible by 40~m \parencite{Vaiciukynas2015}, and trenchless installation held groundwater 0.14--0.25~m higher than trench-installed sections in 60--90\% of observations \parencite{Salo2019}. Method choice matters for evaluating such experiments: paired-watershed, BACIP and distribution-based approaches can yield differing conclusions, a beta distribution deemed most appropriate in one Minnesota dataset \parencite{Pagliari2021}. Broader analyses established controlled drainage's water-quality benefits---raising groundwater tables 19--35~cm and reducing annual outflow 35--52~mm and nitrate losses 15--22~kg~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Kesicka2023}---while lateral seepage explained 96\% of the drainage reduction, so ignoring seepage severely over-predicts outflow \parencite{Youssef2021,Saadat2018,Österholm2015}. Bibliometric analysis of 462 articles confirms controlled drainage's emergence as a leading multidisciplinary water-quality practice \parencite{Kesicka2023b}, and emerging model-predictive-control and drainage-water-recycling tools extend capabilities toward real-time water-table management \parencite{Ikonen2023,Reinhart2020,VandeCraats2021}.

\subsection{Drain Hydraulics, Sediment, Solute, and Specialised Settings}

At the pipe scale, network pressurization introduces storage and delayed secondary peaks. In France, pressurization of the collector network during intense rainfall limited and sometimes reversed discharge, temporarily storing recharge as the water table rose and generating a four-stage sequence that increased the lag to the catchment outlet to 10--25~h \parencite{Henine2010}; a coupled groundwater--pipe model reproduced this, with secondary peaks 11--30~h after rainfall and storage of 11--51~m$^3$ (38--46\% of stored recharge) \parencite{Henine2014}. Drain flow is governed by the system parameter $\sigma=K/(f^2L^2)$, maximum flow rising from 0.025 to 0.56~mm~h$^{-1}$ as $\sigma$ increased \parencite{Bouarfa2000}. Combined tile/mole systems modelled with SEEP/W reproduced discharge well ($R^2=0.811$), a tile-only system being least effective (63\% less drain discharge than the best combined system) \parencite{Tuohy2018}, and drain-flow spatial patterns were driven by topography and geology \parencite{Mahmood2023}. Component-scale studies show tile risers and blind inlets altered flow duration without necessarily changing discharge frequency, blind-inlet infiltration declining to an 8--10-year service life \parencite{Williams2020,Williams2019}; blind or gravel inlets substantially reduced sediment and phosphorus losses (TSS 14.4 versus 40.4~kg~ha$^{-1}$~event$^{-1}$) with a flow-capacity/sediment-control trade-off among designs \parencite{Feyereisen2015b,Li2017b}. In recycled-concrete media, permissible flow fell up to 73\% at 4\% fines \parencite{McCulloch2017}, sheet-pipe drainage drained about 45\% more water with 55\% lower percolation \parencite{Tamura2021,Arif2021}, and two distribution pipes could raise 10-day cumulative infiltration in saturated buffers by up to 133\% \parencite{Jaynes2019b}. Deep-percolation recharge has long been recognised as part of the drainage water balance \parencite{BOOKMAN1959}.

Because streamflow pathways carry material, hydrologic partitioning shapes sediment and solute export. In dual-drainage French fields, mean suspended-sediment yield was 0.79~t~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$, 89\% from surface drainage, tile drainage contributing 46\% and 21\% of runoff but only 9\% and 11\% of sediment \parencite{Gaillot2023b,Grangeon2021}. A sediment-connectivity index captured surface sources well but not subsurface-tile-controlled vertical flow \parencite{Calsamiglia2018}. Solute transport tended toward chemostatic behaviour dominated by antecedent conditions and storm size \parencite{Stops2022}, tile drainage delivered elevated antibiotic-resistance genes correlated to flow rate \parencite{Rieke2018}, and converting surface runoff to subsurface drainage did not necessarily improve water quality, a drainage-type raingarden retaining only 36\% of flow and sometimes exporting more nutrients than it received \parencite{Xu2022}. Beyond humid croplands, subsurface drains lowered surface electrical conductivity and suppressed resalination in saline Chinese soils \parencite{Yan2013,Chang2012}, passive drains stabilised Ethiopian gully banks (cutting bank retreat 52--84\% and soil loss about 70\%) \parencite{Zegeye2021}, and under-phreatic permeability increased downstream in a Burkina Faso valley bottom, so no single infiltration process described the toposequence \parencite{Keita2014}. Coupled SC-EMMA and recession frameworks improved dissolved-phosphorus prediction \parencite{Nazari2021}, and pipe-flow modelling feeds edge-of-field practice design: balancing nitrate removal against travel time gave optimal saturated-buffer widths smaller than current widths, implying removal could be 50--120\% greater at four of six sites, with 57\% uncertainty dominated by the removal coefficient \parencite{McEachran2020}.

\subsection{Data-Driven, Remote-Sensing, and Geostatistical Methods}

Data-driven methods frequently match or outperform process models for the specific outputs they are trained on while requiring less data and computation. Artificial neural networks predicted real-time water-table control accurately (minimum RMSE 60.6--73.8~mm), most best networks being small \parencite{Yang2000}, and predicted saturated conductivity more accurately than pedotransfer functions ($R^2$ up to 0.96), with ANN-based designs closer to measured-data designs and trainable on about 50 measurements \parencite{Ellafi2021}. Random forests outperformed multiple linear regression for agronomic prediction (water-table depth $R^2=0.95$ versus 0.28), capturing nonlinear effects and locating an optimal 7~m spacing \parencite{Djiemon2019}. An Attention U-Net surrogate with adaptive weighted loss cut $L_2$ error about 43\% and reached test $R^2$ of 0.997--1 for head, drainage volume and salt discharge while drastically reducing computation \parencite{Lei2026}. For salinity, a back-propagation network predicted drainage-effluent salinity better than SALTMOD, but SALTMOD remained superior for root-zone salt balance, the ANN being a faster ``gray box'' on minimal input \parencite{Sarangi2006}. Clustering and discriminant analysis delineated management zones, identifying elevation and subsurface drainage as significant discriminators of NO$_3$-N leaching zones (86\% accuracy) \parencite{Bakhsh2005b,Bakhsh2007b}.

Remote sensing combined with deep learning now maps tile-drained extent at regional to national scales with accuracies commonly exceeding 85--96\% and totals within a few percent of census estimates. AgTile-US reported a national tile area of 22.34~Mha ($-0.35$\% versus USDA) with 86.03\% overall accuracy \parencite{Valayamkunnath2020}, and the SEETileDrain random-forest product achieved 0.96 testing accuracy and F1 of 0.90, estimating 185{,}549~km$^2$ (about 9.4\% below USDA-NASS) \parencite{Wan2024}; a Danish selective ensemble predicted drained area over 49.4\% of the region ($\kappa=0.526$) \parencite{Moller2018}. Convolutional segmentation networks reliably delineate visible drain lines: a U-Net exceeded 92\% accuracy including 200~km beyond training data, drains most visible in spring under high radiation \parencite{Redoloza2023}, and Ohio and ensemble segmentation models outperformed Sobel/Canny edge detectors and the Hough transform, with resolution-dependent performance peaking near 400~m/256~pixels \parencite{Song2021,Woo2023}. Earlier decision-tree classification estimated 50\% drained area with 43--44~m sub-watershed spacing \parencite{Naz2009}. Ancillary methods infer drainage-relevant surface conditions: SAR discriminated drained from undrained corn by contrast \parencite{Meunier2001}, Landsat SWIR estimated soil-water content ($r=0.94$) \parencite{Rijal2013}, SAVI estimated cover-crop biomass at 86--107\% of ground values \parencite{Xu2018}, and high-resolution thermal imagery can detect drain lines, though subsurface drainage remained comparatively under-studied \parencite{Khanal2020,Zhang2010}.

Kriging-family methods are consistently the most reliable interpolators for the spatially variable soil properties governing drain spacing. In the Nile Delta, hydraulic-conductivity variation was isotropic with a 5.6~km range, kriging cross-validation giving low errors, and kriged conductivities recommended as design values \parencite{GALLICHAND1991}; a 600~m grid retained nearly all information and a 900~m preliminary survey fixed the optimal density \parencite{GALLICHAND1992}. For clay content no method showed important bias, cokriging marginally best \parencite{GALLICHAND1993}. Accounting for variability has quantified value: measured water-table depth underestimated drainage need by about 40\% and measured salinity overestimated by 59\%, while kriged conductivity widened spacing about 5\% and saved 17{,}155~m of pipe while reducing sample size three- to sevenfold \parencite{Moustafa1998b}; neglecting $K_{sat}$ variability could bias design spacing by $-27$\% to $+3$\% \parencite{Moustafa2000}. The covariance variogram proved superior to the semi-variogram for clay soils, and subsurface drainage itself altered spatial variability, the correlation range increasing about 29\% four years after installation \parencite{Moustafa1998}. Ordinary point kriging suited $K_{sat}$ and soluble salts in Turkey (horizontal correlation 710--1130~m) \parencite{Akis2015,Sepaskhah2004}.

\subsection{Sensitivity, Calibration, and Uncertainty}

Formal analyses converge on saturated hydraulic conductivity and evapotranspiration estimation as the dominant sources of sensitivity and uncertainty. \textsc{DRAINMOD} was most sensitive to vertical conductivity of the restrictive layer and lateral conductivity of the deepest soil layer, parameter interactions appearing only under likelihood weighting \parencite{Wang2006}. Conductivity estimation method produced ``tangible'' differences in simulated water-table fluctuations \parencite{NabiSichani2012}, and the van Genuchten shape parameter $n$, lateral conductivity and depth to the impermeable layer ranked as the leading sensitive parameters in HYDRUS inverse modelling, where monitoring discharge together with water-table depth was sufficient (NSE up to 0.83--0.95) \parencite{Sedaghatdoost2018}. GLUE/extended-FAST frameworks showed annual predictions within confidence bounds while exposing interaction effects invisible to one-at-a-time analysis \parencite{Wang2006}. Multi-objective sensitivity analysis of 17 parameters found daily drainage flow and relative corn yield most sensitive to drain spacing and depth \parencite{Timalsina2023}, the soil-water control volume was most influential for drainage-water-recycling design \parencite{Reinhart2020}, and parameter uncertainty propagated materially into predicted root-zone salinity and crop yield, motivating probabilistic design \parencite{Alzraiee2013,Alzraiee2013b}.

The construction and optimisation of objective functions strongly affects identifiability. A genetic algorithm generally outperformed PEST, combining discharge, water-table depth and salinity being best, though discharge with water-table depth alone already gave excellent results \parencite{Sedaghatdoost2019}. Variational data assimilation---the first for SIDRA-type models---identified $K_{sat}$ as most influential, selecting only 4 of 11 parameters, and cut the five-year cumulative-volume discrepancy to $<$1\% versus 10\% for a prior method \parencite{Chelil2022b}. Regional single-objective calibration in California produced identifiable parameters and predicted water-table variation well (RMSE 2.76~m), multi-objective calibration revealing trade-offs between water-table and pumping predictions, with drainage predictions more sensitive yet largely independent \parencite{Schoups2005}. The review literature concludes that no universal model exists, that automatic calibration is preferable because manual calibration neither guarantees global optima nor supports uncertainty estimation, and that integrated multi-model use is often more practical \parencite{Singh2019b}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Measurement Protocols}

The choice of goodness-of-fit metric materially changes apparent performance and even model ranking, so metric selection is itself a substantive modelling decision. Across 14 Illinois datasets the Kling--Gupta efficiency consistently outperformed the Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency, with the same behaviours recurring across every dataset, while the heteroscedastic maximum-likelihood estimator ranked between them but depended strongly on the error structure, producing a ranking reversal for 4 of 14 datasets under one scenario \parencite{Bruhn2025}. In a paired storm-runoff example, simulations grossly over-predicted all 10 events, yet this was not reflected by $R^2$ or standard error; a regression-based test (intercept $\ne0$, slope $\ne1$) instead deemed the model unacceptable, and RMSE and mean bias error better reflected the discrepancies \parencite{STONE1995,SAVABI1995}. Curve-number estimation for 12 Ohio fields likewise showed correlation and NSE varying widely by method, the complacent--violent method giving the best fit and largest-event prediction \parencite{Wessel2022}.

Performance evaluation extends to the field protocols generating the validation data. \textsc{DRAINMOD} achieved good monthly agreement across five soil datasets, with ROSETTA-estimated $K_s$ driving it as accurately as measured values in coarse-textured soils (drainage errors generally $<$2\%) \parencite{Salazar2008}. \textsc{SIDRA-RU} simulated discharge accurately, especially on silty soils (calibration KGE$'$ 0.44--0.83), a split-sample test confirming national-scale robustness \parencite{Jeantet2021}, and HYDRUS-2D reproduced drainage nitrate very well (efficiency 0.6--1.0), estimating the grey water footprint within about 12\% of field values versus up to 3960\% error for a tier-1 method \parencite{Naderi2025}. For phosphorus-load monitoring, all sampling strategies underestimated load, but flow-proportional designs stayed within $-0.2$ to $-7.2$\% while time-proportional designs worsened to $-51$\%, giving more accurate estimates with fewer samples \parencite{Dialameh2022}. A recording ultrasonic flow meter achieved very high calibration quality ($R^2$ up to 0.9993) with $Q(h)$ uncertainties of 0.1--0.4\% \parencite{Enamorado2007}, while a continuously deployed bromide sensor under- and over-reported concentrations, post-hoc bottle measurement outperforming real-time deployment \parencite{deOliveira2023b}. Non-intrusive geophysics mapped as-built golf-green tile, and both large-ring and small-core methods were valuable for relative comparisons, infiltration being 5--65 times larger in trench backfill than midway between trenches in Swedish clays \parencite{Freeland2014,Messing2006,BUCKLAND1986}.

Accurate discharge measurement underpins model validation and conservation assessment. For a 45$^\circ$ V-notch weir in AgriDrain structures, the fitted $Q=0.011H^{2.28}$ ($R^2=0.992$) held with standard deviations of 0.1--2.9\% of the mean, valid to 16.5~cm depth \parencite{Christianson2019}; a metal-edge V-notch gave $Q=0.749H^{2.25}$ ($R^2=0.999$), higher than the AgriDrain equation \parencite{BinShokrana2021}, and a 22.5$^\circ$ weir's 45$^\circ$ chamfer gave the strongest fit ($R^2=0.9981$) \parencite{Napierala2023}. Because V-notch equations fail once flow overtops, a compound-weir equation estimated overflow within MAPE $<$5\% whereas the combined V-notch-plus-rectangular method under-estimated by over 15\%, materially biasing nutrient-load estimates diverted to a saturated buffer \parencite{Katuwal2024}. Adjustable damming regulators were calibrated for different flow ranges \parencite{Popek2021}, and a low-cost vision-based method detected outlet flow within 12\% of ground truth, releasing an open-source application \parencite{Young2023}.

Aggregative indicators and empirical regressions provide a complementary, policy-facing layer. A global compilation of 60 hydraulic-retention-time--nitrate-removal regressions showed strong within-study linearity but wide between-study variability, slope correlating positively with temperature and negatively with inflow N, and yielded generalised median models (NRE $\approx2.9\times$HRT$+0.37$ overall; $3.2\times$HRT$+0.18$ for a tile proxy) \parencite{Christianson2025c}. A soil quality index was unaffected by tillage or drainage but correlated with corn yield ($R=0.62$), $K_{sat}$ the key physical indicator \parencite{Nakajima2015}, while phosphorus-loss indicators and the Phosphorus Index are framed as source $\times$ transport aggregations expected to be directionally correct against water-quality data \parencite{Reid2018,Zhang2018}, and a riparian-quality index correlated with an independent measure, width alone correlating most strongly \parencite{Terrado2015}.

\subsection{Design Applications and Field Outcomes}

The validated models have been applied extensively to quantify design trade-offs. Narrower spacing and shallower drains improve water-table control and aeration: SEW30 fell sharply from 3461 to 450~cm~day across configurations as drains were made shallower and closer in Iranian paddies, a 0.65~m depth with 30~m spacing providing desired wet-season control \parencite{DarziNaftchali2013,DarziNaftchally2014}. Design analyses balance aeration against drainage volume, with SEW30 rising and drainage volume falling with wider spacing and mild-to-severe aeration risk at 75~m but not 25~m \parencite{Verma2014,Hirekhan2007}. Optimum spacings for trafficability on Illinois claypan soils were 10--13~m \parencite{MOSTAGHIMI1989}, and in South Africa target water tables of 1.0--1.5~m required 25--40~m spacing in clay versus 55--70~m in clay loam, confirming the closer-spacing-for-lower-$K_{sat}$ rule \parencite{Malota2015}. Shallow drainage cut Iowa subsurface drainage by 8.4\% \parencite{Zhou2013,Mendez2004}. Climate-change simulations consistently project higher future subsurface drainage (about 14--80\% depending on model and precipitation) concentrated in winter, with implications for nitrate loss, while recommended designs still maintained relative yields above 80\% \parencite{Singh2009,Chen2016,Feng2018}. Supporting field studies established the reliability of the measurement and property-estimation methods underpinning this modelling: drain-derived conductivity and drainable porosity agreed with independent methods \parencite{BUCKLAND1986}, Rosetta and RETC reproduced measured conductivity and retention accurately \parencite{Malota2022,Malota2024}, cover crops reduce drainage by a robust $\approx30$~mm \parencite{Meyer2019}, and long-term drainage intensity measurably alters soil physical properties, carbon accumulation and workability, with $\le12$~m spacing protecting subsoil from compaction \parencite{Omonode2025,Mante2022,Mante2018,Frison2009,Roy2020}. Together these results demonstrate that the hydrologic and streamflow signature of tile drainage---accurate monthly and annual water balances, elevated low flows and baseflow, damped intermediate flows, modest large-flood effects, shortened and homogenised travel times, scale-dependent flashiness and peak response, and quantifiable design trade-offs---is both mechanistically explicable and reproducible across observational and modelling lines of evidence \parencite{Ahmadi1995,HASEGAWA1994,GALLICHAND1992b,MCCARTHY1992,PalaciosVélez2004,Khan2007,Prathapar1996,Ghorbani2015,Skaggs2017,Singh2006,Singh1999,Gaj2022}.

### Material: Cover Crop Nitrate Loss Mitigation

\subsection{Overview}

A large body of plot-, field-, watershed-, and regional-scale evidence converges on a consistent picture of how agronomic management governs nitrate-nitrogen (NO$_3^-$-N) concentrations and loads in agricultural subsurface (tile) drainage, with cover crops---particularly fall-planted winter cereal rye (\emph{Secale cereale})---emerging as one of the most consistently effective in-field practices. Across the U.S. Corn Belt and comparable humid systems in Canada, Europe, and Asia, drainage nitrate loss is driven jointly by nitrogen (N) input, crop and rotation choice, the amount and seasonal timing of drainage flow, and soil processing of N, so that no single practice is universally decisive but several act in predictable directions \parencite{Dinnes2002}. Under conventional maize and maize--soybean systems, flow-weighted NO$_3^-$-N concentrations commonly exceed the 10~mg~L$^{-1}$ drinking-water standard: more than 70\% of site-years in one twelve-state synthesis exceeded it \parencite{Liu2021}, and corn, corn--soybean, and soybean site-years averaged 14.0, 13.5, and 12.1~mg~L$^{-1}$ across a continental database \parencite{Hertzberger2019}.

Several headline findings are well supported. First, drainage nitrate concentration and load increase with N application rate, while yield plateaus near the economic optimum, so that reducing rate toward the agronomic/economic optimum lowers loss with modest yield penalty \parencite{Jaynes2001,Helmers2012b,Zhao2016,Hertzberger2019}. Second, cover crops---especially winter rye---and perennial or diversified rotations are among the most consistent and largest in-field reducers of drainage nitrate, frequently cutting loads by one-third to more than one-half, with measured multi-year concentration reductions on the order of 25--50\% and simulated regional drainage-N reductions near 40--45\% \parencite{Ruffatti2019,OBrien2022,PrezaFontes2021,Daigh2015b,Gentry2025,Kaspar2012,Malone2018,Feyereisen2006b,Malone2014b}. Third, shifting N from fall to spring or split/side-dress timing, and using soil-test-based rate programs, reduces loss but with smaller and less consistent effects than rate or cropping-system changes \parencite{Randall2003,Dinnes2002,Jaynes2004}. Fourth, tillage exerts weak and inconsistent control over nitrate loss relative to precipitation, cropping system, and N rate \parencite{RANDALL1995,Weed1996,Randall2001}. Fifth, hydrology---the amount and seasonal timing of drainage, itself governed by precipitation---is the dominant control on interannual variability, with most annual loss occurring in spring \parencite{Randall2003,Bakhsh2010,RANDALL1995}. Finally, mitigation practices redistribute N among loss pathways (leaching, N$_2$O, NH$_3$), so evaluating them on a common environmental basis is increasingly emphasized \parencite{PrezaFontes2023,Menegaz2025}. The subsections below synthesize this evidence by management lever, giving central place to the cover-crop literature.

\subsection{Nitrogen Application Rate and Residual Soil Nitrate}

Drainage nitrate loss rises with N application rate, and this is the most consistently documented in-field relationship. In a 22-ha Iowa production field, yearly nitrate mass loss was significantly greater for a high-N treatment (48~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$) than for medium (35) and low (29) treatments, and concentrations ranged 5--45~mg~N~L$^{-1}$, exceeding the maximum contaminant level except under low N during soybean years \parencite{Jaynes2001}. In north-central Iowa, flow-weighted concentrations increased with rate in both continuous corn and corn--soybean systems, four-year average losses ranged 22--68~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$, and concentrations at recommended rates commonly exceeded 10~mg~L$^{-1}$ \parencite{Helmers2012b}. Early Iowa work found losses rose with rate (50--200~kg~ha$^{-1}$) but not linearly, identifying a single 150~kg~ha$^{-1}$ application as a favorable compromise between loss and yield \parencite{Azevedo1997}. Increasing N above the agronomic optimum yielded diminishing returns: at 168--252~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ in Illinois, maize yield differed little (15.3, 14.5, 15.1, 14.9~Mg~ha$^{-1}$) while cumulative loads (14.4--22.8~kg~ha$^{-1}$) were statistically similar \parencite{Li2023b}. A ten-percent rate reduction cut Illinois corn yield about 10\% but did not significantly reduce tile nitrate loss in one study, underscoring that rate response varies with site and hydrology \parencite{Gentry2024}.

Regional and database syntheses formalize these patterns. Across the United States and Canada, maize yield followed a quadratic response peaking at 9.3~Mg~ha$^{-1}$ near 193~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$, drainage N losses increased exponentially with rate, and yield-scaled N loss rose sharply once N surplus exceeded about 23~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$---identifying zero N surplus as a practical target \parencite{Zhao2016}. In the MANAGE drain-concentration database, N rate was the strongest predictor of annual NO$_3^-$-N concentration in corn site-years ($R^2=0.59$), with the $>224$~kg~ha$^{-1}$ category highest (19.2~mg~L$^{-1}$) and no yield gain above 150--224~kg~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Hertzberger2019}. A twelve-state analysis confirmed that higher rates raised yield, load, and concentration, that reducing rate mitigated loss but reduced yield, and that the most cost-effective high-rate scenarios also produced the highest losses \parencite{Liu2021}. A meta-analysis of $\sim$1{,}000 site-years found dissolved N load increased with rate in continuous corn but not corn--soybean \parencite{Christianson2015b}, and a 62-study synthesis reported each unit rise in fertilizer-N rate produced 0.12~kg~NO$_3^-$-N~ha$^{-1}$ loss, with rate explaining 27\% of nitrate-loss variability \parencite{Chatterjee2020b}. Historical measurements likewise scaled loads with rate---e.g., 41, 55, 93, and 180~kg~ha$^{-1}$ at 20--448~kg~ha$^{-1}$ applied \parencite{Ritter2015}.

Residual soil nitrate (RSN) links rate to leaching risk. Across eight states, RSN was minimally affected by rates below the economic optimum N rate (EONR) and increased sharply only above it, following a plateau-quadratic form; at EONR, RSN was lower for at-plant than split applications (42 vs.\ 62~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$), and RSN did not substantially rise until rates exceeded EONR by more than 30~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Bandura2025}. Long-term Minnesota data showed continuous corn accumulated up to 225~kg~ha$^{-1}$ RSN during dry years, then flushed to drainage in subsequent wet years \parencite{Randall2001}. Excessive fall N carried multi-year legacies: a very high fall rate produced the highest concentrations and losses each year and continued to affect nitrate, residual N, and crop response for more than four years \parencite{Jaynes2015}. Encouragingly, long-term Iowa monitoring showed corn yields rising 144~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ over three decades at a nearly constant rate while drainage nitrate stayed flat, indicating improved N-use efficiency without added input but no reduction in loss from yield gains alone \parencite{Clifford2023}. Modeling reinforces the rate--loss coupling: annual precipitation had the greatest effect on N leaching but fertilizer rate increased all N pools \parencite{Li2006}, yield plateaued while nitrate loss accelerated quadratically as spring N rose from 100 to 200~kg~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Malone2007b}, and the leaching breakpoint occurred above the agronomic optimum in both continuous maize and maize--soybean, with a larger buffer in the rotation \parencite{Pasley2021}.

\subsection{Nitrogen Timing, Placement, and Enhanced-Efficiency Products}

Shifting N from fall to spring or in-season application generally lowers drainage nitrate, though effects are smaller and less consistent than rate or cropping-system effects. In Minnesota corn--soybean rotations, fall-applied N without nitrapyrin consistently produced the highest concentrations and losses; flow-normalized losses ranked fall $>$ split $>$ spring $=$ fall+nitrapyrin, with reductions relative to fall of 18\% (fall+NP), 17\% (spring), and 13\% (split) in one study \parencite{Randall2003} and 10\%, 14\%, and 6\% (spring+NP) in a companion study \parencite{Randall2005}. Fall N produced significantly greater tile nitrate concentration and load than spring N under corn in Illinois \parencite{Gentry2024}, and spring or side-dress N reduced three-year mean flow-weighted concentrations relative to fall N while maintaining yield \parencite{Pittelkow2017}. Cited Minnesota work found spring application raised N-use efficiency by more than 20\% and reduced annual tile nitrate losses by an average of 36\% relative to fall \parencite{Dinnes2002}. Spring application at the guideline rate produced equal or greater corn yield than a higher fall rate with nitrapyrin \parencite{Vetsch2019}, and fall-versus-spring effects, while real, were often smaller than effects of weather, drainage volume, crop uptake, and rate \parencite{Liu2021}. Database analyses correspondingly showed out-of-season and pre-plant timings had higher concentrations (15.8 and 15.7~mg~L$^{-1}$) than at-plant and side-dress timings \parencite{Hertzberger2019}.

Split and side-dress applications give mixed results. Split fertilization reduced NO$_3^-$-N export at lower rates ($<167$~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$) but not at higher rates \parencite{Liu2021}, and split applications were not significantly different from non-split for annual flow-weighted concentration in a continental synthesis \parencite{Hertzberger2019}. Side-dress timing at V2, V6, or V12 produced no consistent difference in corn yield or nitrate leaching, with V12 identified as about the earliest reliable sensing stage \parencite{Jaynes2013}. In some cases split N raised loss: a midseason split treatment had consistently higher flow-weighted concentration and greater residual soil nitrate than a single postemergence application \parencite{Jaynes2006}, and at EONR at-plant applications left less residual N than split but showed greater in-season loss, a nuanced tradeoff \parencite{Bandura2025}. Split-applied urea modestly improved water quality relative to fall and spring anhydrous ammonia (10.0 vs.\ 11.7~mg~L$^{-1}$) without raising yield \parencite{Waring2022}. Variable-rate side-dress N informed by remote sensing lowered observed and simulated concentrations, averaging a 40\% reduction over 15 years attributed mainly to 33\% lower input and timing that avoided peak flow, while maintaining maize yield \parencite{Wilson2020b}. Modeling generally found reducing rate lowered loss with small yield penalties, and that timing affected loss more than yield but without a single optimal timing across sites \parencite{Jeong2018}; watershed simulations found reducing rate 20\% with a 40:60 spring/side-dress split cut nitrate load $\sim$18\% without yield loss \parencite{Singh2023}.

Placement and enhanced-efficiency products offer additional but variable benefits. Fertilizer injection and incorporation showed no significant difference from each other in one synthesis \parencite{Liu2021}, while incorporated and injected applications had higher concentrations than surface applications (14.2, 14.1 vs.\ 10.3~mg~L$^{-1}$) in another \parencite{Hertzberger2019}, and injection could increase loss when rate was high \parencite{Jeong2018}. A localized-compaction-and-doming applicator produced 16\% lower flow-weighted concentrations than a spoke injector (16.8 vs.\ 20.1~mg~L$^{-1}$) with similar yield \parencite{Bakhsh2010}. Nitrapyrin generally did not reduce drainage nitrate or improve yield \parencite{Vetsch2019,Pittelkow2017}, though including it with fall N reduced losses 10--18\% in Minnesota rotations \parencite{Randall2003,Randall2005}. Polymer-coated urea increased yield 20\% without drainage but did not consistently outperform non-coated urea for yield \parencite{Nash2015,Nash2015b}; a polymer-coated (ESN) product in Minnesota gave the most robust protection, lowering combined N$_2$O, NH$_3$, and NO$_3^-$ losses 47--49\% below urea and split treatments while maintaining yield, with the largest benefit in wet periods \parencite{Menegaz2025}. Summer fertigation with reduced manure rates cut flow-weighted concentration 16\% versus fall injection (47.7 vs.\ 56.8~mg~L$^{-1}$) and annual load (47.1 vs.\ 55.5~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$) without reducing silage yield \parencite{Gamble2018}. In Missouri, fall anhydrous ammonia with nitrapyrin matched spring anhydrous ammonia for yield and had similar or lower environmental N losses, making it an environmentally acceptable alternative \parencite{Kaur2024,Kaur2024c}. Watershed-scale DSSAT simulations showed the benefit of switching fall-to-spring N was largest when cumulative winter rainfall exceeded 500~mm \parencite{Banger2018}.

\subsection{Nitrogen Source: Fertilizer versus Manure}

Nitrogen source affects drainage nitrate, with manure generally increasing loss relative to inorganic fertilizer at comparable rates. Under continuous corn in Iowa, liquid swine manure produced significantly greater flow-weighted concentrations and losses than UAN with no yield difference, leading authors to recommend corn--soybean rotation for manure management \parencite{Bakhsh2005}; fall-applied manure with chisel plow gave the highest concentrations, while spring manure with no-till gave lower concentrations, though fall manure produced the greatest yields \parencite{Bakhsh2007}. In Canada, liquid-hog manure produced higher soil and drainage nitrate than inorganic fertilizer, with more samples exceeding 10~mg~L$^{-1}$, although yields were equivalent \parencite{Elmi2005}; a carrot study found only a small, nonsignificant manure increase with equivalent yield and quality \parencite{Gordon2005}. In Indiana, annually repeated fall-applied liquid swine manure produced the highest nitrate losses among cropped treatments, with time of manure addition controlling loss more than UAN timing/rate or rotation \parencite{HernandezRamirez2011}. Applying swine manure before both corn and soybean, rather than only before corn, raised concentrations and loads \parencite{Dougherty2020}, and fall manure in chisel-plow plots caused more leaching than spring manure in no-till plots \parencite{Rekha2011}. In syntheses, organic fertilizers boosted yields but increased NO$_3^-$-N losses relative to inorganic sources \parencite{Liu2021}, though one meta-analysis found organic sources raised yield without significantly greater dissolved N load \parencite{Christianson2015b}. Among sources, anhydrous ammonia had the highest maximum N$_2$O loss, ammonium nitrate the highest nitrate loss, and urea the highest NH$_3$ loss \parencite{Chatterjee2020b}. Modeling predicted manure gave lower leaching than UAN and spring lower than fall, with fall manure to both crops the highest long-term loss (37.2~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$) \parencite{Malone2007,Malone2007b}.

\subsection{Cover Crops: Drainage Nitrate Reductions}

Cover crops---particularly winter cereal rye---are among the most consistent and largest in-field reducers of drainage nitrate. The clearest and most repeatable signal is a reduction in drainage-water nitrate concentration. In a long-term Iowa plot study, a rye winter cover crop significantly reduced concentrations by 48\% over five years (somewhat less than the 58\% seen over its first four years), while an oat fall cover crop reduced concentrations by 26\%, about half as much as rye; neither significantly reduced cumulative loads because annual drainage varied among plots, yet both remained viable options for corn--soybean systems and rye stayed effective even after more than four years of annual establishment \parencite{Kaspar2012}. Comparable Iowa work found no-till and chisel plow with rye consistently decreased NO$_3$-N loss seasonally and annually relative to chisel plow, and even with low rye biomass reduced concentrations by more than 30\%; however, no-till with rye did not reduce loading relative to no-till alone because leaching was already low, and the authors concluded most tile-drained corn--soybean farms could reduce their environmental impact with a rye cover crop \parencite{Waring2020}.

Illinois and Iowa studies quantify large load reductions when rye is well established. In continuous corn in Illinois, split N plus rye reduced flow-weighted concentration and load by 35\% and 37\% relative to pre-plant N and yield-scaled losses by about 33\% without yield penalty \parencite{PrezaFontes2021}; a companion study found in-season N plus rye decreased leaching 37\% and, on an environmental-damage-cost basis, matched pre-plant N despite shifting losses toward N$_2$O \parencite{PrezaFontes2023}. Cover crops proved more consistent and effective than shifting fertilizer timing: added to spring and fall systems, rye cut flow-weighted concentration 39\% and 38\% and load 40\% and 47\%, reaching 61--64\% reductions in a wet year and averaging 66\% lower loading than a zero-N control \parencite{Ruffatti2019}. A no-till rye system reduced concentrations and lowered losses 59\% and 67\% relative to no-till with oat or no-till alone, altering the load--drainage slope without reducing yield \parencite{OBrien2022}. Rye after corn significantly reduced mean annual concentration and load under soybean, contributing to a 43\% decrease in cumulative four-year load (from 115 to 65~kg~ha$^{-1}$) \parencite{Gentry2024}. Continuous corn with rye had a four-year mean of 5.6~mg~L$^{-1}$, lower than rotation systems, with losses reduced in years of low-to-normal precipitation and high cover-crop growth \parencite{Daigh2015b}. Split N plus rye also gave the lowest dissolved N$_2$O and nitrate loads among fertilized treatments, cutting cumulative N load 56\% versus pre-plant N \parencite{Oliveira2025}. In two Indiana watersheds, cover crops reduced field-scale NO$_3^-$-N losses 58--99\% in most years, pooling to a 65\% reduction, and reduced NH$_4^+$-N losses 45\% \parencite{Vincent2025}.

Multi-year and northern/western-margin studies show meaningful but often statistically marginal cumulative effects that depend on establishment. Over six years in Iowa, rye reduced measured cumulative drainage 12.1\% and NO$_3$-N loss 20.4\%, but significant drainage differences were absent every year and significant loss reductions occurred only in 2011 and 2012 \parencite{MartinezFeria2016}. In Minnesota, winter rye after corn reduced discharge 11\% and nitrate-N loss 13\% over three years, reducing residual soil nitrate, discharge, flow-weighted concentration, and loss in favorable-weather years \parencite{Strock2004}. An Iowa land-cover comparison found rye did not significantly reduce annual nitrate loss but reduced concentration seasonally---rye--corn lower than corn from March to June, rye--soybean lower than soybean from July to November---though the largest reductions came from kura clover living mulch and perennial forage, which lowered concentrations below the 10~mg~N~L$^{-1}$ standard \parencite{Qi2011b}. In South Dakota, significant reductions in leached nitrate-N and total N occurred only in the one of three years with well-established, high-biomass rye \parencite{Bawa2023}. In a Minnesota corn-silage system receiving high-rate dairy manure, rye reduced spring soil NO$_3$-N 46\%, increased ground cover roughly fourfold, and lowered drainage concentration from 53 to 39~mg~L$^{-1}$ with effects persisting more than a year, but loads remained high (52~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$) and phosphorus was unaffected, so additional practices were judged warranted \parencite{Krueger2013}.

\subsection{Cover Crop Mechanisms: Soil Nitrate, Biomass, and Weather Dependence}

The mechanism underlying reduced leaching is assimilation of soil mineral N into cover-crop biomass during the fall--spring fallow window, which shifts nitrate out of leaching-vulnerable depths and reduces the residual pool available for transport. In Illinois, cereal rye and tillage radish accumulated substantial N before winter termination (rye 188.1--249.9; radish 131.9--226.8~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$), and fall-applied N without a cover crop left a greater share of soil NO$_3$-N at 50--80~cm (40\%) than with radish (31\%) or rye (27\%); cover crops shifted NO$_3$-N into agronomic depths (0--20~cm) and reduced it at environmental depths (20--80~cm), with low-C/N winter-kill radish reducing nitrate at depth while high-C/N overwintering rye better protected N against loss during wet springs \parencite{Lacey2015}. Several studies corroborate this directly: rye reduced residual soil nitrate in favorable years in Minnesota \parencite{Strock2004}, rye--soybean showed a smaller early-to-late-spring soil-nitrate increase than soybean alone \parencite{Qi2011b}, rye reduced mid-June soil NO$_3$ only in 2009 and 2011 (partly offset by higher N fertilization in the rye treatment) \parencite{MartinezFeria2016}, and the 46\% reduction in spring soil NO$_3$-N was the proximate driver of lower drainage nitrate in the manured Minnesota watershed \parencite{Krueger2013}. More broadly, plots with plants had lower soil and vadose-water nitrate than barren plots at 30--90~cm, confirming that living cover removes nitrate \parencite{Bosompemaa2021}. Beyond assimilation, rye altered soil biology: in South Dakota it increased microbially active carbon and water-extractable organic nitrogen relative to no cover crop, though SOC and most soil-health indicators did not change over three years \parencite{Bawa2023}; and long-term simulation of a Mississippi wheat cover crop showed net N mineralization and denitrification 15\% and 9\% higher, net mineralization averaging 21.1~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ greater, and autumn-to-spring denitrification 17\% lower \parencite{Yang2021}.

Cover-crop effectiveness is strongly biomass- and weather-dependent, and both biomass and N uptake are governed by weather and sowing date. Biomass and N uptake were low under poor conditions, weakening reductions \parencite{PrezaFontes2021}; an oat cover crop had limited, inconsistent effects, reducing concentration only in a high-biomass year \parencite{OBrien2022}. Cereal rye biomass varied 1.37--5.94~Mg~ha$^{-1}$ with biomass N of 23.5--45.7~kg~ha$^{-1}$, highest under late termination \parencite{Gentry2025}, and growth improved when seeding shifted from post-harvest drilling to broadcasting into standing crop \parencite{Waring2024}. Winter triticale captured 44--164~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ and reduced profile soil nitrate 33--53~kg~ha$^{-1}$, with 60--100\% of capture by mid-May and minimal residual N below 60~cm \parencite{Nance2007}. Biomass varied widely across sites and years---highest in 1998--1999 then declining in Minnesota \parencite{Strock2004}; much greater at Nebraska's ENREC than SCAL and highest under wetter, warmer conditions \parencite{Birru2023}; low in two of three South Dakota years \parencite{Bawa2023}. Minnesota modeling showed mid-September planting produced far more biomass (7.0~Mg~ha$^{-1}$ by 30 May) than 30 October planting (3.3~Mg~ha$^{-1}$) \parencite{Feyereisen2006b}, and aboveground N-uptake fractions in Iowa simulations ranged 0.78--0.87 \parencite{Malone2018}. The water-quality benefit is contingent on establishment: the South Dakota reduction was attributed to well-established growth in only one of three years \parencite{Bawa2023}, and successful establishment after corn was estimated in only one of four years in southwest Minnesota \parencite{Strock2004}. Regional modeling explained more than 95\% of site-to-site variation in simulated nitrate-loss reduction, with temperature during rye growth, precipitation, N rate, and corn yield the main drivers, rye being more effective in warmer, less wet southern-Midwest locations \parencite{Malone2014b}; however, a synthesized Iowa dataset did not support a strong direct biomass--response relationship, cautioning that biomass alone does not fully predict site outcomes \parencite{MartinezFeria2016}.

\subsection{Cover Crop Effects on Drainage Volume and Soil Water}

Effects of rye on drainage \emph{volume} are generally modest and frequently non-significant, so nitrate-load reductions are driven mainly by concentration reductions and cumulative water use rather than by large discharge reductions \parencite{Kaspar2012,MartinezFeria2016,Waring2020,Qi2011b}. In an Iowa lysimeter experiment, rye reduced May--July drainage 21\% relative to bare soil across three years and significantly lowered drainage at each pumping event and weekly soil-water storage, yet the 9\% lower annual drainage was not significant; the seasonal effect was attributed to enhanced spring water use, with May rye evapotranspiration averaging 2.4~mm~d$^{-1}$, 60\% higher than bare-soil evaporation of 1.5~mm~d$^{-1}$, so that soil-water depletion reduced drainage volume, decreased NO$_3$ load, and improved trafficability \parencite{Qi2010b}. A companion study reported average annual drainage of 327~mm (about 40\% of rainfall), roughly 55\% occurring March--June, with land-cover treatments including rye not significantly changing drainage volume \parencite{Qi2011b}. Rye maintained higher soil water storage than conventional systems and raised evapotranspiration without a yield disadvantage \parencite{Qi2011}. Drought-year evidence indicates rye does not deplete soil moisture at the cash crop's expense: across three sites in the 2012 drought, rye either had no significant effect on soil water or increased it (at Iowa ISUAG, soil-water content and storage rose, especially after termination), temporal variation explained most soil-moisture variability, and rye did not lower soil water and in places increased conservation \parencite{Daigh2014b}. Consistent with modest water effects, rye transpiration ranged 11--44~mm with a strong positive relationship to shoot biomass \parencite{MartinezFeria2016}.

\subsection{Cover Crop Management, Yield, and Harvested-Rye Systems}

A central, well-supported conclusion is that rye cover cropping generally imposes no consistent cash-crop yield penalty, particularly for soybean. Winter rye did not affect subsequent soybean yield in Minnesota \parencite{Strock2004}, had no effect on soybean yield or grain N uptake in an Iowa tillage comparison (lower corn yield being attributable to no-till, not rye) \parencite{Waring2020}, and corn and soybean yields in rye treatments were not significantly lower than conventional systems in another Iowa study, unlike kura-clover corn \parencite{Qi2011b}. Under projected climate change, average annual crop-yield differences with and without rye were only about 0.1~Mg~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Malone2020}, and long-term Nebraska simulations showed no significant corn penalty in continuous corn \parencite{Birru2023}. Documented exceptions concentrate in maize, drought years, and silage: in Iowa, rye reduced maize yield 6\% on average over six years, with the largest penalties in 2012 (34\%) and 2013 (22\%) \parencite{MartinezFeria2016}; corn-silage yield was 16\% (2.7~Mg~ha$^{-1}$) lower after rye in the manured Minnesota watershed \parencite{Krueger2013}; and some studies noted modest corn reductions \parencite{Ruffatti2019,Dougherty2020}, while many report no penalty \parencite{OBrien2022,PrezaFontes2021,Gentry2025}.

Management strongly modulates both biomass and N-loss outcomes. Earlier fall sowing is consistently more effective: Minnesota modeling showed rye sown 15 September reduced NO$_3$-N losses 11.1~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ (45\%) relative to a standard rotation losing 24.9~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$, whereas 1, 15, and 30 October sowings reduced losses only 7.8, 5.8, and 4.6~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$, with earlier kill dates also mattering \parencite{Feyereisen2006b}. Delaying spring termination increases biomass with limited yield cost \parencite{Birru2023}, and shifting termination $\pm$10 days affected rye biomass and corn yield more than soybean yield but had only small effects on annual drainage and nitrate loss \parencite{Chatterjee2025}. Rye benefits are robust to fertilizer timing: an Illinois study reported NO$_3$-N loss reductions of 43.6\% and 45.4\% for fall- and spring-applied N, with rye reducing tile drainage volume by roughly 21\% under both \parencite{Gupta2022}, and fall-applied N into a standing cover crop did not significantly affect corn-silage yield or N uptake \parencite{Lacey2015}.

For harvested-rye systems, fertilizing the rye boosts biomass and energy with only a small N-loss cost. In Iowa, applying 120~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ to harvested rye reduced simulated drainage N loss 54\% relative to no cover crop (and 18\% relative to unfertilized, unharvested rye) \parencite{Malone2018}. Across a broader regional analysis, later unharvested termination reduced N loss an additional 6\% (1.4~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$), harvesting 90\% of aboveground rye before soybean reduced loss more than 10\% ($>$2.0~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$), and applying 50~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ to harvested rye increased biomass more than 50\% while raising loss less than 5\% ($<$1.0~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$) \parencite{Malone2023}. Economically, annual pre-soybean harvested-rye biomass averaged 2.2, 3.7, and 6.4~Mg~ha$^{-1}$ across increasingly favorable scenarios, and fertilizing raised average producer revenue from \$276~ha$^{-1}$ at 0~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ to a maximum of \$468~ha$^{-1}$ at 140~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ (plateauing near \$465 at $\sim$120~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$), with 8.3~Mg~ha$^{-1}$ harvested biomass at 120~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$; energy output reached 145.2~GJ~ha$^{-1}$ (fertilized) versus 64.7~GJ~ha$^{-1}$ (unfertilized), with net energy ratios of 12 and 14, and fertilizing and harvesting still reduced drainage N loss \parencite{Malone2018}.

\subsection{Quantified Cover-Crop Reductions and Modeling Confidence}

Synthesizing across studies, simulated and measured drainage N-loss reductions cluster around 40--60\% when rye is well established, with concentration reductions of 25--50\%. Iowa modeling estimated that unfertilized rye killed without harvest reduced drainage N loss 44\%, and harvesting 20 days later without April fertilizer reduced it 56\% \parencite{Malone2018}. A DSSAT application in central Iowa simulated a 57.3\% reduction in cumulative tile nitrate loss under cereal rye \parencite{Chatterjee2025}. Multi-system Midwest modeling reported average annual reductions of 11.7--31.8~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ (treatment-specific 23.9\%, 42.5\%, 40.0\%, 30.0\%), noting these were conservative relative to field observations \parencite{Malone2014b}. Across 40 sites, simulated drainage N loss ranged 20.8--44.4~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ and cover crops reduced loss nearly 45\% \parencite{Malone2023}. Climate-change projections for Iowa are striking: rye reduced average annual drainage N loads from 47.5 to 18.9~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ under baseline climate (60\%) and from 63.6 to 17.0~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ under future climate (73\%), keeping loads below the 28.0~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ target for all six GCMs, the larger future benefit reflecting greater rye growth under higher temperature and CO$_2$ \parencite{Malone2020}. Long-term Mississippi simulation of a wheat cover crop showed nitrate leaching 20\% lower than winter fallow (68.2 vs.\ 84.8~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$), with autumn-to-spring reductions of 19.6, 18.6, and 8.2~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ in wet, normal, and dry years and benefits accruing mainly in the cover-crop growth window \parencite{Yang2021}. More broadly, modeling estimated a winter cover crop reduced tile nitrate yield 34\% at watershed scale \parencite{David2015}, cut predicted loss 31\% under pre-plant UAN \parencite{Malone2007b}, and combined with a 20\% rate cut and split N reduced load 32\% \parencite{Singh2023}; a winter rye cover crop could reduce Chesapeake N loading 11.2~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ \parencite{Woodbury2018}. Under future climate, cover-crop reductions were projected to persist but may not fully keep pace with rising baseline loss \parencite{Gupta2023}, and at watershed scale, cover crops on 35.5\% of cropland cut subbasin nitrate load 15.5\% and raised median N-use efficiency from 0.49 to 1.02 \parencite{Makhtoumi2025}.

Much of this quantitative synthesis rests on process-based models calibrated and validated against field drainage and nitrate data. In Illinois, DSSAT's CERES-Wheat reproduced rye growth, tile drainage, and nitrate-loss patterns for calibration and validation, though it did not replicate cover-crop effects on cash-crop yield well \parencite{Gupta2022}. A modified DSSAT/CERES-Wheat setup in Iowa reproduced nine-year cumulative drainage flow well ($r^2=0.87$, NSE $=0.82$) and simulated cumulative nitrate loss close to observations, though rye biomass was overestimated by 17.7\% \parencite{Chatterjee2025}, and Nebraska CERES-Maize/CERES-Wheat calibration was satisfactory \parencite{Birru2023}. The APSIM-based Iowa analysis replicated measurements well and yielded robust empirical relationships among drainage, NO$_3$-N, and precipitation \parencite{MartinezFeria2016}. The purpose-built RyeGro soil--plant--atmosphere model fit limited field data and, coupled with a stochastic weather generator, estimated probabilities of subsurface N-loss reduction, with hydrologic parameters more influential than N-cycling ones \parencite{Feyereisen2006b}. The RZWQM-based Midwest study described most of the observed nitrate-leaching difference between cover-crop and no-cover treatments and was conservative relative to field data \parencite{Malone2014b}, and the Iowa climate-change study identified rye as the first practice shown capable of meeting future drainage N-load goals without affecting cash-crop production \parencite{Malone2020}. These consistently conservative validated models reinforce the field evidence that fall-planted rye is an effective, weather-dependent, and generally yield-neutral practice \parencite{Malone2014b,Malone2018,Chatterjee2025,MartinezFeria2016,Yang2021,Gupta2023b}.

\subsection{Regional Aggregation and Gulf of Mexico Loading}

Scaling site results to the drained landscape indicates that widespread rye adoption could achieve regionally significant reductions in nitrate export. For the five-state region (Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Iowa, Minnesota), winter rye was estimated to reduce nitrate loss to the Mississippi River by approximately 20\%, with potential adoption across ten representative counties of 34.4--80.9\% of agricultural land, limited mainly by fall tillage after corn and non-corn/soybean land uses; under a conservative scenario, 2.534~million~ha of no-till corn--soybean drained cropland planted to rye would reduce N loss about 63.0~million~kg~N, a broader 7.662~million~ha scenario about 180.6~million~kg~N (the Mississippi-draining portion $\sim$153~million~kg~N, equal to 19\% of the 813~million~kg~N delivered annually to the Gulf), at competitive removal costs of US\$2.24--3.25 per kg N \parencite{Kladivko2014}. A statewide DSSAT analysis for Illinois found cereal rye reduced tile-flow nitrate-N loss and nitrate leaching 30.6\% and 29.4\% on average while reducing drainage and deep percolation and generally increasing evapotranspiration \parencite{Gupta2023b}. A five-state energy-cover-crop assessment estimated a region-wide N-loss reduction of 216~million~kg~yr$^{-1}$, corresponding to a 27\% reduction relative to the assumed baseline Gulf load, with nine hotspot clusters occupying 19\% of the area but contributing 48\% of the reduction and also providing 18.5~million~Mg biomass, 0.21~EJ of renewable natural gas, and 7.5~million~Mg~CO$_2$ of sequesterable biogas CO$_2$ \parencite{Malone2023}.

\subsection{Crop Rotation, Diversification, and Perennial Systems}

Cropping system exerts strong control on drainage nitrate, generally exceeding tillage and often rivaling N rate. Continuous corn consistently lost more nitrate than corn--soybean or perennial systems \parencite{Weed1996}, and had higher concentrations and losses than corn--soybean, leading authors to judge continuous corn environmentally unsustainable \parencite{Kanwar1997}. Maize--soybean rotations produced greater yield and lower yield-scaled N loss than continuous maize \parencite{Zhao2016} and shifted the leaching response with a larger buffer above the agronomic optimum \parencite{Pasley2021}. Changing a southeastern Indiana site from continuous corn to corn--soybean, reducing rate, and adding a cover crop lowered flow-weighted concentration from 28 to 8~mg~L$^{-1}$ over 14 years \parencite{Ritter2015}, and rotation changes reduced simulated tile nitrate loads more on silt loam than sandy loam \parencite{Ahmed2007b}. More diverse rotations delivered large reductions with maintained yield: a three-year rotation (corn--soybean--wheat/double-crop soybean) had a flow-weighted mean of 4.2~mg~L$^{-1}$ versus 8.0 for a control, with average load 4.9 versus 12.5~kg~ha$^{-1}$ and exceedance of 10~mg~L$^{-1}$ in only 1\% of samples \parencite{Gentry2025}.

Perennial and prairie systems are the most effective at limiting drainage nitrate. Prairie systems had concentrations $\le$1.4~mg~L$^{-1}$, 2--195 times below row crops \parencite{Daigh2015b}; restored prairie grass reduced losses to near rainfall levels \parencite{HernandezRamirez2011}; and perennial alfalfa and CRP plots had 0.3--4~mg~L$^{-1}$ versus 14--40 for row crops, with four-year Iowa losses of 217, 204, and 202~kg~ha$^{-1}$ for corn systems versus 7 and 4 for alfalfa and CRP \parencite{Randall2001}. Perennial plots had the lowest concentration and loading among treatments \parencite{Waring2024}. However, the benefit can be short-lived on conversion back to row crops: nitrate losses were low only in the first year, then similar to row crops as residual soil nitrate rose, though former-alfalfa treatments averaged 20\% less drain flow \parencite{Huggins2001}. Simulated novel perennials (intermediate wheatgrass, alfalfa) cut tile nitrate loads $\sim$91--92\% in perennial years, and converting all corn--soybean area to alfalfa or wheatgrass reduced watershed losses 78--81\% \parencite{Wilson2023}. Bioenergy perennials also reduce loss: strategically implemented miscanthus cut total N leaching and DIN export 19--26\% under future climate \parencite{Ferin2023}; replacing maize with switchgrass could reduce Chesapeake N loading 18.2--18.9~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ \parencite{Woodbury2018}; and bioenergy crops on marginal land slightly reduced watershed nutrient losses \parencite{Guo2018}. Diversified rotations, cover crops, perennials, wetlands, bioreactors, and drainage control were all recognized as complementary reducers \parencite{Dinnes2002}. Drainage often raised soybean and corn yield modestly (e.g., +6\% soybean, +8\% corn without N) \parencite{Cassman2025}, though on Fargo clay drainage sometimes reduced yield in dry years and corn--soybean consistently outyielded continuous corn \parencite{Acharya2019b}; rotation raised corn yield without changing leaching over six years \parencite{Bakhsh2005}, and organic rotations offered water-timing benefits with sometimes comparable yields \parencite{Logsdon2021}.

\subsection{Tillage and Residue Management}

Tillage exerts weak and inconsistent control over drainage nitrate loss compared with precipitation, cropping system, and N rate. Over an 11-year Minnesota study, drain flow was higher under no-till than conventional tillage, but nitrate losses were only slightly higher under conventional tillage, and higher no-till flow did not translate into higher nitrate flux \parencite{RANDALL1995}; an 11-year average showed moldboard plow and no-till had similar losses (43 and 41~kg~ha$^{-1}$), with cropping system affecting loss far more than tillage \parencite{Randall2001}. In Iowa, tillage only slightly influenced soil nitrate, no-till and ridge-till usually had the lowest concentrations and losses, and rotation had greater influence \parencite{Weed1996}; tillage had little influence within a corn--soybean rotation, and continuous corn had higher losses regardless of tillage \parencite{Kanwar1997}. Regional syntheses found conventional and reduced tillage did not differ significantly in drainage N load \parencite{Zhao2016}, though conventional tillage had greater discharge while conservation tillage had the highest concentration (16.1~mg~L$^{-1}$) \parencite{Hertzberger2019}, and modeling found tillage intensity had negligible effects on yield and N$_2$O, with greater intensity slightly reducing flow and N loss \parencite{Wang2016b}.

Where soil-health benefits of reduced tillage accrue, effects on nitrate remain equivocal. No-till improved soil organic matter, aggregate stability, and water-holding capacity in Michigan, but nitrate loads were lower under no-till only in some water years (9.6 vs.\ 18.3~kg~ha$^{-1}$ in one year, higher in another) and yields were lower under no-till \parencite{Masri2024}. No-till increased soil organic carbon (0.12~Mg~C~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$), reduced the EONR by 28~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$, and raised returns \$172~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Cassman2025}, and conservation treatments raised soil organic matter over the long term, with cover crops reducing concentration in both tillage systems (e.g., no-till 8.0 vs.\ 4.4~mg~L$^{-1}$ with rye) \parencite{Waring2024}. Residue and stover management had limited effect on nitrate: residue removal had little impact on concentrations or yields \parencite{Dougherty2020}, 50\% stover harvest reduced simulated drainage N loss only 1.1~kg~ha$^{-1}$ while post-emergence N timing reduced it far more \parencite{Malone2019}, and 38\% stover removal did not significantly change water quality but raised sediment and nutrient losses, offsettable with bioenergy crops \parencite{Guo2018}. Continuous corn with residue removal plus a rye cover crop achieved similar or lower nitrate losses than other row crops \parencite{Daigh2015b}, and reduced tillage had little effect on nitrate but increased dissolved-P loss in one Iranian study \parencite{Farahani2020}.

\subsection{Drainage Water Management, Controlled Drainage, and Irrigation}

Managing the drainage system itself---controlled drainage, subirrigation, and water-table control---reduces drainage volume and nitrate export, though with pathway tradeoffs. Simulated drainage water management reduced annual drain flow $\sim$56\% and drain-flow nitrate loss $\sim$46\% for both continuous corn and corn--soybean, but increased nitrate in surface runoff 3--4~kg~ha$^{-1}$, so net reduction reaching surface water was smaller (32--38\% when combined with reduced rate) \parencite{Ale2012b}. A subirrigation/controlled-drainage system in Ohio lowered deep-profile soil nitrate 46\%, raised corn and soybean yields 19\% and 64\%, and increased N uptake \parencite{Fisher1999}. In Missouri, free and managed drainage raised corn yield 15--21\% over no drainage, with managed drainage averaging a further 4\% \parencite{Nash2015}, though managed drainage did not consistently outyield free drainage across years \parencite{Nash2015b}. In Chinese paddy fields, raising the water-table control level in the first week after fertilization reduced total N losses $\sim$9.5\%, mainly via reduced subsurface outflow \parencite{He2019b}. Controlled drainage slightly increased corn yield and reduced flow, N loss, and concentration, but increased N$_2$O \parencite{Wang2016b}, and was repeatedly identified as a supplementary option where in-field management is insufficient \parencite{HernandezRamirez2011}. A caveat: on an existing field system, drainage water management did not reduce load because water moved laterally to an adjacent free-draining system \parencite{David2015}. In arid maize systems, demand-based irrigation with intermittent leaching (threshold $\sim$11~g~kg$^{-1}$ in the 60--100~cm layer) controlled salinity and sustained yield, whereas insufficient leaching cut yield $\sim$86.8\% over 12 years \parencite{Liu2024}.

In irrigated and specialty systems, water application governs nitrate leaching. Sprinkler irrigation produced lower total nitrate and dissolved P than flood and furrow irrigation and, combined with biochar, reduced concentration, making sprinkler an appropriate water-conserving practice \parencite{Farahani2020}; greater absolute nitrate loss under furrow than sprinkler reflected lower furrow efficiency \parencite{Hurtado2011}. In drip-irrigated mulched Florida vegetables, increasing irrigation volume and N above recommendations raised nitrate leaching 32--66\%, and drainage lysimeters and soil cores agreed while ceramic suction cups underestimated leaching \parencite{Zotarelli2007}. Soil amendments had localized effects: phosphogypsum decreased and refuse lime increased flow-weighted concentrations in some events, without significant differences in total seasonal loss \parencite{Hurtado2011}.

\subsection{Hydrologic and Climatic Controls}

Hydrology---drainage amount and its seasonal timing, driven by precipitation---is the dominant control on interannual and among-plot variability in nitrate loss. Flow-weighted concentrations rose sharply in wet years following dry periods, losses were lowest in dry years, and the tillage effect was small relative to precipitation \parencite{RANDALL1995}. Minnesota drainage averaged 38~mm during a dry period but 507~mm during a wet period; corn--soybean plots ranged from $<$50~mm and 7--18~mg~L$^{-1}$ in dry years to $>$350~mm and $\sim$35~mg~L$^{-1}$ in a wet year \parencite{Randall2001}. Loss is strongly seasonal: 62\% of annual drainage and $\sim$68--70\% of annual nitrate loss occurred in April--June in Minnesota \parencite{Randall2003}, with 71\% of drainage and 73--77\% of loss in April--June in a companion study \parencite{Randall2005}; in Iowa, $\sim$60\% of tile flow and nitrate leaching occurred March--May \parencite{Bakhsh2007}, and the March--June share of seasonal loss ranged 25--99\% across years, with spatiotemporal variability producing three- to fourfold differences even under identical management \parencite{Bakhsh2010}. Concentrations were significantly affected by seasonal rainfall and crop conditions \parencite{Bakhsh2005}, nitrate load and drainage were tightly correlated (0.86--0.99) while precipitation correlations were weaker \parencite{Masri2024}, and loads were linearly correlated with cumulative annual drainage, with management altering the slope \parencite{OBrien2022}. Regionally, at 148\% of mean precipitation drainage N loss reached 67.5~kg~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Zhao2016}, and wetter conditions were associated with greater dissolved N losses, though very high rates could overshadow precipitation \parencite{Christianson2015b}. Even with no N applied, nitrate loss remained substantial ($\sim$8.3~mg~L$^{-1}$), reflecting background mineralization \parencite{Waring2022}, and non-growing-season mineralization from soil and residues is an important source \parencite{Gentry2024}; most drainage and loss occurred outside the growing season in cool humid systems \parencite{Gordon2005}. Long-term monitoring is essential: short 5--10-year windows can show apparent trends differing from the full record \parencite{Clifford2023}, tile systems respond within 6--14 months but excessive fall N legacies persist for years \parencite{Jaynes2015}, and weather modulates enhanced-efficiency products, with ESN lowering N$_2$O chiefly in wetter periods \parencite{Menegaz2025}.

\subsection{Watershed Programs, Soil Testing, and Nitrogen-Use Efficiency}

Watershed-scale implementation of soil-test-based N programs and prioritized conservation confirms in-field mechanisms at landscape scale. Adopting the late-spring nitrate test (LSNT) across a 400-ha Iowa subbasin reduced drainage nitrate concentration by at least 30\%, associated with reduced fertilizer use, while corn yields matched non-limiting strips \parencite{Jaynes2004}. LSNT at watershed scale produced significant crop responses, with stalk nitrate and SPAD useful indicators, and was effective for reducing loss though it may raise producer risk \parencite{Karlen2005}. Modeling reproduced the LSNT-driven decrease, giving long-term losses of 20.4, 22.2, and 27.3~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ for LSNT, single spring, and single fall applications, with early-season weather explaining 90\% of interannual LSNT-rate variation \parencite{Malone2010}. Soil-test programs (PSNT, LSNT) generally reduced residual soil nitrate and loss versus yield-goal management \parencite{Dinnes2002}, and changing timing and reducing rate through soil testing cut concentrations 30\% in a central Iowa subbasin \parencite{Ritter2015}. Field-level prioritization tools scaled cover crops and grassland conversion, raising watershed N-use efficiency and cutting nitrate load, with conservation tillage least effective \parencite{Makhtoumi2025}. Alternative farming practices reduced drainage 41\% and concentration from 17.2 to 8.2~mg~L$^{-1}$, cutting nitrate losses 59--62\% and staying below 10~mg~L$^{-1}$ \parencite{Oquist2007}. Because 85\% of a Minnesota watershed's nitrate load came from tile drainage, substantial perennialization was needed for a 30--50\% watershed reduction \parencite{Wilson2023}. Adoption remains a constraint: farmers commonly used soil testing but were less familiar with cover crops, controlled drainage, wetlands, and bioreactors, with financial, operational, and social barriers \parencite{David2015}. N-use efficiency and partial factor productivity were highest for side-dress and lowest for very high fall N \parencite{Jaynes2015}, and EONR at an N:corn price ratio of 5.6 is proposed as a sustainability standard balancing profit and environmental risk \parencite{Bandura2025}.

\subsection{Process Understanding, Models, and Loss-Pathway Tradeoffs}

Process-based models---RZWQM, DRAINMOD-N~II, DNDC, DSSAT, Daisy, APSIM, SWAT, CoupModel---reproduce tile flow, nitrate loss, and yield with satisfactory-to-excellent skill and are used to extrapolate management scenarios \parencite{Jeong2018,Ahmed2007b,Ale2012b,Pasley2021,Motarjemi2021,Conrad2009}. A modified DNDC captured discharge and leaching magnitudes across fertilizer treatments \parencite{Li2006}; Daisy simulated cumulative discharge and nitrate leaching well (NSE 1.00 and 0.87) though daily fluxes and frost/thaw periods were harder \parencite{Motarjemi2021}; DRAINMOD-N~II reproduced monthly losses and quantified that $\sim$20\% rate reduction cut losses 10--17\% \parencite{Ale2012b}; and regression equations explained 76--95\% of variation in yield, drainage, concentration, and loss \parencite{Malone2007}. RZWQM applications identified reduced rate and spring/split timing as effective, with 150~kg~ha$^{-1}$ single spring N favorable \parencite{Azevedo1997,Malone2007b}, and climate-adaptation modeling projected declining yield and rising drainage N loss under warming, with an optimum rate near 120~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ balancing economics and environment \parencite{Wang2016b}.

Finally, mitigation practices redistribute N among loss pathways, so single-pathway evaluation can mislead. In-season N plus rye cut leaching 37\% but raised annual N$_2$O 26\%, leaving total N loss and environmental-damage cost (519--612~US\$~ha$^{-1}$ among fertilized treatments) statistically unchanged versus pre-plant N \parencite{PrezaFontes2023}. Yet split N plus rye had the lowest dissolved N$_2$O and nitrate loads, showing stacked practices need not trade one pathway for another \parencite{Oliveira2025}, and no consistent nitrate--N$_2$O tradeoff emerged across tillage and cover-crop systems \parencite{OBrien2022}. ESN protected against N$_2$O, NH$_3$, and NO$_3^-$ simultaneously (47--49\% lower combined loss) while maintaining productivity \parencite{Menegaz2025}. N$_2$O emissions rose nonlinearly with N rate, with a threshold near 120~kg~N~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Wang2016b}, and each unit of fertilizer-N produced 0.01~kg~N$_2$O-N and 0.12~kg~NO$_3^-$-N loss, with subsurface-drained fields having the highest mean N$_2$O loss \parencite{Chatterjee2020b}. Emerging monitoring links crop performance to losses: early-season NDVI was negatively correlated with cumulative N$_2$O, suggesting remote sensing can flag poorly performing fields \parencite{Fontes2019}. Economic framings increasingly accompany these tradeoffs, from environmental-damage-cost accounting \parencite{PrezaFontes2023} to soil-health return analyses valuing no-till and drainage \parencite{Cassman2025} and switchgrass reference prices for N reduction \parencite{Woodbury2018}. The consistent conclusion across scales is that combining two or more practices---rate optimization, in-season timing, cover crops or diversified rotation, and drainage management---works in harmony to reduce nitrate loss most cost-effectively, since in-field management alone is often insufficient \parencite{Dinnes2002,Singh2023}.

### Material: Drainage Management Outcomes and Viability

\subsection{Overview}

A large and internally consistent body of evidence establishes that the way a
subsurface (tile) drainage system is designed and operated governs the volume of
water it discharges, the partitioning of that water among competing
pathways---drain flow, surface runoff, evapotranspiration, and deep
seepage---and, through those hydrologic shifts, the losses of nitrogen,
phosphorus, sediment, and salts to receiving waters, as well as crop yield and
farm profitability. Three design levers recur throughout the literature: drain
spacing, drain depth, and outlet (water-table) control. Controlled drainage
(CD), also termed drainage water management (DWM), raises the outlet elevation
to hold a higher water table during non-cropping or low-demand periods, storing
water in the field and reducing cumulative drain outflow
\parencite{Singh2020,Strock2011,Blann2009}. The dominant, well-supported
mechanism is a coupling between flow and load: because most nitrate and exported
salt leave drained fields dissolved in drain water, reductions in solute loss
under controlled or shallower drainage are of roughly the same magnitude as the
reductions in outflow, with concentration changes secondary
\parencite{Wesström2007,Frankenberger2024,Lalonde1996,Tolomio2018}.

The evidence base spans multi-year paired-field experiments, controlled tank,
lysimeter, and soil-column studies, long-term monitoring, and calibrated
simulation with DRAINMOD, HYDRUS, SWAP, FLUSH, SaltMod/SahysMod, and
SWAT-family models across humid, arid, monsoonal, coastal, and cold-climate
settings, giving both process understanding and quantified outcomes. Four
consolidated conclusions frame this synthesis. First, controlled and shallow
drainage reliably reduce drain outflow and the loads of nitrate, phosphorus, and
salt it carries, redirecting rather than eliminating the stored water. Second,
subsurface drainage is a proven technology for leaching salts and reclaiming
waterlogged and saline land, lowering the water table below the root zone and
converting unproductive land into cropland. Third, drainage and its managed
variants raise crop yields---most strongly on poorly drained soils and in wet
years---while maintaining or improving water-use efficiency. Fourth, drainage
investment delivers favourable agronomic, financial, and social returns, with
net benefits maximised when disposal costs, water-use efficiency, and
environmental externalities are incorporated into design and appraisal. Meta-
analyses anchor the headline magnitudes: across 15 studies conventional drainage
volumes exceeded controlled drainage volumes by an average of 40\%, with CD
reducing nitrogen loss by 40--50\% and surface-water phosphorus loss by 25--35\%
\parencite{Strock2011}; a meta-analysis of 61 studies (1050 data pairs) found CD
decreased drainage volume by 19.2\%, reduced NO$_3$-N concentration and load,
and slightly increased crop yield (0.11\%), a ``win--win'' outcome
\parencite{Wang2020c}; a global meta-analysis of 891 experiments confirmed
drainage significantly reduces soil salinity and raises yield
\parencite{Yang2025}; and a U.S. meta-analysis of 154 comparisons found
controlled drainage and subirrigation raised yield by 8.0\% over free drainage
\parencite{Singh2022b}.

\subsection{Hydrologic Outcomes: Outflow Reduction and the Repartitioning of Water}

\subsubsection{Reduction of subsurface drain outflow}

The most robust and frequently replicated finding is that controlled drainage
reduces cumulative and annual subsurface discharge relative to free
(conventional) drainage. In Sweden, CD produced much lower total outflow in both
measured years and lowered nitrate and phosphorus losses in proportion
\parencite{Wesström2001,Wesström2007}. In Quebec, water-table control at 0.25
and 0.50~m above the drain reduced total flow by 58.7\% and 65.3\% (1992) and
40.9\% and 95\% (1993), with nitrate-loss reductions up to 95.7\% attributed
primarily to decreased flow \parencite{Lalonde1996}. Iowa comparisons found
controlled and shallow drainage cut subsurface volume by 37\% and 46\%
(four-year averages) and by 60\% and 58\% over five years
\parencite{Helmers2012,Schott2017}. Ohio field data showed 16--24\% reductions
in annual discharge \parencite{Pease2017}, and across six northwest-Ohio sites
mean daily managed-zone reductions ranged 40--100\%, with linear mixed models
explaining 57--92\% of variance \parencite{Gunn2015}. Indiana paired-field work
found the strongest reductions in the dormant season (up to 100\%) with annual
nitrate-load reductions of 15--31\% \parencite{Adeuya2012}, and event analysis
showed CD reduced event volume and peak flow and increased time to peak, with
antecedent conditions controlling response \parencite{Lahdou2019}.

Similar reductions recur worldwide: Italian water-table management reduced total
discharge by 81\% \parencite{Tolomio2018}; Egyptian and Iranian semiarid studies
reported significantly reduced outflow \parencite{Wahba2001,Jouni2018}; and
Indian saline Vertisols showed CD cutting outflow by about 64\% (11.0 versus
30.2~cm) and mean discharge from 3.10 to 1.12~mm~day$^{-1}$
\parencite{Karegoudar2019}. Michigan simulations projected 59--72\% reductions
\parencite{BinShokrana2023}, and a Michigan saturated-buffer study attributed
most performance to the CD component, which cut annual discharge by 47.1\% and
nitrate-N load by 48.0\% \parencite{Ghane2025}. In Finnish cultivated peat, CD
reduced discharge by 862~mm (thin) and 457~mm (thick peat) with only a modest
groundwater rise \parencite{Tähtikarhu2025}, and other Finnish work reported
11--23\% reductions and 17--36~cm higher water tables \parencite{Salla2022}.
Dutch reviews reported reductions of 14\% in a lysimeter and 16--85\% across
soils, with increased retention \parencite{deWit2022}, and tank experiments
confirmed the mechanism under controlled boundaries (8\% and 6\% volume
reductions at 10 and 20~cm control, drainage duration extended 17~h)
\parencite{Zheng2025}. Collectively these results establish outflow reduction,
extended drainage duration, lower and later peaks, and increased in-field
storage as consolidated, transferable effects of outlet control.

\subsubsection{Repartitioning of water among pathways}

A consolidated finding is that controlled and shallow drainage redirect rather
than eliminate stored water, most consistently into surface runoff and
secondarily into evapotranspiration and seepage. DRAINMOD simulations for Iowa
showed that at 4.0~mm~day$^{-1}$ intensity, shallow and controlled drainage each
reduced annual subsurface drainage by about 25--27~mm (roughly 15\%), most of
which reappeared as surface runoff with little change in evapotranspiration
\parencite{Singh2007b}. Belgian, North Carolina, and Canadian studies
consistently report CD decreasing subsurface drainage and increasing surface
runoff \parencite{ElSadek2002,ElSadek2002b,DEAL1986,Skaggs1995,Golmohammadi2016,Ndulue2024};
at the watershed scale in Ontario this occurred most during March--April
snowmelt, with a slight evapotranspiration increase
\parencite{Golmohammadi2016}. Polish simulations projected an approximately
fivefold increase in surface runoff from drained arable land
\parencite{Sojka2020}, and Finnish modeling found all CD methods increased
topsoil-layer runoff while raising evapotranspiration and reducing total runoff,
with design choices expressed most clearly in runoff pathways
\parencite{Isomäki2024}. Increased evapotranspiration under CD---up to a 10\%
ET$_\mathrm{act}$ increase for corn---is a recognised secondary sink
\parencite{deWit2022,DEAL1986,Ndulue2024}.

At the field-water-balance scale, the tile drain, not the soil surface, is
usually the principal conduit in humid settings. In Indiana, surface runoff was
only 1--10\% of annual precipitation versus 26--45\% for subsurface drainage
(simulated balance 7\% runoff, 34\% drain flow, 51\% evapotranspiration), with
all ponding generated by saturation excess \parencite{Saadat2020}. Iowa pothole
modeling found tile discharge and evapotranspiration accounting for 52.4\% and
47.5\% of precipitation \parencite{Amado2018,Martin2019}, and UK field drains
were the dominant pathway transferring runoff, sediment, and most sediment to
the stream, responding in every monitored event \parencite{Deasy2009}. Relative
to undrained or surface-drained conditions, however, improved subsurface
drainage generally reduces surface runoff, peak flows, sediment, and phosphorus
even as it increases nitrate delivery
\parencite{FAUSEY1995,SKAGGS1994,THOMAS1995,Gramlich2018}. In Oregon it reduced
watershed runoff and sediment yield by about 65\% and 55\%, eliminated baseflow,
and lowered peak flow \parencite{ISTOK1983}; Albanian sites recorded
surface-runoff reductions of 30--56\% \parencite{Grazhdani1996}; Minnesota
modeling found reduced runoff, soil loss, and sediment yield, especially under
no-till \parencite{Maalim2013}; and in North Carolina improved drainage cut
surface runoff by 192~mm~yr$^{-1}$ (66\%) while raising total outflow by 10\% and
strongly attenuating peak runoff (101 to 28~mm~day$^{-1}$)
\parencite{KONYHA1992}. Sandy loams showed subsurface drainage minimizing runoff
and total phosphorus loss whereas the reverse held on clay loam, phosphorus
speciation being mainly a function of soil texture \parencite{Eastman2010}.
Mechanistically, good drainage increases infiltration, lowers solute
concentrations in surface runoff \parencite{Tong2016,Tao2017}, and by lowering
the water table limits saturation-excess runoff and surface sealing
\parencite{Augeard2005}.

\subsection{Nutrient and Sediment Loss Reductions and the Flow--Load Coupling}

Because nitrate is highly mobile and travels dissolved in drain water, the
reduction in nitrate-N load under controlled drainage tracks the reduction in
outflow closely. Swedish work made this explicit: the relative decrease in N and
P loading equalled the reduction in outflow rate, with high-risk N-loss periods
coinciding with high outflow and high soil mineral N \parencite{Wesström2007}.
Global syntheses reinforce that nutrient reduction is due mainly to reduced flow
rather than consistent concentration reductions
\parencite{Frankenberger2024,Wang2020c}. A DWM synthesis drawing on 290
plot-years and 31 sites reported a mean NO$_3$-N load reduction of
13.3~kg~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$ (46\% removal efficiency), a mean flow reduction of
126~mm~yr$^{-1}$, and dissolved reactive and total phosphorus reductions of 34\%
and 36\%, with the paired concentration reduction not significant
\parencite{Frankenberger2024}. Reported load reductions are large and
consistent: 61\% (controlled) and 49\% (shallow) in Iowa \parencite{Schott2017};
36\% and 29\% in Iowa \parencite{Helmers2012}; about 47\% at watershed scale in
Indiana, where load fell from 236.3 to 126.4~ton, mostly in the
November--March control period \parencite{Ale2012}; and 54\% for a
saturated-buffer system \parencite{Ghane2025}. In India CD roughly halved
seasonal NO$_3$-N loss (11.2 to 5.54~kg~ha$^{-1}$) \parencite{Karegoudar2019},
Italian management lowered annual N loss by 92\% (29 to 2~kg~ha$^{-1}$) and P
loss by 65\% with cumulative nitrate loss strongly correlated with cumulative
outflow \parencite{Tolomio2018}, and Chinese and Iranian studies reported drain
nitrate-loss reductions of about 15--29\% with higher soil and crop NO$_3$-N
\parencite{Dou2021,Dou2023,Jouni2018}. A broad review placed CD among effective
mitigation options (NO$_3$ reductions of 18--75\%, alongside 12--100\% for
bioreactors and 25--78\% for wetlands) \parencite{Castellano2019}.

Denitrification is a secondary, well-documented pathway where the water table is
held high: Canadian farm-scale work found greater relative abundance of
nitrate-reducing bacteria under CD and reduced suspended solids and particulate
phosphorus by damping macropore flow \parencite{Tanga2025}. Several syntheses
caution that in poorly drained soils nitrate reduction is attributable mainly to
reduced effluent rather than increased denitrification \parencite{THOMAS1995},
and Albanian data found system type barely affected concentrations, outflow
volume being the dominant control \parencite{Grazhdani1996}. Belgian modeling
reproduced an adverse relationship whereby wider spacings and higher water tables
increase denitrification and reduce leaching but also reduce plant uptake, so
total winter NO$_3$-N losses fall under CD \parencite{ElSadek2002,ElSadek2002b}.
Phosphorus responses are generally favourable but smaller and occasionally
reversed: CD lowered P loads in Sweden and Iran
\parencite{Wesström2007,Wesström2001,Jouni2018}, yet North Carolina simulations
found a small P efflux increase even as nitrate fell by up to 34\%
\parencite{DEAL1986}, and Iranian data showed slightly higher P concentrations
under CD though lower losses overall \parencite{Jouni2018}. Timing matters:
autumn--winter is the most hazardous period, and P peaks can shift toward periods
of highest outflow under CD \parencite{Tolomio2018,Wesström2007}. A recurring
caveat is that CD may increase loss through seepage and surface runoff, partially
offsetting drain-flow reductions \parencite{Frankenberger2024}.

\subsection{Design Levers: Spacing, Depth, Outlet Control, and Enhanced Configurations}

Beyond outlet control, spacing and depth systematically shift the flow--loss
balance coherently across field and simulation studies. Increasing spacing
reduces subsurface drainage and nitrate leaching to drains but increases surface
runoff, denitrification, and nitrate in runoff while decreasing plant uptake,
defining an optimum-spacing trade-off \parencite{ElSadek2002,ElSadek2002b}.
DRAINMOD-N found increasing spacing from 20 to 40~m decreased NO$_3$-N losses by
47\% while still meeting production goals \parencite{Skaggs1995}, and the MANAGE
Drain Load database (91 publications, 1279 site-years, 1961--2012) confirmed that
wider spacing and shallower placement reduce dissolved N loss, that deeper drains
show disproportionately higher N loads at a given discharge, and that the mean
and median fractions of precipitation appearing as drainage were 25\% and 20\%,
with wet years producing greater loads \parencite{Christianson2015}. Shallower
drains move less water and nutrient offsite: reducing depth from 120 to 90~cm
gave roughly 20\% reductions in annual volume and nitrate-N in Minnesota, and
76~versus~120~cm gave about 40\% \parencite{Strock2011}; regional syntheses agree
that shallow drains contain less sediment and nutrient and narrow spacing moves
more offsite \parencite{FAUSEY1995}. Chinese salinity studies identified
site-specific optima (8~m spacing at 0.7~m depth for a greenhouse soil
\parencite{Shao2012}; 0.9~m depth at 10~m spacing for chloride removal
\parencite{Yan2013b}).

Because different spacing--depth combinations achieve similar intensity, design
can be tuned to jointly meet productivity and water-quality goals. Latvian
experience over 16--17 years on about 200 fields showed similar intensity from
different combinations, with deep systematic drainage (1.3--1.5~m) giving the
best draining and economic indices in sandy and loamy soils
\parencite{SHKINKIS1979}. Belgian optimization identified a 50~m spacing at
1.25~m depth as the profit optimum but showed widening to 100~m cut total
nitrate-N loss by 20\% (37 to 29~kg~ha$^{-1}$) at small profit cost
\parencite{ElSadek2002b}. North Carolina simulations found deeper drains
increased profit but substantially increased nitrate losses, the general
prescription being to use the minimum subsurface intensity required, fit systems
to soil, and apply CD when maximum intensity is unnecessary
\parencite{Breve1998}; irrigated-system reviews likewise conclude deep drains are
often unnecessary for salinity control \parencite{Ritzema2011}.

Shallow, multi-level, and enhanced configurations extend these principles.
Shallow drainage reproduces much of the flow- and load-reduction benefit,
reducing Iowa subsurface volume by 46--58\% and nitrate by 29--49\% generally
without reducing yield \parencite{Helmers2012,Schott2017}; a multi-state analysis
found quicker water-table drawdown, reduced yield variability, and 3.4~cm less
total flow than deep drains, though with more surface runoff and a lower
benefit--cost ratio \parencite{Ghane2021b}. In saline irrigated settings drain
depth controls exported salinity: Australian deep drains flowed continuously and
exported the largest salt loads (5867~kg~ha$^{-1}$) whereas shallow drains flowed
only after irrigation, with low salinity and the greatest salt-load reduction
\parencite{Christen2001b}. Multi-level (bilevel) systems cut the time the water
table exceeded 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0~m by 84\%, 67\%, and 41\% and produced much
lower shallow-drain salinity (4.7 versus 31.7~dS~m$^{-1}$) while removing similar
total salt \parencite{Hornbuckle2007}; analytical theory shows adding shallow
drains between deep drains raises drainage rate and allows wider deep-drain
spacing \parencite{Hornbuckle2012}, and managed deep drains occupy an
intermediate position \parencite{Ayars2006b,Christen2001b}. Engineered
enhancements alter flow further: a permeable filter increased discharge (about
1.9 times conventional) and accelerated drawdown while reducing runoff
\parencite{Tao2016,Tao2017}; combined open-ditch-plus-subsurface designs improved
waterlogging removal \parencite{Ren2021,Tao2025}; siphon-type drainage raised
flow rates 27--90\% \parencite{Ren2023}; artificial macropore tubes generated
flow where none occurred and desalinated the profile \parencite{Chen2021}; and
supplementary drainage of a clay field raised mean annual discharge 2.6-fold and
cut topsoil runoff by 75\% \parencite{Häggblom2019}. Controlled drainage with
subirrigation raised groundwater levels, supplied 170--410~mm of infiltration,
and increased Dutch yields by up to 64\%
\parencite{deWit2022,deWit2024,FAUSEY1995,Almen2021,Madramootoo2007}, while land
retirement with drains closed can eliminate drainage entirely
\parencite{Purkey2001}.

\subsection{Salt Leaching and Reclamation of Saline and Waterlogged Soils}

\subsubsection{Reclamation efficacy}

Subsurface drainage reliably lowers the water table below the root zone and
removes salt from the upper profile, converting saline and waterlogged land into
productive cropland, with topsoil salinity reductions commonly 35--80\% and
corresponding yield gains, validated at full production scale
\parencite{Yang2025,Derby2024}. In the Northern Great Plains, tile-drained
topsoil salinity declined while an undrained area did not, and soybean and corn
yields improved within three years \parencite{Derby2024}. In India, drained-block
salinity fell from 7.18 to 4.62~dS~m$^{-1}$ (35.7\% mean, 16.0--66.3\% range)
while undrained rose, and yields rose 18.75--27.62\% \parencite{Sharma2006};
monsoon leaching brought weighted-average ECe to 1.4, 2.1, and 3.4~dS~m$^{-1}$ at
25, 50, and 75~m spacing \parencite{Sharma2000}; wide-spacing reclamation lowered
topsoil salinity from 8.4 to about 2.1~dS~m$^{-1}$ and raised cropping intensity
from 143\% to 191\% \parencite{Manjunatha2004}; and Tungabhadra evaluation reduced
head- and middle-reach ECe from about 9 to 1.9--3.8~dS~m$^{-1}$
\parencite{Karegoudar2022}. Chinese reclamations are similarly large: pipe
drainage cut 0--80~cm salinity from 35 to 15~g~kg$^{-1}$ versus 25--35 for open
ditches \parencite{Wang2020}; drip-plus-pipe cut EC from 14.42--15.74 to
2.86--6.91~dS~m$^{-1}$ and raised seed cotton yield 52\% \parencite{Heng2021}; and
after four years the 0--1.0~m layer fell from 27.6 to 4.1--7.2~dS~m$^{-1}$
\parencite{Zhang2021}. Desalinization is surface-concentrated: 0--20~cm rates
exceeded 80\% while deeper layers lagged \parencite{Han2023}, and the
meta-analysis found reduction greatest at $\leq$20~cm and nonsignificant below
80~cm \parencite{Yang2025}. Comparable reductions are reported for Turkey
(topsoil about 80\% at 20~cm) \parencite{Bahçeci2009}, Tunisia (mean 23.3\%)
\parencite{HajAmor2019}, and Canada (ECe 14.08 to 9.58~dS~m$^{-1}$, barley yield
up to twentyfold) \parencite{Steppuhn2006,Steppuhn2017}. Reclamation homogenises
the field (coefficient of variation 48.13\% to 28.14\%, 7~g~kg$^{-1}$ critical
salinity) \parencite{Yu2015,Yu2025} and improves soil physical condition
(porosity, infiltration 5.3 to 13.6--16.0~mm~h$^{-1}$, lower bulk density, SARe,
ESP) \parencite{Sharma2000,Yang2025}. In Egypt drainage raised yields 13--16\%
and farm income 9--44\% \parencite{Kotb2000}, whereas where the water table stays
shallow, combined waterlogging and salinity are more damaging than waterlogging
alone and yields fail above 12~dS~m$^{-1}$ \parencite{Kahlown2002}.

\subsubsection{Design parameters governing salt removal}

Closer spacing and greater depth shorten the desalinization period and increase
salt removal, though depth benefits saturate and shallow drains lose
effectiveness. A DRAINMOD analysis found the desalinization period negatively
related to intensity: at 1.2~m depth, salinity fell 55.5\% in two years at 20~m
spacing but only 5.9\% in five years at 200~m spacing, and a slightly saline
target within a decade required spacing below 30~m \parencite{Chen2025}. The
meta-analysis generalises this with climate-specific layouts---spacing $>$30~m
and depth $>$1.2~m in humid climates, 10--20~m spacing with depth $>$1.2~m in
arid climates with shallow water tables \parencite{Yang2025}. Field and column
studies identify optima near 15--20~m
\parencite{Wang2020,Wang2025c,Han2023,Chen2022,Yang2022c}, with desalination
decaying and sometimes turning negative beyond about 30~m \parencite{Zhu2025b}.
Depth effects are pronounced but bounded: pipes shallower than 1.0~m were
essentially invalid for discharging salt \parencite{Qian2021b}, and desalination
indices correlate positively with depth
\parencite{Qian2021b,Tian2023c,Tian2024}, yet SaltMod/SahysMod show diminishing
returns beyond about 1.4~m \parencite{Bahçeci2007,Bahçeci2006}. For yield under
saline irrigation an intermediate 0.8--1.2~m depth is often preferred, saving
10--15\% irrigation water with little yield penalty
\parencite{Feng2017,Feng2019,Muhammad2021}. Envelope and filter design is a
first-order control: the envelope factor dominated over pipe material (single
spun-bonded geotextile, 3.0\% perforation optimal) \parencite{Li2022}, a
double-layer geotextile integrated filtration and drainage best ($>$95\% salt
removal) while triple layers clogged \parencite{Wang2024d}, and the
O$_{90}$/d$_{90}$ ratio predicts clogging \parencite{Elzoghby2021}. Complex
stratigraphy modifies the optimum---pipes should be placed below
low-permeability silty interlayers \parencite{Guo2024b,Tian2024}---and deeper,
wider drains desalinize the whole profile whereas shallow, close drains act
mainly in the top 80~cm \parencite{KAMRA1991}. Economic analyses converge on
roughly 20~m spacing at 1.2~m depth as the maximum-return layout
\parencite{Chen2025}.

\subsubsection{Drainage-water salinity, salt balance, and temporal decline}

Because tile drains intercept salt-laden water, effluent salinity is
characteristically higher than irrigation-water salinity and declines over years
as stored salt is depleted \parencite{Li2022,Karegoudar2022}, with ECdw/ECiw
ratios of 11--17 in Iran \parencite{Akram2013} and 8000~mg~L$^{-1}$ irrigation
water producing 14{,}000~mg~L$^{-1}$ drainage water in California
\parencite{KARAJEH1994c}. Salt-balance accounting is the standard diagnostic:
salt removed exceeded salt added early on though cumulative added later exceeded
removed \parencite{Manjunatha2004}, an oasis system exported about 94\% of
imported salt while discharging 42\% of irrigation water \parencite{Askri2022},
and a straw-drainage module removed about 1.7 times the input salt
\parencite{Lu2019}; but removal can fall short with clogging (30\% then 15\%
\parencite{BenAissa2013}; no more than 27\% \parencite{HajAmor2019}). Pipes
discharged 34--46\% of soil salt-storage change during the season
\parencite{Liu2025c} and 40\% of total salt over four years \parencite{Heng2021}.
The temporal decline is a robust, long-validated pattern: most residual salt is
removed in under ten years, with effluent-salinity--drain-age relationships stable
across decades \parencite{JOHNSTON1993}, though modeling indicates 15--50 years
to full equilibrium, with drainage-water salinity more sensitive to leaching
fraction than to spacing (raising it from 0.2 to 0.4 halved equilibration time)
\parencite{Kelleners2000}. Effluent salinity typically rises then declines with a
lag of about two years \parencite{Manjunatha2004,Sharma2006,Jayawardane2001}, and
cumulative drainage governs leaching (about 70\% of salt at 1.0~m removed by
339~mm) \parencite{Bahçeci2009}. Salt export increases with both depth and
spacing (an 80~m/4~m system exported roughly 36\% more than 20~m), depth having
the larger effect where groundwater quality declines with depth
\parencite{GRISMER1993,KAMRA1991,KAMRA1994}. At watershed scale, region-wide tile
drainage raised tile salt loading from 6 to 359~kg~ha$^{-1}$ while reducing
groundwater loading, giving a modest net increase but episodic loading pulses
\parencite{Addab2022}, and the input/output salt-mass ratio stabilises near 0.4,
with higher application efficiency enhancing buildup \parencite{KARAJEH1994c}.

\subsubsection{Vertical and ionic leaching structure and hydraulics}

Leaching is vertically stratified and ionically selective. Desalinization is
strongest at the surface and weakens with depth (upper $>$ middle $>$ lower), with
deeper layers sometimes resisting or reversing
\parencite{Chen2022,Han2023,Narjary2024,Tian2024}, modulated by texture
(clayey sublayers limit leaching) \parencite{Qin2025}. Ion mobility follows
characteristic sequences (Na$^+$ $>$ Cl$^-$ $>$ SO$_4^{2-}$ $>$ Mg$^{2+}$ under
heavy rain \parencite{Yu2016}), with Cl and Na leached most efficiently
(91\% and 88\% at 0--20~cm) and Ca persisting largely as precipitated gypsum
\parencite{Qian2025,Sharma2000}. Re-accumulation is a frequent complication:
seasonal dynamics follow a ``wash-out--recovery--accumulation'' pattern
correlated with reproductive growth, some wide-spacing treatments showing
secondary-salinization risk \parencite{Zhu2025b}, late-stage re-salinization can
occur despite effective spring leaching (more severely under controlled than free
drainage) \parencite{Dou2022}, and surface accumulation reappears in dry periods
\parencite{Yu2016,Shi2021b}. Salinity around drains is spatially organised, with
desalinization zones forming within about 0.0--0.3~m of the drain and low-salinity
zones within about 5~m
\parencite{Lu2019,Heng2021,Han2023,Mirlas2003,Benyamini2005}.

A mechanistic body of work explains why leaching is often inefficient and how to
improve it. Under complete ponding, flow concentrates near the drain (about 80\%
of flow through 20\% of soil volume; full-profile desalinization required 5400~h
versus 900~h for the top 1~m) \parencite{RAO1991}, with steeply falling
strip-flow fractions \parencite{Youngs2000}. Two remedies follow: progressive or
partial flooding leaches the poorly flushed central area first (sequential
ponding used about 50\% less water \parencite{RAO1991}; a four-strip schedule used
only 1.7\% of the water and 8.3\% of the time \parencite{Youngs2000}), and
low-permeability barriers or surface mulches redistribute flow (a 10~m barrier
saved 52--82\% of leaching water \parencite{Yu2018}; a surface mulch cut leaching
time and effluent volume by 64\% and 81\% \parencite{Zhang2022}). Soil structure
governs efficiency: macropores bypass infiltration, raising time-to-leach about
twelvefold and water use eighteenfold \parencite{Xin2016}, and dual-domain
mass-transfer limitations prolong leaching \parencite{Zhang2024}. Applied-water
strategy matters: multiple low-ponding cycles matched fewer high-ponding cycles,
desalination rising with repeated cycles (about 96\% after the fourth) and
deeper ponding improving desalting \parencite{Jia2023}; more salt discharged
under flood than drip leaching, load rising linearly with discharge
\parencite{Li2022}. Ditching and deep vertical rotary tillage enhance
infiltration and desalination \parencite{Tian2023,Li2026}, whereas fine-textured
soils leach slowly and inefficiently \parencite{MANN1982}.

\subsubsection{Saline and brackish water reuse}

Subsurface drainage enables safe agricultural reuse of saline and brackish water
where salt balance is maintained by adequate leaching. Blended drainage water up
to 9~dS~m$^{-1}$ produced about 90\% wheat yield in India, cyclic use generally
outperforming continuous use \parencite{Sharma2004}; five-year data gave relative
wheat yields of 95.8, 90.3, 83.7, and 77.8\% at 6, 9, 12, and 18.8~dS~m$^{-1}$
with post-leaching root-zone salinity kept below 4~dS~m$^{-1}$
\parencite{Sharma1998}; and conjunctive-use economics favour blending
\parencite{AGNIHOTRI1992}. In China, brackish leaching discharged more salt than
imported in favourable treatments \parencite{Li2023}, saline (6~g~L$^{-1}$)
irrigation matched freshwater wheat yields with continued salinity decline
\parencite{Gao2023}, and drainage kept saline irrigation viable up to
6.25~dS~m$^{-1}$ for maize \parencite{Feng2017}, 4.0--4.4~dS~m$^{-1}$ for grain
\parencite{Feng2021}, and 8.0~dS~m$^{-1}$ under cut-soiler drainage
\parencite{Neha2022}, with cotton optima at 2--4~g~L$^{-1}$ and the preferred
depth increasing with salinity \parencite{Li2024,Lu2019}. Reuse nonetheless
carries sodicity and accumulation risks: cyclic reuse caused profile salinity
fluctuations and sodic surface soils (SARe~$>$~11)
\parencite{Mitchell2000,Jayawardane2001}, though modeled reuse over subsurface
drains caused only marginal groundwater deterioration \parencite{KARAJEH1994c}.
Blending criteria and reduced-evaporation, low-EC combinations are codified as
core salinity-control components \parencite{Kotb2000,Cuevas2019}.

Integration with complementary practices raises performance. Combined
subsurface-pipe-plus-open-ditch systems reached 170\% of a single ditch's
efficiency \parencite{Yao2024}; timing drainage to snowmelt and rain kept the
water table within 1~m and more than doubled surface salt leaching
\parencite{Yu2016}; autumn/pre-planting irrigation raised sunflower yield
9.68--14.70\% \parencite{Liu2023,Liu2025c}; windbreaks increased snow water
equivalent and, with drainage, multiplied barley yield
\parencite{Steppuhn2006,Steppuhn2017}; cut-soiler shallow drainage cut salinity
50--60\% \parencite{Neha2022,Neha2022b}; and where upward hydraulic pressure
dominates, drainage with relief wells was most effective
\parencite{Benyamini2005,Mirlas2003}. Low-cost straw-drainage modules and
cut-drains extend the approach to resource-limited settings
\parencite{Lu2019,Okuda2020}, and regional coupling of optimised drip irrigation
with tailored drainage reduced district-wide irrigation by 10\% and salinity by
15\% \parencite{Liu2026}. Italian analysis showed drainage altered redox
conditions above the drains, favouring nitrification \parencite{Mastrocicco2013};
at basin scale, however, persistent waterlogging and salinity in Central and
South Asia reflect deteriorated infrastructure and inadequate effluent disposal,
underscoring that drainage must be managed within an integrated salt balance
\parencite{Dukhovny2007,Qureshi2008,Ritzema2016,SKAGGS1994}.

\subsection{Crop Yield and Agronomic Outcomes}

\subsubsection{Magnitude of yield gains}

Direct comparisons of drained versus undrained systems repeatedly document
substantial yield increases, primarily by relieving early-season waterlogging.
Artificial drainage raised soybean yield by 275~kg~ha$^{-1}$ (8\%) in experiments
and 157~kg~ha$^{-1}$ (4\%) in producer fields, framed as effective where excess
water occurs early \parencite{Mourtzinis2021}. In a long-term Indiana experiment
corn yields progressively increased in all drained treatments across 24 corn
years while the undrained control stayed flat, though soybean was unaffected by
spacing \parencite{Rui2024,Kladivko2005}. The largest relative gains occur in
heavily stressed environments: Nile-Delta drainage doubled cotton and rice yields
and raised wheat and clover by 50\% over ten years \parencite{ELMOWELHI1988};
acid saline ``kari'' soils in India gained 1.36~t~ha$^{-1}$ (61\%)
\parencite{Mathew2001}; Bangladeshi waterlogged saline clay gained 30--64\%
(individual surface- and subsoil-drainage treatments 91\% and 81\%)
\parencite{Islam2025,Islam2022}; and an Alberta system raised barley grain yield
from 1310 to 3900~kg~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{BENNETT1982}. More moderate but robust
gains dominate temperate systems: shallow drainage in North Carolina raised corn
by 4\% and soybean by 14.3\% \parencite{Watkins2024}; Iranian paddies exceeded
controls by 13.4--31.3\% and up to 66--70\%, the Bilevel treatment highest
\parencite{DarziNaftchali2017b,DarziNaftchali2014}; Chinese blind-ditch and pipe
drainage raised rice by 8.0\% and 6.0\% \parencite{Qi2025b}; and canola in Iran
improved roughly 60\% over surface drainage
\parencite{Asgari2021,Yang2022,Zhang2024c,SerranoCoronel2018}.

\subsubsection{Controlled drainage, subirrigation, and drainage water recycling}

Managed practices deliver benefits beyond free drainage when they retain water
for crop use in dry periods, quantified at an 8.0\% average yield increase over
free drainage across 154 comparisons \parencite{Singh2022b}. In Missouri, free
drainage and drainage water recycling raised corn yield by 20\% and 26\% over a
nondrained control (recycling gains reaching 48\% in individual years), avoiding
drought losses \parencite{Kaur2021}; in North Carolina recycling raised corn and
soybean by 8\% and 17\% with subirrigation raising the water table 15~cm
\parencite{Moursi2024}. Subirrigation performance is strongly site-dependent,
governed by lateral hydraulic conductivity: across three Ohio sites it improved
relative yield to 87--93\% and 80--91\% at two sites but had almost no effect at
Defiance owing to very low conductivity, and simulations projected it
increasingly outperforming free drainage under future climate
\parencite{Gunn2018,Gunn2019}. In Minnesota, CD and subirrigation kept soil water
optimal in both wet and dry years \parencite{Niaghi2019}; in claypan soybean
systems, drainage plus subirrigation improved yield, reduced interannual
variability, and allowed earlier planting \parencite{Nelson2011}; and combining
DWM with foliar pyraclostrobin produced synergistic soybean gains up to
1070~kg~ha$^{-1}$ \parencite{Nelson2011b}. Uzbek controlled drainage reduced
water stress between irrigations and increased grain yields
\parencite{Dukhovny2018}.

\subsubsection{Mechanisms, water-use efficiency, and irrigation savings}

The proximate mechanism is relief of excess-water stress and restoration of
root-zone aeration \parencite{BENNETT1982,DarziNaftchali2014}, with growth-stage
sensitivity (cotton flowering/boll-forming most sensitive; surface waterlogging
worse than subsurface) \parencite{Qian2021}. Improved aeration propagates through
physiology: drainage raised cotton emergence, root vigour, chlorophyll, and
dry matter, path analysis identifying emergence rate as the largest direct
contributor \parencite{Yang2022}; lowered leaf Na$^+$ and Na$^+$/K$^+$ in
sunflower \parencite{Islam2022}; raised leaf area index and growth rate in canola
\parencite{Asgari2021}; shifted rice biomass toward panicles, raising NUE by
16.1\% \parencite{DarziNaftchali2022b,Qi2025b}; and improved rhizosphere water
status through favourable root traits \parencite{Chen2025b}, while excessive
watering and no/shallow drainage increased root rot \parencite{Han2017}. Drainage
raised crop water use where growth improved (North Dakota peak ET 239 versus
183~mm; seasonal ET up 16\% and 7\%) \parencite{Rijal2012,Yang2017,Yang2022},
with an optimum water-table drawdown rate near 1.25~cm~h$^{-1}$ for cranberry
\parencite{Bulot2017}.

Controlled drainage repeatedly improves irrigation water-use efficiency without
sacrificing yield. In Egypt, simple management of existing systems improved WUE by
15--20\% (about 16\% wheat, 23\% maize, 15\% alfalfa, 20\% cotton) with no yield
loss \parencite{Wahba2005}; CD cut applied irrigation by 15.6\% (wheat) and 14\%
(maize), raised water productivity, and could save roughly 1.33~BCM for
El-Beheira \parencite{Mahmoud2021}; modified layouts reduced applied water 30--40\%
without harming salinity or yield \parencite{ELATFY1991}; and controlled-water-table
wheat achieved the highest crop water productivity at 50\% depletion with a 60~cm
water table \parencite{ElHassanin2022}. Iranian paddies raised water productivity
\parencite{DarziNaftchali2017b,Asgari2021}, Pakistani controlled drainage produced
6.5~t~ha$^{-1}$ wheat with large irrigation savings reservable for drought
\parencite{Khan2003,Sarwar2001}, and in China buried pipes raised water
productivity, highest at 1.4~m burial \parencite{Liu2025}. In arid systems the
yield benefit is often inseparable from salinity control: Nile-Delta drainage cut
top-1.5~m salinity from 5.3 to 2.2~dS~m$^{-1}$ \parencite{ELMOWELHI1988},
Kuttanad drainage leached cations and homogenised chemistry within two years
\parencite{Mathew2001}, crop-straw drains removed salt more efficiently than
plastic pipe during initial reclamation \parencite{Lu2018}, and modeling showed
supplemental irrigation $\geq$100~mm~yr$^{-1}$ kept the salt balance favourable
\parencite{Li2018,Sarwar2001,Zhang2024c,Yang2022,Liu2025,Guo2025}, though
installation without maintenance fails to control shallow water tables
\parencite{Malota2016}.

\subsubsection{Design, weather dependence, and boundary conditions}

Yield response is mediated by spacing and depth and is soil- and rainfall-
specific. Narrower spacing generally raised yield and reduced rainfall sensitivity
\parencite{Rui2024,Kladivko2005,Nelson2017}. Modeling for a Portsmouth sandy loam
required spacing of 40~m or less (30~m under poor surface drainage) for 80\%
relative yield \parencite{HARDJOAMIDJOJO1982}; across four soils, profit-maximising
spacings ranged 19--40~m near a drainage intensity of 1.1--1.2~cm~day$^{-1}$
\parencite{Skaggs2006}; Iowa DRAINMOD predicted significant gains with
soil-specific optima \parencite{Arora2024}; and Minnesota relative yield rose from
43\% undrained to 99\% at 23~m spacing and 135~cm depth \parencite{Sands2015}. In
the Canadian Prairies over-drainage was a risk---15~m spacing promoted yield loss,
40~m being a threshold avoiding negatives \parencite{Cordeiro2015}---and in North
Dakota narrower spacing suited wet years and wider spacing dry years and sugarbeet
\parencite{Acharya2019}. In China deeper burial and closer spacing generally
raised yield and desalination \parencite{Liu2025,Yang2022,Guo2025,Hou2018}, and a
0.3--1.5~m managed water table increased cane and sugar yield \parencite{CAMP1982}.

Yield benefits scale with excess-water pressure and thus with weather: only
undrained controls showed negative rainfall responses
\parencite{Rui2024,JORJANI1991}, wet years favoured narrower spacing and dry years
wider \parencite{Kladivko2005,Acharya2019,CAMP1982,Cordeiro2015}, and Japanese
yields declined with heavy-rain days only without drainage \parencite{Takeshima2023}.
Drainage also captures value through earlier field operations: it advanced sowing
about 7--8 days (worth 91--126~kg~ha$^{-1}$) \parencite{Mourtzinis2021} and avoided
29--52\% harvest population losses in wet years \parencite{Nelson2011}. The
evidence also delineates limits: where existing surface drainage is adequate,
subsurface drainage did not improve grain yield (only protein rose)
\parencite{Wiersma2010}; medium-textured soils were most responsive while fine and
coarse showed no clear effect \parencite{Singh2022b}; over-drainage can induce
drought \parencite{Cordeiro2015,DeBoer2006}; site hydraulics can nullify
subirrigation \parencite{Gunn2018,Gunn2019}; an optimum water-table depth exists
that varies with soil, crop, and permeability
\parencite{HOOGHOUDT1952,Bigelow2001}; and installation without maintenance
underperforms \parencite{Malota2016}. Drainage benefits are thus contingent on
excess-water stress being present and on appropriate, well-maintained design.
Drainage is increasingly embedded in integrated soil-and-water conservation, with
no single practice resolving difficult soils
\parencite{GARDNER1992,Manik2019,Li2021b,Rui2024}, and cover crops or perennial
forage modestly reducing early-season flow \parencite{Goeken2015,Daigh2014}, while
catchment-scale flood impacts remain less certain \parencite{Rogger2017}. Adoption
is a leading adaptive practice: about three-fourths of surveyed U.S. producers
reported drained cropland \parencite{Morton2015}, and close to 90\% of Finnish
farmers rated subsurface drainage important, ahead of irrigation
\parencite{PeltonenSainio2021}.

\subsection{Persistence of Benefits under Climate Change}

Climate-projection studies consistently conclude that the hydrologic and
water-quality benefits of controlled drainage persist, and in some seasons
strengthen, under future conditions. In Ohio, conventional discharge was
projected to decline while CD annual reductions remained near 4--6\% and summer
growing-season reductions increased, indicating greater warm-season value
\parencite{Pease2017}. Michigan simulations projected free-drainage discharge
rising 20\% by mid-century while CD continued to reduce discharge by 63--72\%,
future and historical performance being statistically indistinguishable
\parencite{BinShokrana2023}. Finnish scenarios projected CD effects remaining
similar to the historical interval \parencite{Salla2022}, and Canadian projections
of warmer, wetter conditions found CD reducing subsurface drainage more strongly
than free drainage, though rising temperature drove dry-stress yield reductions,
motivating capture, storage, and reuse of drainage water
\parencite{Ndulue2024,Gunn2018}. Chinese and Polish studies found appropriate
outlet-blocking strategies could reduce future runoff and maintain groundwater at
present levels \parencite{Awad2021,Sojka2020}, Norwegian monitoring demonstrated
rapid system response to precipitation-induced groundwater rise
\parencite{Deelstra2015}, and Polish work showed the greatest flood risk under
rapid snowmelt on near-saturated soil \parencite{Szejba2020}.

\subsection{Socio-Economic Returns and Investment Viability}

\subsubsection{Yield, income, and productivity gains}

Investment in subsurface drainage generates substantial, multi-dimensional
socio-economic returns---higher yields, cropping intensity, farm and household
income, land value, employment, and reduced out-migration
\parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007,AbdelDayem2007}. Drainage typically raises average
yields by roughly 10--30\% and lifts net farm income where leaching water and
sustainable disposal are assured \parencite{AbdelDayem2007,Datta2000,Wichelns2002}.
In the Nile Delta, \textcite{Wichelns2002} report annual gross production values
rising 500--550 per hectare and net incomes by 375, 200, and 180 per hectare in
non-saline, saline, and rehabilitation areas, with yield improvement accruing
progressively (50\% in year one to 100\% by year four). In India, the Uttar
Pradesh project reclaimed over 175{,}000~ha of sodic land, raising farm income
over 150\%, cropping intensity from 49\% to over 200\%, and household income by
53\% \parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}; Haryana pilots increased cropping intensity
and wheat yields by 50\% to over 200\% as fallow land was cultivated and salinity
fell from about 50 to 5~dS~m$^{-1}$ \parencite{Datta2002,Datta2000}. Long-term
drainage also improves the soil resource base, raising organic carbon,
hydraulic conductivity, and enzyme activity and improving the soil quality index
by 76--136\%, with rice--wheat yields rising 328--665\% and carbon-sequestration
co-benefits up to 4.43~Mg~C~ha$^{-1}$~yr$^{-1}$
\parencite{Mukhopadhyay2023,Mukhopadhyay2023b}. In Pakistan a project lowered the
water table 40\%, raised cropping intensity from 102\% to 157\%, and lifted living
standards 30\% \parencite{Ghumman2011}, and at aggregate scale Indian waterlogging
fell over 70\% despite less than 10\% drained area \parencite{Valipour2014}.

\subsubsection{Financial returns, program scale, and cost-effectiveness}

Formal appraisals confirm favourable financial performance. The RAJAD project
(14{,}925~ha) reported a benefit--cost ratio of 1.28--2.87, payback of
4.5--6.9~years, and an internal rate of return of 18--35\%
\parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}; the Ismila case yielded an IRR of 8--9\% rising to
about 10--11.5\%, presented as hard evidence of feasibility for small-scale,
manually installed drainage provided water for leaching and sustainable disposal
are available \parencite{Datta2000,Datta2002}. Nile-Delta drainage gave farm-level
benefit--cost ratios of 10.3 (non-saline) and 5.5 (saline) \parencite{Wichelns2002},
though not all appraisals are high (the Pakistan FDP recovered only 1.7\% per year
\parencite{Ghumman2011}). Project-planning appraisal in Texas indicated present
values of 83.1--90.9 million dollars, a 43.4 million-dollar household-income
increase, and over 46 new jobs \parencite{ROBINSON1992}, and Serbian
MODFLOW-plus-benefit--cost analysis guided phased investment
\parencite{Bozic2014}. The scale of national programmes reflects this importance:
Egyptian drainage serves about 2.4 million hectares (main), 1.9 million
(subsurface pipe), and 310{,}000 (rehabilitated) as a national success story
\parencite{AbdelDayem2007}; India reclaimed about 1.39 million ha countrywide
\parencite{Gopalakrishnan2007}; U.S. Reclamation built over 20{,}930~km of drains
protecting about 400{,}000~ha \parencite{TeKrony2004}; and globally 12--17\% of
cultivated area is drained \parencite{Valipour2014}.

Careful design and site selection sharply reduce cost. For the Nile Delta,
\textcite{Gabr2024} developed design charts giving minimum network costs of
205--374 per hectare at 300~m lateral length (37\% below 100~m), lower than
conventional approaches. Syrian optimization found vertical drainage economically
better than horizontal for its case, favouring the largest feasible spacing
\parencite{Bakour2021}. Disposal infrastructure follows economies of scale: for
Australian evaporation basins, unit cost declined with size, leakage control and
geometry being critical, with pipe drains cheaper than open drains for smaller
basins \parencite{Singh2001}, while conversion to sprinkler irrigation and
tubewell drainage with reuse gave the lowest net present cost
\parencite{Bethune2008}. Field water management modulates volumes: reducing field
length and set time was the most effective surface-irrigation upgrade, and
economic outcomes were strongly conditioned by disposal costs
\parencite{HANSON1994}. Technology lowered active-control costs: a new
flashboard-riser cut operation time and gave an average CD implementation cost of
12.93 per year per hectare \parencite{Napierala2024}, redesigning for gravity
discharge reduced pumping dependence \parencite{Imran2021}, and modernization cut
open-drain size and O\&M costs \parencite{Ritzema2023}.

\subsubsection{Materials, modernization, capacity development, and policy}

Technological change has persistently lowered costs and raised quality. Since the
1950s--60s, corrugated plastic pipes became the standard and factory pre-wrapping
of synthetic envelopes reduced installation costs by roughly 50\%
\parencite{Yannopoulos2020}; the shift from manual to mechanized drainage required
new materials, laser grade control, and quality-control systems
\parencite{Ritzema2006,AbdelDayem2007,Ritzema2007}, and Reclamation's development
of reliable design drain spacing was its most significant contribution to the
international knowledge base \parencite{TeKrony2004}. Linking applied research and
capacity development to implementation is judged ``value for money,'' with
benefits easily outweighing costs \parencite{Ritzema2007}: over 40 years of
partnership produced some of the largest, most modern programmes and documented
concrete savings---EM38 salinity measurement, three-step performance assessment,
avoidance of unjustified interceptor drains, groundwater modeling that reduced
design discharge, and lowered conservative depths---while capacity development
built mutual trust and modernized practice
\parencite{Ritzema2007,Ritzema2008,Ritzema2006}.

Because drainage water carries disposal costs and externalities, its economics
interact with policy. In California an unregulated policy produced applied-water
and drainage volumes above efficient levels, whereas a direct drainage fee induced
efficiency (society net benefit 1132~US\$/ha), with outcomes highly sensitive to
irrigation uniformity \parencite{DINAR1989}. The San Joaquin experience underscores
that economics alone do not govern outcomes: Kesterson selenium caused severe bird
mortality, leading to the conclusion that management must be technically sound,
economically viable, and socially acceptable \parencite{Letey1999}. Regional
assessments frame investment within basin-wide accounting: in Colorado combination
alternatives deepened the water table up to 1.93~m, cut soil salinity up to
950~mg/L, reduced river salt loading roughly 40--45\%, and raised relative yield up
to 9.6 points \parencite{Burkhalter2006}; the Grasslands Bypass Project cut
selenium loads 60\% \parencite{Quinn2009}; and New Zealand mitigation could reduce
N and P loads by 16\% and 23\% \parencite{McDowell2021}. However, artificial
drainage shifts diffuse phosphorus from overland to subsurface flow, giving only a
modest net reduction and acting as a connectivity pathway for preferential-flow
phosphorus \parencite{Lucci2014,Allaire2011,Dadfar2010}. Synthesising
these strands, drainage design increasingly must reconcile production with
environmental values: drainage water quality is advanced as a necessary design
criterion, and active water-table and discharge management, improved
characterization, and decision support are key future directions, since deeper
drains and wider spacing raise drain-water salinity, flow, and salt load
\parencite{Ayars2015,Yannopoulos2020,Ritzema2023}.

\subsection{Landscape Extent, Modeling, and Monitoring}

Quantifying where and how much land is drained frames the significance of these
effects. Regional mapping in Germany estimated drained proportions of about 25\%
overall, highest on hydromorphic soils (30--40\%), transferable to national scales
\parencite{Hirt2005,Hirt2011}; U.S. drainage-extent datasets diverge at watershed
scale, so best estimates depend on local conditions \parencite{Jame2022}; and
regional requirement studies documented drainable surpluses and recommended design
rates (e.g., 2~mm~day$^{-1}$) with generally good performance
\parencite{Kumar2006,Güngör2023,Ali2001,MartínezBeltrán2007,Rimidis2003}. Drain
depth is the key determinant of lateral setback for protecting wetlands
\parencite{Tangen2018}, vulnerability indices must distinguish surface from
subsurface drainage \parencite{Baffaut2020}, small-catchment and drainage-field
runoff agree well \parencite{Veinbergs2022}, and watershed models such as SWAT
under-represent subsurface drainage without explicit adaptation
\parencite{Manguerra1998,Golmohammadi2016}. Socio-economic and landscape
considerations temper further expansion in some contexts: Finnish analyses found
replacing remaining open ditches could reduce social welfare through biodiversity
and landscape losses, so surface drainage remained socially optimal for lower land
qualities \parencite{HietalaKoivu2004,Saikkonen2014}.

The quantitative understanding above rests on a mature, validated toolkit.
Physically based models reproduce field water tables and salt dynamics well:
DRAINMOD and DRAINMOD-S captured water tables (NSE up to 0.84) and salinity (NSE
0.40--0.92) and derived spacing/depth recommendations
\parencite{Chen2025,Tian2023,Tian2023c,Tian2024,Davoodi2019,Li2024}, and modified
DRAINMOD explained 63\% of corn-yield variation, with Indiana calibration reaching
Nash--Sutcliffe efficiencies of 0.70--0.85 for drain flow
\parencite{HARDJOAMIDJOJO1982,Ale2009,Wang2006b,DeBoer2006}. HYDRUS variants and
their couplings simulated water--salt transport, drainage mass, and crop response
accurately \parencite{Li2023,Dou2022,Wang2024c,Li2026,Jia2023,Liu2022,Feng2019,Feng2021},
salt-and-water-balance models (SaltMod, SahysMod, SWMS-2D, SALTIRSOIL\_M) predicted
root-zone salinity and effluent salinity closely enough for management
\parencite{Bahçeci2006,Bahçeci2007,Yao2017,Qian2021b,Abdelkrim2021}, and analytical
solutions matched barrier/mulch and dual-domain observations
\parencite{KAMRA1991,KAMRA1994,Zhang2022,Zhang2024,Youngs2000}. A simple
Hooghoudt-based method estimated profit-optimising depth and spacing
\parencite{Skaggs2006,HOOGHOUDT1952,KARAJEH1994,KARAJEH1994b,Sands2015}, watershed-
scale SWAT-Salt linked local tile behaviour to downstream salinity
\parencite{Addab2022,Liu2026,ElAgha2020}, and long-validated empirical
effluent-quality relationships remain reliable across decades
\parencite{JOHNSTON1993,Kelleners2000}. On the monitoring side,
electromagnetic-induction and apparent-EC surveys with geostatistical methods
enable rapid, nondestructive, depth-resolved assessment
\parencite{Narjary2024,Song2023,Yu2025,Singh2020d,Mirlas2003,Gates2002}, and
emerging IoT-plus-LSTM digital-twin systems achieved high-accuracy soil-EC and
water-content forecasting (test R$^2$ up to 0.97) \parencite{Qin2025}.
Collectively, this modeling and monitoring capability is what allows the design
and salt-balance principles summarised here to be transferred with confidence to
new sites, supporting the overarching conclusion that drainage systems can be
deliberately designed and managed---through spacing, depth, and outlet
control---to satisfy agricultural production and environmental objectives
simultaneously \parencite{Skaggs1995,Breve1998,ElSadek2002b,Strock2011,Ritzema2016,Blann2009,Gramlich2018,FAUSEY1995,SKAGGS1994}.

### Material: Geospatial Drainage Infrastructure Mapping

\subsection{Overview}

Because buried tile networks are frequently undocumented, poorly documented, or out of date, a substantial body of work has established that non-invasive remote sensing and near-surface geophysical methods can detect and map agricultural subsurface drainage. The evidence spans two complementary families of technique: (i) aerial and unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) imagery in the visible (VIS/RGB), multispectral (MS), near-infrared (NIR) and thermal infrared (TIR) bands, which sense drainage indirectly through soil-surface and vegetation contrasts, and (ii) near-surface geophysical methods---ground-penetrating radar (GPR), electromagnetic induction (EMI), electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) and time-domain reflectometry (TDR)---which sense the pipes, or the moisture and resistivity anomalies around them, more directly. Systematic reviews spanning dozens of studies converge on several well-supported conclusions: no single method or spectral band is universally superior; detection success depends strongly on soil moisture, soil composition, drain depth and diameter, and the timing of surveys relative to rainfall, irrigation and field operations; and combined strategies---particularly UAV RGB/TIR imagery together with GPR---are the most promising \parencite{Carlsen2024,Wienken2024,Koganti2021}. Complementing detection, a further line of evidence documents machine-learning and hydrologic approaches that extend mapping to watershed and regional scales, and GIS/GPS and digital-elevation-model (DEM) based tools that plan, design and manage drainage systems once their layout is known \parencite{Cho2019,Yimer2023,Shedekar2018,Falasy2024,Falasy2025,Falasy2025b}. An established understanding of drain envelopes and near-drain flow physics underpins the moisture and resistivity contrasts that these methods ultimately exploit \parencite{Stuyt2006}. This section synthesises what these lines of evidence have established and how strongly.

\subsection{UAV and aerial imagery: spectral bands and detection rates}

Multiple field campaigns have quantified how well VIS/RGB, MS and TIR imagery reveal drain lines through surface soil-moisture and vegetation patterns. In the largest multi-site assessment---29 sites across Indiana, Iowa, Michigan and Ohio---UAV visible-color (VIS-C) imagery detected at least some drain lines at 48\% of sites (14 of 29), multispectral imagery at 59\% (17 of 29), and thermal infrared imagery at 69\% (20 of 29); TIR generally performed best overall, although at some sites VIS-C or MS gave better insight into drain-line patterns than TIR \parencite{Allred2020}. All three imagery types thus have significant potential for mapping drainage pipe systems, and because some combination of VIS-C, MS and TIR yields more complete drainage-pattern information than any single band, best practice at any site is to acquire all three; even where drainage detection fails, the imagery remains useful for the topographic information it provides \parencite{Allred2020}.

\subsubsection{Thermal infrared imagery}

TIR has emerged as the most consistently effective single band under many conditions. In Ohio, UAS TIR imagery detected roughly 60\% of the known subsurface drainage infrastructure, whereas under very dry conditions drainage pipes were not detected with VIS or NIR imagery, indicating that TIR may be better suited than VIS or NIR under dry surface conditions \parencite{Allred2018}. A downscaled Illinois experiment established the feasibility of using thermal rather than optical imagery: raw optical images revealed no detectable tile-related features and optical-based processing failed, while thermal images provided higher contrast between regions near and away from tiles, and the proposed thermal processing successfully detected and localised pipes even amid tillage-induced surface line features \parencite{Woo2019}. That work found no strong relationship between soil and atmospheric temperature, supporting the hypothesis that surface soil-temperature differences are driven by tile-related moisture patterns, and identified the optimum thermal acquisition window as within 12~h after 2.54~mm of precipitation, presenting the method as an effective tool for precisely locating tile systems over large areas \parencite{Woo2019}.

The exploited thermal signal is small: UAV TIR surveys in Ohio measured apparent radiant temperature differences of less than 1.0\,\textdegree C between soil over drain lines and soil between them, yet this was sufficient to map subsurface drainage patterns and showed substantial promise for farm fields \parencite{Allred2021}. Timing within the day mattered: late morning through late afternoon was best based strictly on consistency of success, whereas early morning and early evening were transition periods when no TIR drain-line response may appear and should be avoided; near sunrise or sunset, drain lines can appear as darker shaded linear features, often easier to distinguish from lighter farm-operation features \parencite{Allred2021}. Meteorological conditions constrained data quality: high relative humidity ($>$60\%) can prevent production of a complete orthomosaic by degrading TIR image quality (the relationship between percent calibrated images and relative humidity was negative), air temperature and dew point showed very low correlations with orthomosaic production, and the positive wind-speed correlation was described as misleading \parencite{Allred2021}.

TIR has also been applied to locate drainage outlets in surface water. In a preliminary feasibility assessment across five sites, UAV TIR detected outlet-related thermal responses at a small stream (Site~1) and a drainage ditch (Site~2)---about a 1\,\textdegree C change in water apparent radiant temperature from upgradient to downgradient of the outlet in each case---but not at three moderate-to-large streams (Sites~3--5); at Site~1 the direction of the thermal response reversed between cold winter and warmer periods \parencite{Allred2022}. Because other factors in ditches and streams may produce water-temperature variations of similar magnitude to a pipe-outlet response, practical outlet mapping is limited in some settings \parencite{Allred2022}.

Thermal detection is not universally successful. At Illinois sites, thermal imagery showed only limited thermal contrast related to the drainage pipe---below the sensor's thermal resolution---attributed to a modest preceding rain event, a long interval between rain and acquisition, or persistent hot wind during the survey; here RGB imagery clearly revealed the pipe network at one site and provided the best guide to where pipes might be detected in the thermal data \parencite{Kratt2020}. The timing of collection relative to precipitation was again emphasised, and both thermal and RGB imagery nonetheless demonstrated potential to elucidate drainage pipes, with UAS thermal and RGB surveys characterised as a transformational method for agricultural water management \parencite{Kratt2020}.

\subsubsection{Visible, multispectral and photographic imagery}

Beyond TIR, visible and multispectral imagery contribute independent information. Longer-standing aerial photographic work in the Czech Republic showed that functional drainage lines could be recorded both during and outside the vegetated period owing to visual differences in soil surface or vegetation, and that the primary drainage-feature type in panchromatic natural-color images correlated with that in infrared photos, with no differences in basic drainage type or presence/absence between synchronously obtained images \parencite{Tlapáková2017}. Critically, all test localities revealed drainage systems absent from documentation, older systems were often missing from the registry, and differences between actual and plotted lines confirmed the need to revise the national amelioration database \parencite{Tlapáková2017}. Field verification showed that drain condition and functionality, drainage-line characteristics, building technology and current soil condition controlled surface manifestations such as local waterlogging; success varied with meteorological and spatial conditions during flights, and only a small fraction of identified systems fell into the ``complete and well visible'' category \parencite{Tlapáková2017}. The method was judged an effective means of acquiring up-to-date, precise digital documentation of drainage position and functionality, with recommendations to combine standard large-scale aerial imaging with complementary UAS recording of smaller parcels, to combine imagery with information on actual field conditions, and to exploit detailed digital surface models and more frequent imaging \parencite{Tlapáková2017}.

\subsection{Ground-penetrating radar and antenna configuration}

GPR directly senses buried-pipe and constructed-layer reflections, with performance strongly modulated by soil conditions, antenna orientation and survey geometry. Early work on golf-course tees and greens found that antenna center frequencies of 250--1000~MHz worked reasonably well for mapping buried drainage pipe networks and delineating the areal extent of constructed soil layers, while higher frequencies (especially 900--1000~MHz) were best for determining depths and thicknesses of constructed layers; computer-modelled synthetic GPR profiles guided equipment selection and processing and aided interpretation---for example in picking the bottom of constructed sand and gravel layers and identifying reflection hyperbolas corresponding to buried pipe \parencite{Allred2005}. EMI using a ground conductivity meter with relatively short intercoil spacing was responsive to constructed sand/gravel layers, and both EMI and GPR were concluded to provide valuable information for tee and green maintenance and remodelling \parencite{Allred2005}.

Antenna orientation relative to drain lines interacts strongly with soil moisture. In an Ohio case study, under moderately dry conditions antennas perpendicular to the drain lines produced the strongest responses, with scaled, signal-enhanced maximum pipe-response amplitudes averaging 50\% greater than the parallel orientation across Pipes~1--4, A and B (120\% greater for the Pipe~2 banded response); under saturated wet conditions the parallel orientation was strongest (overall amplitudes about 25\% greater than perpendicular, 15\% for Pipe~2); and under drained wet conditions parallel orientation was marginally stronger (about 10\% for Pipe~2) \parencite{Allred2013}. These results provide practical guidance on GPR antenna set-up for locating and evaluating field drainage pipes \parencite{Allred2013}.

GPR integrated with RTK/GNSS positioning has proven a valuable, efficient tool for determining drainage-pipe patterns. Across three agricultural sites in Maryland and Ohio it delineated a complex rectangular system at Site~1, enough of a herringbone system at Site~2 to unambiguously identify its type despite incomplete detection, and random, likely very old lines at Site~3 interpreted as draining wet areas of a hay field \parencite{Allred2018b}. Soil homogeneity aided detection---responses were easier to isolate in the more homogeneous soils at Site~1---and a novel use of spiral or serpentine transect segments established drain-line presence and directional trends at Sites~2 and 3 \parencite{Allred2018b}. Data collection required only four to six hours per site without a dense transect grid, and the approach was presented as a substantial improvement over tile-probe and trench-excavation methods because it was faster, better able to resolve drain-line trends, and caused no pipe damage \parencite{Allred2018b}.

Three-dimensional GPR extends these capabilities but remains soil-limited. Across twelve Danish sites, a high success rate in mapping drain pipes with 3D-GPR was achieved at five sites while the other seven were less successful; detection was generally better when the survey direction was perpendicular or at an angle to the expected drain orientation, and surveys parallel to the drain lines reduced detectability \parencite{Koganti2020}. The inverse relationship between 3D-GPR penetration depth and mean electrical conductivity (0--1.5~m) was generally confirmed, supporting the idea that higher soil EC increases signal attenuation, although localised penetration depth and EC showed site-specific behaviour---strong-to-moderate negative correlations at some sites, weak or none at others \parencite{Koganti2020}.

\subsection{Geoelectrical and injection-based methods}

Time-lapse ERT, often coupled with an injected contrast agent, locates drainage networks by exploiting induced resistivity changes. On a small French agricultural plot, field time-lapse ERT coupled with water injection located the drainage network, with drains clearly defined after sufficient injected water; ERT and TDR measurements showed similar temporal trends, deeper horizons affected earlier than surface layers, and their good correlation was said to confirm and validate ERT for this application \parencite{Jouen2018}. Synthetic 3D modelling delimited the method's sensitivity: infiltrations 0.3~m thick were not detected regardless of width (0.4--1.0~m) or bulk resistivity ratio (0.89--0.69), whereas detection was best for thicknesses greater than 0.55~m and widths greater than 0.7~m \parencite{Jouen2018}. This was reported as the first experimental demonstration of preferential flows beneath a drainage system; water injection combined with ERT is a good solution for locating drainage systems, though 3D time-lapse ERT reaches its limits on larger plots \parencite{Jouen2018}.

An alternative contrast mechanism uses pressurised air rather than water. Laboratory and field experiments demonstrated that coupling ERT monitoring with high-pressure air injection from the drainage outlet detects drain position in both space and depth, with high-pressure injection causing an instantaneous increase in electrical resistivity around the drains \parencite{Henine2021}. In the laboratory, 10~mbar produced only small resistivity changes whereas 30, 39 and 55~mbar produced significant increases; in the field, low-pressure injection at 50~mbar with warm air (25--35\,\textdegree C) did not increase resistivity, while high-pressure injection at 100 and 150~mbar produced a significant resistivity contrast around the drain after about 10~min \parencite{Henine2021}. High-temperature air injection did not raise soil temperature around the drains enough to affect the signal, indicating a response driven by displacement rather than heating; the known drain positions were successfully located, offering new perspectives for detecting buried pipes \parencite{Henine2021}.

\subsection{Complementary and multi-method strategies}

A recurring, well-supported finding is that pairing methods with different sensitivities yields more complete and better-validated maps than any single technique. Across four Midwest USA sites (Ohio and Michigan), UAV imagery and GPR were complementary for mapping subsurface drainage \parencite{Koganti2021}. At Site~1 both detected drainage patterns and GPR additionally confirmed pipe locations and provided depth where UAV imagery faded; at Site~2 recent UAV imagery under cover crop revealed drainage locations while GPR was useful where UAV imagery showed no pipe signature; at Site~3 UAV imagery on bare ground revealed no signatures but GPR confirmed the field was drained and determined pipe orientation; and at Site~4 UAV imagery was mostly successful after well-timed rainfall whereas GPR had limited success owing to shallow penetration and noisy data \parencite{Koganti2021}. UAV imagery was described as feasible and cost-effective while GPR served as a complementary mapping and validation technique that also provided depth and orientation; the combined UAV--GPR approach was presented as more optimal, with a recommendation to use both technologies when available \parencite{Koganti2021}.

Systematic reviews reinforce and generalise these conclusions. A synthesis of 19 peer-reviewed remote sensing studies (15 on individual tiles, 4 on drained areas/fields) found visual inspection the most common approach, with geoprocessing and deep learning used for individual-tile detection only in the oldest study and in four of the most recent; imagery acquired on bare soil and after rainfall was generally most favourable, TIR often performed well, no single band was universally superior, and reported accuracies varied so widely---with differing evaluation procedures---that robust comparison is difficult \parencite{Carlsen2024}. That review concludes that deep learning shows the best performance for large-scale delineation, that multitemporal acquisition may help reduce confusion with linear farm features, and that standardised evaluation frameworks and publicly available drainage-map datasets are needed \parencite{Carlsen2024}. A second review screening 69 publications found that no single geophysical or remote sensing method consistently demonstrated high success, and concluded that combining GPR and UAV technologies with RGB and TIR imagery is the most promising approach \parencite{Wienken2024}. It documented method-specific favourable conditions: for GPR, moderate soil moisture and lower-EC soils; for EMI, low soil moisture and higher-EC soils; for ERT, high soil moisture and electrically resistive soils; and for remote sensing, RGB best on bare soil or during vegetation, MS during active crop growth, and TIR in late morning to early afternoon under recent irrigation or rainfall \parencite{Wienken2024}. Detection success depends strongly on soil moisture, soil composition, drainage depth and diameter, and timing relative to agricultural and weather conditions \parencite{Wienken2024}.

A cross-cutting practical challenge is that linear features caused by drain lines can be confused with linear features produced by field operations, though knowledge of drainage-system installation and field operations helps distinguish them \parencite{Allred2020}. Timing relative to rainfall is repeatedly decisive: surveying too soon after rainfall sometimes prevented detection, while in other cases drain lines became clear only hours or a day later as soils drained and dried \parencite{Allred2020}.

\subsection{Large-scale and watershed delineation}

Beyond field-scale detection, machine learning and hydrologic filtering extend mapping to regional and watershed scales. High-resolution subsurface drainage maps have been developed using satellite big data and random forest machine learning via Google Earth Engine, motivated by the very limited availability of reliable drainage records in the United States; in this framework soil variables were important for identifying potential drainage areas while land surface temperature distinguished where drainage had actually occurred \parencite{Cho2019}. At the watershed scale in Canada and Belgium, a remote sensing approach achieved 87.8\% accuracy in the first study area but only 17.3\% in the second, whereas a decision-tree classification identified potential drainage units better, capturing nearly 96.8\% of the ditch network in the first area and about 58\% in the second \parencite{Yimer2023}. Adding groundwater-level-based filtering contributed 19.4~km\textsuperscript{2} of drained area not detected by remote sensing, and shallow groundwater-table maps closely followed the ditch network in the second area; combining remote sensing, decision-tree classification and groundwater-table information---a novel complementary filtering step---improves and accurately identifies drainage units \parencite{Yimer2023}.

\subsection{GIS-based design, mapping and management tools}

Once layouts are detected or planned, GIS/GPS and DEM-based tools support design and management. GPS, remote sensing, DEMs and related software can integrate spatial and temporal information on soil type, hydrology and crop management at a site, enabling precise conservation practices and improved drainage management; overlaying georeferenced drainage layouts on DEMs or contour maps can delineate zones of water-table elevation for controlled drainage and subirrigation, and improved drainage management could raise yields and enhance soil and water conservation while helping adapt to a changing climate \parencite{Shedekar2018}. Recent advances allow frequent use of precision information for more effective, lower-impact drainage management \parencite{Shedekar2018}.

A suite of publicly accessible QGIS plugins developed and tested in Illinois streamlines drainage design by easing the laborious identification of high points, plateaus, draws and depressions from contour lines and their integration into a layout: a LiDARThAn tool reduces LiDAR elevation datasets to a specified pixel size and creates a dot at each pixel centre, while a Plot Field Laylines tool detects depressions by contrasting filled and unfilled DEMs to guide placement of mains and outlet points \parencite{Falasy2025}. Run consecutively, the four QGIS-based tools produced topologically sound, unidirectional flow tile networks and were judged efficient---executing objectives in simple ``one clicks'' and providing layout-design guidelines---with user testing finding them simple, user-friendly and much faster than the independent-task or direct methods \parencite{Falasy2025}. A companion toolset addresses topology and flow ordering: it identified and fixed positional digitisation errors (fractured lines, overshoots and dangles), a Network Flow-Path Generator produced a layout with zero unconnected segments, and QGIS Topology Checker found 71 errors in a layout created with commercial drainage software versus 34 in the layout from the new tools \parencite{Falasy2024}. Additional tools produced flow-ordering outputs and added elevation, length, slope and cumulative flow-length fields; the plugins were easy to use, reduced the required expertise, and their complete combination of techniques for identifying and fixing fractured line networks was highlighted as a major novelty \parencite{Falasy2024}.

Extending to depth placement and sizing, interactive QGIS tools automate and optimise drainpipe depth placement and estimate pipe sizes for each segment, sequencing both downstream-to-upstream and upstream-to-downstream \parencite{Falasy2025b}. Tested on a tile network, they produced exportable outputs for buried elevation depths, nominal pipe sizes and price estimations; validation showed burying slopes consistent with existing software, although the new tools sometimes generated smaller sizes---for the outlet segment they gave an 11.15\,\textquotedbl\ actual and 12\,\textquotedbl\ nominal size versus 12.88\,\textquotedbl\ actual and 15\,\textquotedbl\ nominal from the existing software \parencite{Falasy2025b}. Critically, the new tools completed burying and sizing in under 10 minutes whereas the proprietary software required over two hours; the tools improve workflow efficiency, transparency and accessibility of subsurface drainage design within a GIS environment, with exportable outputs enabling cost analysis and integration with drainage-installation machines \parencite{Falasy2025b}.

\subsection{Drain envelopes and near-drain flow physics}

Interpretation of geophysical and hydraulic signals near drains rests on an established understanding of drainpipe envelopes and near-drain soil processes. Thin envelope materials substantially reduce entrance resistance---the reduction significant up to about 5~mm thickness and up to an envelope-to-soil hydraulic conductivity ratio of 20---while for less permeable surrounds entrance resistance increases substantially up to about 10~mm thickness and for conductivity ratios below 0.2; at conductivity ratios of 10 and above, envelopes largely eliminate differences in entrance resistance between drain pipes \parencite{Stuyt2006}. Approach-flow resistance is lowest for fully radial approach flow or conditions engaging the entire drain circumference, and it decreases with increasing discharge toward a constant minimum \parencite{Stuyt2006}. Field observations and CAT-scan studies showed that flow resistance near a drain is largely associated with soil macroporosity and the geometric arrangement of the near-drain macropore network, and that good installation practice favours the service life of wrapped drains whereas well-designed envelopes cannot compensate for unfavourable soil properties or poor installation \parencite{Stuyt2006}. These features of drains, envelope materials and near-drain physical processes underpin the moisture and resistivity contrasts that remote sensing and geoelectrical methods ultimately exploit \parencite{Stuyt2006}.
